Why we misinterpret people. Chapter one of Understanding people. This is an audio adaptation of the chapter. The examples are fictional. You can find the transcript and research links on the chapter page.

On Tuesday, Rowan says, Your idea looks promising. In Thursday's meeting, Rowan says, I don't think it's ready yet. You feel embarrassed. You think, Rowan wanted to make me look bad in front of everyone.

You might be right. But so far, you know what Rowan said. You do not yet know why Rowan said it. An idea can be promising and still need work. Before deciding what to do, try to find out a little more.

Here is one habit to practise. When you feel sure why someone acted as they did, pause and ask: What do I know, and what am I guessing?

First, separate the words you heard from the reason you gave them.

You heard Rowan say, I don't think it's ready yet. You think Rowan meant, I want to make you look bad. Rowan said the first sentence. You guessed the reason behind it.

That guess may turn out to be right. For now, keep it separate from the words you heard. Otherwise, you may start responding to an intention Rowan never had.

You do not have to pretend you were not hurt. I felt embarrassed tells us how the meeting affected you. It does not tell us whether Rowan meant to embarrass you.

Next, consider another reason.

Perhaps Rowan found a problem with the budget after speaking to you on Tuesday. Perhaps Rowan liked the idea but thought it needed more work. You took promising to mean ready. Or perhaps Rowan wanted to look good in front of the team, even if that made you look bad.

These are possibilities to check, not a complete list. You are not being asked to excuse Rowan. You are checking whether another reason fits what happened. More than one could be true.

Be careful about how you read the next message. If Rowan replies quickly, do you think, They had their excuse ready? If Rowan replies slowly, do you think, They are avoiding me? If either reply seems to prove Rowan is against you, you may be giving your first guess too much weight.

This brings us to confirmation bias. Confirmation bias means looking for information, or understanding it in a way, that supports what you already believe.

For example, you think Rowan dislikes your idea. You notice every criticism but overlook the positive comments. Your existing belief then seems better supported. You may not even realise you are doing it.

Psychologist Raymond Nickerson examined earlier studies of confirmation bias in a paper published in nineteen ninety-eight. He described how people can favour information that supports what they already believe. This research helps explain the pattern. It cannot decide whether you or Rowan are right in this example.

What could you ask Rowan?

Why did you try to embarrass me assumes that Rowan meant to do it. Instead, you could ask: On Tuesday you said the idea looked promising. Today you said it wasn't ready. What needs more work?

Then listen to the answer. If you are still unsure, ask yourself: What would help me check that? You do not need to memorise a different tactic for every possible reply.

If Rowan says, We cannot afford the extra staff, you could ask, Can we look at the figures? If Rowan says, I liked the idea, but it wasn't finished, you could ask, What still needs doing?

Both questions ask for something specific. The figures might show a budget problem. A list of unfinished work might show that you understood promising differently. Compare the answer with relevant information before deciding what to do. Asking a question does not guarantee an honest answer or agreement.

Rowan could see a real problem and also want to protect their reputation. You may learn enough to improve the proposal without knowing every motive.

Now check your own understanding. Think back to Tuesday. Did Rowan say the idea was ready, or did you hear more agreement than they intended? Checking your understanding is useful. It does not mean Rowan's behaviour was your fault.

You can still ask Rowan to behave differently. Even if you never find out why Rowan spoke that way, you can say: If you spot a problem before a meeting, please tell me beforehand so I have time to work on it. You are asking for a specific change. You do not need to prove that Rowan wanted to hurt you.

If Rowan repeatedly gives no clear answer, change what you rely on. Ask for clear decisions in writing. Private praise is not a promise of support in a meeting. You do not need to keep asking the same question indefinitely.

If asking directly could put you at risk, seek a safer way to get support. Understanding someone's motives is not a condition you must meet before protecting yourself.

There is another useful concept: correspondence bias. This means deciding that someone's behaviour shows what they are like as a person, while giving too little weight to the situation they were in.

For example, you decide Rowan is an unhelpful person after one critical comment, without checking whether a new budget problem affected the proposal. You have treated the comment as proof of Rowan's character while overlooking a possible reason for it.

In a paper published in nineteen ninety-five, Gilbert and Malone examined earlier research on this bias. Their review gives us a reason to ask what was happening around Rowan before deciding what Rowan is like. It does not mean every judgment about character is wrong.

Remember the distinction. Confirmation bias concerns how you handle information. Correspondence bias concerns how you explain behaviour: you may judge the person while overlooking their situation.

A little more about the evidence.

In two experiments reported in nineteen eighty-four, researchers found that asking participants to consider an opposing explanation reduced the particular judgment errors being studied more than telling them to be fair and unbiased. That supports trying another explanation. It does not prove that the exercises in this book will solve a disagreement.

You may have heard that we blame other people's character but blame circumstances for our own actions. A meta-analysis published in two thousand and six combined results from one hundred and seventy-three studies and found this was not a general rule. Results depended on the situation, including whether the event was positive or negative.

Research from one population may not apply everywhere. A paper called The weirdest people in the world made this point in twenty ten. For Rowan's case, ask how people in this team usually disagree. Do not assume you know why someone acted because of their nationality. The transcript links to these papers so you can explore their methods and limits.

Let's try a different fictional situation. This time it concerns your sister, not Rowan.

You suggest a day out. Your sister replies, That sounds expensive. You think, She never wants to spend time with me.

What do you know? What are you guessing? How could you check? If you can safely pause the audio, give yourself a moment to answer before continuing.

Here is one possible answer. You know she said it sounds expensive. You are guessing she does not want to spend time with you. She might be worried about the cost or dislike this particular outing. You could ask, Which cost worries you? That lets her explain without assuming her motive. One reply cannot settle everything about your relationship.

For everyday use, remember three questions. What did they say or do? Why do I think they did it? How could I check?

Once this week, when you catch yourself guessing why someone acted, choose one ordinary situation and use those questions. If it is safe and useful, make the check. Afterwards, note what you learned and what you will do next, even if you are still unsure of the person's motive.

You can keep notes on the chapter page or in My situations in the app. If you are listening while driving or doing something that needs your attention, leave the notes until later.

If you want more practice, notice one piece of information that your first explanation struggles to account for. Or notice a judgment about someone's character and ask what circumstances you have not checked. Looking for a possible bias does not prove your conclusion is wrong.

The main messages are these. Knowing what someone said does not always tell you why they said it. Consider another possible reason, including a misunderstanding of your own. Ask a question that helps you find out more. And you can request a change in behaviour without proving an intention to hurt you.

Why we misinterpret people. A short refresher.

When you feel sure why someone acted, ask three questions. What did they say or do? Why do I think they did it? How could I check?

Here is a new fictional example. You offer to organise your father's appointment. He says, I'll do it myself. You think, He doesn't trust me.

You know he declined that offer. You are guessing why. He might want to choose the appointment time, prefer a different kind of help, or simply have it under control. These are possibilities, not a complete list.

You could ask: Would help with one part be useful, or would you prefer to handle it? Respect his answer. Later, compare it with an occasion when he accepted help. What differed?

Notice confirmation bias: are you remembering refusals and overlooking accepted help? Notice correspondence bias: are you deciding he is stubborn without considering this particular offer?

After trying something, review what happened. Did you understand his preference better? Did your response leave room for his choice? A respectful no can still be a useful outcome.
