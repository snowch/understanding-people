0:00 — Silence at a residents’ meeting
A residents’ association discusses a shared courtyard. Longstanding owners speak freely, while several tenants stay quiet. The chair thinks they are uninterested. Salma wanted to ask about children playing there, but did not know whether tenants could vote. Another tenant had to leave for work. Someone else feared that criticism might reach the landlord.

0:23 — Ask about this group
Before judging silence, ask what makes speaking easy or risky here. Culture includes shared customs and ideas about politeness, responsibility and belonging. Families and workplaces have customs too. But a broad description of a culture cannot tell you what Salma thinks. Ask about this meeting rather than expecting her to speak for everyone from her background.
Chapter explanation: https://snowch.github.io/understanding-people/chapters/07-culture-groups-power.html#culture-supplies-meanings-not-a-script-for-each-person

0:48 — Usually done or expected?
A descriptive norm concerns what people commonly do, or believe others commonly do. A prescriptive norm concerns what people believe others expect or approve of. Newcomers usually stay quiet describes a pattern. Newcomers are expected to stay quiet describes an expectation. Those are different claims, even when both could be true in the same group.
Chapter explanation: https://snowch.github.io/understanding-people/chapters/07-culture-groups-power.html#distinguish-what-people-do-from-what-they-are-expected-to-do

1:12 — Watch the response to disagreement
The chair may say everyone can speak. Members also notice whether questions are welcomed, interrupted or mocked. Compare the stated rule with what happens in practice. Notice who receives a different response and who already knows how to put an item on the agenda. Confidence alone does not explain who gets heard.
Chapter explanation: https://snowch.github.io/understanding-people/chapters/07-culture-groups-power.html#distinguish-what-people-do-from-what-they-are-expected-to-do

1:33 — Knowing the process can help
Sociologist Annette Lareau’s observations of family life examined how social class related to children’s dealings with organisations. Familiar ways of asking and negotiating could help people obtain support. This does not classify every family. In the residents’ example, explaining voting rights and how to submit questions may help newcomers. Those are practical suggestions, not outcomes established by that study.
Chapter explanation: https://snowch.github.io/understanding-people/chapters/07-culture-groups-power.html#institutions-can-reward-familiar-ways-of-participating

2:01 — What are people agreeing to?
One resident may hear an invitation as helping with maintenance once. Another may hear it as taking responsibility every week. Watching attendance would miss that difference. Explain the commitment and ask what people understand it to mean. Research across societies also cautions against assuming that a familiar arrangement has the same meaning everywhere.
Chapter explanation: https://snowch.github.io/understanding-people/chapters/07-culture-groups-power.html#anthropology-widens-what-seems-like-ordinary-behaviour

2:25 — Who can safely refuse?
Ask who controls the agenda, budget and keys; who carries the costs; and who can challenge a decision. These questions do not produce a power score. They identify practical differences. Telling a tenant to speak up does not remove a risk to housing or essential support. Do not pressure someone to raise a concern publicly when doing so may put them at risk.
Chapter explanation: https://snowch.github.io/understanding-people/chapters/07-culture-groups-power.html#map-power-through-practical-questions

2:49 — A new route is not a guarantee
Suppose Salma submits a question in advance and the chair publishes an answer. That shows the route worked for one concern. It does not show that everyone feels safe to disagree. Clarifying that only some residents can vote may reveal an unfair arrangement rather than fix it. Transparency lets the group discuss the rule honestly.
Chapter explanation: https://snowch.github.io/understanding-people/chapters/07-culture-groups-power.html#improve-one-arrangement-then-check-whose-voice-it-reaches

3:11 — One question to remember
When someone stays quiet or does not join in, ask: What makes taking part easy or risky here? Check information, expectations and consequences. Notice your own position too. A meeting may feel easy to you because you do not face the same risks. Improve one arrangement where you can, then check whose participation it actually supports.
