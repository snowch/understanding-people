0:00 — Looking away does not settle it
A contractor says a replacement part was ordered on Monday. When you ask for an update, they hesitate and look away. You suspect a lie. The delay matters, but the gesture does not tell you why it happened. Start with what you need to establish: whether the part was ordered, when, and when it is expected to arrive.

0:20 — What accuracy studies found
Bond and DePaulo combined findings from research reports about truth-or-lie judgments. Without the independent records an investigation might provide, participants were right about fifty-four times out of a hundred on average. They classified truths correctly about sixty-one times out of a hundred, and lies about forty-seven. Those figures are research averages, not the probability that this contractor is lying.

0:47 — Nervousness is not proof
Vrij, Hartwig and Granhag reviewed research on body language and deception. Known bodily signs of lying were weak and unreliable. Avoiding eye contact does not establish dishonesty. Combining several uncertain gestures does not create a reliable lie test. You can notice discomfort and offer time to check the details without deciding what caused it.

1:11 — Seek evidence for the specific claim
Corroboration is additional evidence that supports a claim, especially from an independent source. A supplier’s dated order confirmation could support the contractor’s account. Check what the record actually shows. A quotation can show an enquiry without showing that an order was completed. Records can be incomplete or wrong too; their source and scope matter.

1:35 — What about investigative approaches?
The High-Value Detainee Interrogation Group, managed by the FBI, reviewed research on questioning people. Its report supports gathering information and building cooperation without coercion. It does not validate everything sold as an FBI technique, or establish that an investigative method transfers unchanged to a child, friend or customer. A former investigator’s story is different from a study comparing outcomes.

2:03 — More talk is not necessarily more truth
Ask what a method’s result actually was. Did people speak more, provide more facts, provide more accurate facts, or confess more often? Those are different outcomes. For this repair, ordinary questions may be enough: What confirmation did you receive? Could you forward it? What can we agree if there is still no confirmed delivery date by Friday?
Chapter explanation: https://snowch.github.io/understanding-people/chapters/09-truth-lies-uncertainty.html#check-account

2:27 — Check your own stake
If visitors are arriving soon, you may want to accept vague reassurance because you need the repair finished. If another contractor let you down, you may suspect this one too quickly. Neither feeling establishes whether this order was placed. Ask whether you would want the same evidence if the answer were one you disliked.
Chapter explanation: https://snowch.github.io/understanding-people/chapters/09-truth-lies-uncertainty.html#include-your-own-stake-in-the-answer

2:47 — Decide without a final verdict
If the dates do not match, ask why. A misunderstanding, an error or a lie could explain the difference. A smooth answer is not proof either. You may still be able to request a reliable delivery plan or choose another supplier. Repeated missed commitments can justify changing an arrangement without proving every motive behind them.
Chapter explanation: https://snowch.github.io/understanding-people/chapters/09-truth-lies-uncertainty.html#do-not-turn-uncertainty-into-an-accusation

3:10 — One question to remember
When an answer matters to a decision, ask: What would help me check that? Identify the claim, seek relevant information, and note what remains unknown. Avoid private surveillance or unnecessary personal information. The aim is a decision you can explain using evidence, not winning a contest over who can read someone’s face.
