Why we misinterpret people

On Tuesday, your colleague Rowan says, “Your idea looks promising.”

On Thursday, during a meeting, Rowan says, “I don’t think it’s ready yet.”

You might feel embarrassed. You might think, “Rowan wanted to make me look bad in front of everyone.”

But notice something: an idea can be promising and still need work. Rowan’s two statements are not necessarily contradictory.

You know what Rowan said. You do not yet know why Rowan said it. The guess could be right. For now, keep it separate from the words you heard.

Your feelings matter too. Saying “I felt embarrassed” describes how the meeting affected you. It does not establish whether Rowan meant to embarrass you.

Before deciding what to do, consider another possible explanation. Perhaps Rowan discovered a budget problem after Tuesday’s conversation. Perhaps Rowan liked the idea but thought it needed more work. You understood “promising” to mean “ready”. Or perhaps Rowan wanted to protect their reputation in the meeting.

These are possibilities to check. We do not know which is right. More than one could be true. A useful question to remember is: “What do I know, and what am I guessing?”

Two kinds of bias can make this distinction harder to maintain. The first is confirmation bias. This means looking for information—or understanding it in a way—that supports what you already believe.

Suppose you think Rowan dislikes your idea. You notice each criticism but overlook positive comments. That makes your existing belief seem better supported. You may not realise you are doing it.

Psychologist Raymond Nickerson examined earlier research on this pattern in a paper published in 1998. That research helps explain the idea; it cannot tell us whether your conclusion about Rowan is right.

The second is correspondence bias. This means judging someone’s character from their behaviour while giving too little weight to their circumstances.

For example, you decide Rowan is an unhelpful person after one critical comment. You have not checked whether a new budget problem affected the proposal. Gilbert and Malone examined earlier research on this bias in a paper published in 1995.

The two ideas are different. Confirmation bias concerns how you handle information. Correspondence bias concerns how you explain behaviour: you may judge the person while overlooking their situation.

So what could you ask Rowan? “Why did you try to embarrass me?” assumes the intention you are trying to find out about. Instead, you could ask: “On Tuesday you said the idea looked promising. Today you said it wasn’t ready. What needs more work?” Then listen.

If you are still unsure, ask yourself: “What would help me check that?” If Rowan mentions staffing costs, you could ask, “Can we look at the figures?” If Rowan says the proposal is unfinished, you could ask, “What still needs doing?”

These questions ask for something specific. They do not guarantee an honest answer or agreement. Compare the answer with relevant information before deciding what to do. You can still feel hurt while asking a useful question.

Now consider a different situation. A friend hasn’t replied to your message for two days. You think, “They don’t care about me.”

Pause the video and answer three questions: What do you know? What are you guessing? What could you ask or check?

You know your friend has not replied for two days. You are guessing that this means they do not care about you.

They might be avoiding you. They might also be busy, unwell or unsure what to say. You could ask, “Did you see my message?” That checks something you do not yet know. It does not settle everything about the friendship.

There is an important limit to all this questioning. You do not have to discover someone’s motive before responding to repeated harmful behaviour.

Where it is safe, you can request a specific change. You could ask Rowan to raise problems before a meeting so you have time to address them.

If asking directly could put you at risk, seek appropriate support rather than using a conversation to test the person. Understanding someone does not require accepting harm.

To practise, choose one ordinary situation from your own life this week. Write three short answers: “What did they say or do?” “Why do I think they did it?” “How could I check?” If it is safe and useful, make that check.

Afterwards, note what you learned. Did it change your explanation, or what you will do next? You may still be uncertain about the person’s motive. You may nevertheless have enough information to choose a useful next step.
