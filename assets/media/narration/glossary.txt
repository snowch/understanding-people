Understanding people. Glossary.

Plain-English meanings for terms used in the book. Each example is fictional. Use the chapter link to return to the explanation and its research sources.

The reference library provides expanded explanations and practical checks; its source log records evidence and access limits.

These definitions explain how the book uses each term. They do not replace the qualifications in the chapter.

Autonomy.

Feeling that an action is your own choice and that you agree with doing it. This can include choosing to accept help.

Example: Leila chooses an occasional gardening session because it fits what she wants to contribute.

Read in Chapter 3.

Read the full autonomy reference.

Base rate.

How common something is in the relevant group before you consider a particular clue or test result.

Example: In Chapter 2’s invented group, 10 of 1,000 people deliberately broke the rule. The base rate is 1%.

Read in Chapter 2.

Big Five.

A way of describing personality on five broad scales. A person can be higher or lower on each scale; these are not five types of person.

Example: Alex can be less outgoing than a friend while being similarly organised. Where you fall on one scale does not decide where you fall on the others.

Read in Chapter 6.

Explore the five personality dimensions.

Boundary.

A limit on what you will accept or take part in, backed by an action you can take.

Example: “I will bring food, but I will not organise a gathering I did not agree to organise.”

Read in Chapter 11.

Read the full boundaries reference.

Cognitive flexibility.

Being able to change your approach or way of thinking when you need to.

Example: When neither proposed visit date works, Noor considers a shorter visit on a different weekend.

Read in Chapter 5.

Competence.

In self-determination theory, feeling able to handle a task and make progress.

Example: After someone shows Leila how to plant a seedling, she feels able to do the next one herself.

Read in Chapter 3.

Read the full competence reference.

Confirmation bias.

Looking for information—or understanding it in a way—that supports what you already believe.

Example: You think a colleague dislikes your ideas. You notice each criticism but overlook their positive comments, making your belief seem better supported. You may not realise you are doing it.

Read in Chapter 1 · Explore causes, recognition and practice.

Read the full confirmation-bias reference.

Correspondence bias.

Deciding that someone’s behaviour shows what they are like as a person, while giving too little weight to the situation they were in.

Example: A colleague gives a short reply. You decide they are rude without considering that they are facing an urgent deadline. You have judged their character while overlooking a possible situational explanation.

Read in Chapter 1 · Explore causes, recognition and practice.

Read the full correspondence-bias reference.

Corroboration.

Additional evidence that supports a claim, especially evidence from an independent source.

Example: A supplier’s order confirmation supports the contractor’s statement that a part was ordered.

Read in Chapter 9.

Descriptive norm.

What people in a group commonly do, or believe others commonly do.

Example: At the residents’ meeting, newcomers usually stay quiet. Staying quiet is the usual behaviour, whether or not anyone thinks it should be.

Read in Chapter 7.

Extrinsic motivation.

Doing an activity for a result beyond the activity itself. The result may matter to you personally, or you may feel pressure to obtain it.

Example: Leila gardens because she values improving the neighbourhood, even on days when she does not enjoy the work.

Read in Chapter 3.

Explore motivation and incentives.

Gaslighting.

Psychological abuse that undermines someone’s trust in their own memory, perceptions or judgment. One disagreement about a memory is not enough to establish it.

Example: Someone repeatedly denies events, dismisses evidence and tells others that the person challenging them cannot trust their own memory. This pattern warrants concern and support; the example alone is not a test for abuse.

Read in Chapter 11.

Read the full gaslighting reference.

Habit.

A learned tendency to respond fairly automatically when a familiar situation occurs.

Example: After many evenings spent scrolling on the sofa, Alex sits down there and reaches for the phone before deciding what to do. The familiar setting prompts the familiar action.

Read in Chapter 6.

Read the full habits reference.

If–then plan.

A plan that connects a particular situation to an action: if that situation occurs, you will do the planned action.

Example: Leila plans: “If the care visit finishes by ten, I will take the ten-twenty bus.”

Read in Chapter 3.

Intrinsic motivation.

Doing an activity because you enjoy it or find the activity itself interesting.

Example: Leila gardens because she enjoys growing plants.

Read in Chapter 3.

Explore motivation and incentives.

Leading question.

A question worded in a way that suggests the answer the questioner expects.

Example: “You promised on Tuesday, didn’t you?” suggests both that there was a promise and when it was made.

Read in Chapter 8.

Manipulation.

In this book, influencing someone in a way that makes it harder for them to choose freely with the information they need.

Example: A relative knows you agreed only to bring food, but tells others you will organise the entire gathering. They use the expectation they created to pressure you into extra work.

Read in Chapter 11.

Motivated reasoning.

Reasoning in which what you want to be true influences how you judge the information.

Example: Maya wants to see herself as a considerate friend, so she quickly dismisses Dev’s complaint that her public offer was embarrassing. What she wants to believe about herself affects how she judges his complaint.

Read in Chapter 4.

Explore beliefs, identity and motivated reasoning.

Narrative identity.

The changing story you tell about your life: how your past, present and hopes for the future fit together.

Example: Suppose Dev describes learning to manage alone when he was younger. He sees independence as part of who he is now and wants to keep it in the future. This story connects his past, present and hopes.

Read in Chapter 4.

Explore identity and beliefs.

Open question.

A question that invites someone to explain in their own words, rather than giving a yes-or-no answer.

Example: Imani asks Rosa, “What do you still need to decide about the holiday?”

Read in Chapter 8.

Personality trait.

A tendency in how someone thinks, feels or behaves across occasions. It does not describe everything they do.

Example: Alex often prefers quieter gatherings but sometimes enjoys a large party.

Read in Chapter 6.

Explore the personality references.

Prescriptive norm.

What people in a group believe others expect or approve of.

Example: A newcomer believes that members will disapprove if they interrupt the chair. This is about what the group expects, rather than how often people actually interrupt.

Read in Chapter 7.

Relatedness.

Feeling connected to other people and cared about by them.

Example: Leila feels welcome when another gardener remembers her name and invites her to work alongside them.

Read in Chapter 3.

Working memory.

Holding a small amount of information in mind while using it.

Example: Noor keeps two possible visit dates in mind while checking which one fits her other plans.

Read in Chapter 5.
