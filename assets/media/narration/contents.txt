You can use this book in two ways. Read the chapters to build a foundation, or start with a question from an ordinary situation and follow the relevant links. The situations range across family, friendship, work, education and buying decisions. You do not need a separate theory of people for each setting.

This audio is a guide to choosing your route. The written contents gives you the full chapter list and links. All twelve chapters are available as a first draft, with concepts before examples, selected research, brief takeaways and practice. The material has not undergone external subject-matter review.

The first part concerns seeing clearly. Begin with the distinction between what someone did and the explanation you give it. Then consider what an observation, a study or a number can establish. Those questions help you avoid turning a plausible account into a fact simply because it feels convincing.

If you arrived feeling certain that you know why someone acted, this is a useful place to begin. The aim is not to abandon every judgment. It is to identify which part is observed, which part is inferred, and what might change your view.

The second part asks what shapes behaviour. It explores needs and competing goals, beliefs and personal history, emotion and stress, personality and habits, and culture and power. These offer different questions to investigate rather than a checklist that reveals a hidden motive.

For example, a person may want to act but lack the time or opportunity. Another may understand an offer differently because accepting it would affect their independence. A useful explanation considers the actual conditions, not just a label about what sort of person they are. These examples are fictional possibilities.

The third part concerns learning about another person. It covers questions, listening and disagreement, followed by truth, lies and uncertainty. You will find reasons to ask rather than assume, but also reasons not to treat an answer or a confident impression as proof.

The fourth part turns toward action: motivation and cooperation, conflict and boundaries, and putting the questions to work. You can choose a reasonable response while a cause stays uncertain. For fear, coercion or an urgent concern, use the support page rather than creating a confrontation to test a theory.

Before choosing your next page, name your immediate question. Are you trying to check a judgment, understand a practical difficulty, hear someone's answer more accurately, or decide on a limit? Pick one. You can pause the player while you think.
