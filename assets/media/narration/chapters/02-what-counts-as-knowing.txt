Understanding people. What counts as knowing?

Before judging a person from a pattern, separate four questions: what happened, what might happen next, why it happened and what you should do. Evidence that answers one may not answer the others. This chapter helps you identify the missing information without postponing every decision.

One habit to practise.

When a pattern makes you want to change a rule, ask: “What else could explain this pattern?”

Description, prediction and explanation are different.

A description reports an event. A prediction uses a pattern to anticipate another event. An explanation proposes why the event happened. A decision also involves costs, responsibilities and what people consider fair.

Brief fictional example: People who reply quickly to an invitation attend more often. Reply speed may predict attendance without causing it: predictable work hours could make both easier. Requiring fast replies might exclude people rather than increase commitment.

Julia Rohrer’s 2018 methods paper explains how researchers examine causal claims in observational data. A third factor, reverse influence or selection can produce an association. Statistical adjustment depends on assumptions; it does not automatically establish a cause. Read the paper.

Check what the record includes.

Ask what was measured, whose experience is represented and what an empty entry means. A missing purchase is not necessarily a rejected offer. An attendance list may omit people who were never invited. Several people repeating one account do not supply several independent observations.

Check one detail that could change your decision. This is often more useful than collecting more examples from the same incomplete source.

Count false alarms as well as detections.

A warning is easier to interpret when you know how common the event is and how often the warning is wrong.

Here is an invented arithmetic example, not a real screening tool. Of 1,000 people, 10 deliberately broke a rule. A warning flags 8 of those 10 and wrongly flags 99 of the other 990.

Definition.

Base rate.

How common something is in the relevant group before you consider a particular clue or test result.

Example: In Chapter 2’s invented group, 10 of 1,000 people deliberately broke the rule. The base rate is 1%.

In this invented group: Broke the rule: 10 people. Flagged: 8. Not flagged: 2.

In this invented group: Did not break it: 990 people. Flagged: 99. Not flagged: 891.

Only 8 of the 107 flagged people are cases: about 7.5%. Finding 80% of cases does not mean that 80% of flagged people are cases.

In 1995 experiments, Gerd Gigerenzer and Ulrich Hoffrage found that presenting probability information as counts could improve reasoning on the tasks studied. The example uses that presentation; its numbers say nothing about dishonesty in your circumstances. Read the paper.

If the starting frequency is unknown, leave it unknown. Do not invent a precise probability about a person. A low population frequency also does not erase specific evidence of harm.

Update confidence without inventing certainty.

New information can weaken one explanation without proving another. “I have less reason to think this was deliberate” is a legitimate update. So is “The record supports the event, but intent remains uncertain.” Decide in advance what would count against your preferred account.

Match the checking to the decision.

Ask whether another check could change what you do. A reversible booking change needs less certainty than a public accusation. Facts can reveal consequences without deciding which trade-off is fair. Where harm is plausible, support or protection need not wait for certainty about motive; see Chapter 11.

Key takeaways.

Description, prediction, cause and decision are different questions.

Check missing cases, source independence and false alarms.

Update your explanation and use proportionate checks; uncertainty need not prevent action.

The association is established in the observed customers. Regular spenders may be more likely to get cards. Comparing spending before card ownership can help examine that possibility, though it will not eliminate every alternative. A fair trial would need suitable design, not merely a confident prediction.
