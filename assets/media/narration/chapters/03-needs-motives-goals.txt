Motivation is easier to understand when you separate wanting an outcome, having a workable opportunity, and carrying out the action. Someone can care and still be unable to act under current conditions. Someone can also have an opportunity and genuinely prefer another goal.

Before adding encouragement or pressure, ask what is making the action hard to do. Apply that question to your own stalled plans as well. It leaves room for missing skills, competing commitments, an unclear next step, or an agreement that was never freely chosen.

Thomas Webb and Paschal Sheeran combined forty-seven experiments that changed people’s intentions. Behaviour generally changed less than intentions did. This supports looking beyond commitment when an action stalls. It does not identify the cause of a particular missed task.

Imagine a fictional student who wants to revise but cannot tell which topics he understands. Practice questions might reveal a learning difficulty that another speech about effort would miss. A different student could need time, materials or a different agreement. The same missed action can point to different questions.

Enjoying an activity is different from valuing what it achieves. Doing something because the activity itself interests you is intrinsic motivation. Doing it for a result beyond the activity is extrinsic motivation. That result can be personally important, or it can be something you feel pressured to obtain.

In a separate fictional setting, a gardener might enjoy growing plants and also value improving the neighbourhood. On a cold day, the community benefit could still matter even when the digging is unpleasant. A task does not have to become enjoyable for your reason to be genuine.

Richard Ryan and Edward Deci distinguish these forms of motivation in their education review. Joshua Howard and colleagues combined studies of students and found different associations between motivation, persistence and well-being. Those associations do not prove that changing someone’s stated reason will produce the action you want.

Three further questions concern the experience of doing the activity. Does the person have a meaningful say? Do they feel able to handle it and make progress? Do they feel connected to and cared about by others? In self-determination theory these concern autonomy, competence and relatedness.

Autonomy means experiencing an action as your own choice and agreeing with doing it. It can include choosing support. In another fictional example, an adult daughter declines help organising a move but welcomes help carrying boxes. Receiving assistance and keeping decisions can coexist.

Ryan and Deci discuss these needs in education. Using them in another setting gives you questions to ask, not a way to identify hidden motives in advance. Ask what kind of support would actually help, and accept that the answer may differ from what you first offered.

Look at time, money, transport, skills, access and other responsibilities. Ask what this commitment would displace. You might want both company and rest, or a promotion and time for care. A choice can be difficult because two valued goals compete.

Susan Michie and colleagues developed a framework around capability, opportunity and motivation after reviewing behaviour-change frameworks. It organises inquiry. It does not diagnose why one person acted as they did. Match support to the obstacle, and keep open the possibility of a realistic no.

Only then make a plan specific. An if–then plan connects a situation to an action, such as taking a particular bus if a care visit ends in time. Martin Hagger and Aleksandra Luszczynska reviewed planning interventions in health settings. Results varied, and no sentence can create missing resources.

A shared responsibility still needs a workable owner. Understanding a barrier does not remove its consequences. Agree what each person can actually commit to, including what will happen if circumstances change.

Consider a fresh fictional situation. A volunteer values a project but misses its early start because a care visit is unpredictable. The organiser needs dependable cover. What change could respect both concerns? Pause the player for longer if you want time to think.

A later task, or an occasional role without essential early cover, might fit if available. Agree the arrangement together and review it. Attendance once would show feasibility on that occasion, not prove that care duties explained every absence.

This week, choose one stalled action of your own. Ask what is making it hard to do, check one suspected barrier and try a feasible adjustment. Afterwards, review what happened rather than automatically increasing pressure. If another person is involved, agree the change with them.
