Understanding people. Involving a third person.

Involving a third person can provide support, information, mediation or accountability. It can also carry pressure, disclose private material or create competing stories. The action is not automatically manipulation. Popular uses of triangulation often combine several different arrangements that need examining separately.

Remember: Clarify the third person’s role and consent.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

How it may arise.

People may seek help because direct conversation is difficult, expertise is needed or safety is a concern. They may also recruit agreement, pass messages or avoid speaking directly about an ordinary issue. These are possibilities, not a complete list. The relevant question is what role the third person plays and what effect the involvement has.

What the evidence supports.

Tal Eyal and colleagues tested interpersonal predictions across a series of experiments. Imagining another person’s perspective did not consistently improve accuracy; a final experiment found that obtaining information through conversation helped. Conversation does not guarantee honesty, complete understanding or safety. These were specific prediction tasks, not tests of every relationship. Eyal, Steffel and Epley (2018). Perspective mistaking: Accurately understanding the mind of another requires getting perspective, not taking perspective.

Jason Colquitt and colleagues combined 183 organisational-justice studies. Fair outcomes, procedures, interpersonal treatment and information were related but distinguishable aspects of perceived fairness. Most evidence concerns organisations. Disagreement about fairness is not proof of dishonesty or a single objectively correct allocation. Colquitt and colleagues (2001). Justice at the millennium: a meta-analytic review of 25 years of organizational justice research.

NHS public guidance describes emotional, physical, sexual and economic abuse and routes to confidential support. Services are UK-specific. It is guidance, not a test that determines a person’s motive or an individual safety assessment. NHS: Domestic violence and abuse.

What else could explain it?

Confidential advice differs from publicly recruiting allies. Appropriate safeguarding or reporting can require involving others without everyone’s approval. Mediation requires conditions different from taking sides, and may be unsuitable where threats or coercion limit safe participation. Do not require someone to confront you directly before they are allowed to seek support.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

What to notice and check.

Ask what information was shared, with whom, for what purpose and with what authority or consent. Distinguish direct accounts from repeated reports of the same source. Notice whether the third person can hear each relevant perspective or is being asked to deliver a verdict. Include your own privacy and role limits.

How to respond.

For an ordinary conflict, agree whether the third person is advising, facilitating, making a decision or simply listening. Share only information necessary for that role. Avoid using them to pressure someone into contact or agreement. Where safety, rights or a formal process are involved, use an appropriate professional route rather than an informal family court.

A brief fictional example: Fictional example: Two siblings ask an aunt to help arrange care duties. Agreeing that she will clarify options rather than decide who is the good child gives her a useful role. Either sibling can still seek separate confidential advice.

Key takeaways.

Third-party involvement can help or create pressure.

Purpose, information-sharing and authority need clarity.

A person may seek support without first confronting the person concerned.

Put it into practice.

Describe one third-party involvement and the role each person appears to expect.

Clarify purpose, privacy and decision authority where appropriate.

Review whether the involvement improved information or options without multiplying pressure and disputed reports.
