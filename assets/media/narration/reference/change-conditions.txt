When an action does not happen, asking whether someone cares is only one possible starting point. Can they do the action? Do their circumstances allow it? What reasons or automatic responses move them towards or away from it? Those are questions about capability, opportunity and motivation.

Together they form a framework often called COM-B. The name is less important than the distinction. Knowing how to do something does not create time to do it. Having time does not mean accepting the goal. The conditions can interact, so avoid assigning the person to a fixed category.

Start small enough to observe. Be more responsible is too broad to investigate. Sending a completed travel form before Thursday gives you a particular action, setting and deadline. You can then ask what part is difficult and compare a time when a similar action was easier.

Capability includes relevant skills and understanding. Opportunity includes equipment, time, access and social conditions. Motivation includes reasons, priorities and responses that may be habitual. An instruction that becomes clearer can also make the task feel less daunting. One change may therefore affect more than one condition.

Susan Michie and colleagues developed this framework after reviewing approaches to behaviour change. They explored its use in tobacco-control and obesity policy. It offers a way to analyse conditions and design changes. It is not a diagnostic test, and naming a possible barrier does not establish what caused one missed action.

Thomas Webb and Paschal Sheeran combined forty-seven experimental tests in which intentions changed. Changes in behaviour were generally smaller. That is a reason to distinguish a stronger intention from actual follow-through. It does not tell us whether a particular broken promise reflects a skill problem, an obstacle or a different priority.

Imagine a fictional volunteer who repeatedly fails to submit a form. The organiser sends more reminders. Then they discover the form cannot be completed on the volunteer’s phone. An accessible alternative addresses something the reminders did not: the route through which the action must happen.

This example illustrates a specific opportunity barrier. It does not show that every missed form has the same cause. If an accessible route still does not help, ask what remains difficult. Perhaps the instructions are unclear, or the person no longer wants that role. Keep the explanation open to revision.

Saying they lack motivation can become an empty circle: they did not do it because they lacked motivation, and we know that because they did not do it. Ask instead what matters to them and what the task costs. A refusal can concern a goal they have never accepted.

Include your own contribution. Did you set an unrealistic deadline or leave essential information out? For a shared responsibility, negotiate a feasible contribution rather than secretly trying to engineer compliance. Sometimes the appropriate change is a demonstration or a simpler step. Sometimes it is reconsidering the goal or making another arrangement.

Choose one action you want to take yourself, or one genuinely shared task. Describe exactly what would happen and name a suspected missing condition. What small adjustment could test that possibility? Take a moment to decide. Pause the player if you want longer.
