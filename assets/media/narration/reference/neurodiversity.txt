Neurodiversity concerns variation in how people experience, process and respond to the world. Neurodivergent is an umbrella term, used in different ways and often including autism and ADHD. It is not one diagnosis, a personality type or a fixed set of needs that everyone shares.

The practical cue is to investigate the fit between people and the setting. Ask about this individual's experience rather than making an accommodation plan from a label. Silence, interruptions, missed cues or a preference for routine can have many explanations, and none identifies a diagnosis by itself.

Noise, pace, ambiguous language or changing expectations can make communication difficult. The mismatch may involve the interaction rather than a problem located entirely inside one person. A request for preparation time or direct wording can provide information about access; it does not establish unwillingness or a lack of care.

Susan Michie and colleagues reviewed behaviour-change frameworks and proposed looking at capability, opportunity and motivation together. Their framework was examined in policy settings and is not a diagnostic test. Here it suggests questions about task conditions, without proving which barrier caused a particular action.

Catherine Crompton and colleagues studied how stories were passed between autistic, non-autistic and mixed groups. In that task, information transfer and rapport were poorer in mixed groups than within groups of the same neurotype. The findings support attention to communication fit, rather than automatically locating difficulty in autistic participants.

The study does not establish a rule about every autistic person or all forms of neurodivergence. Tal Eyal and colleagues also studied interpersonal predictions and found that imagining another person's perspective did not consistently improve accuracy. In a final experiment, getting information through conversation helped, though conversation cannot guarantee understanding, honesty or safety.

Consider a fictional colleague who seems disengaged during noisy brainstorming but contributes clearly when questions arrive in advance. Agreeing preparation time and a quieter format examines the conditions for participation. It does not establish autism, or explain every earlier disagreement with that colleague.

Ask what makes the actual task difficult, and compare occasions when communication works better. Distinguish essential requirements from habits that are simply familiar to you. Clear written decisions, advance information, breaks or another communication medium may help, but the person's own feedback should guide the choice.

Avoid requiring unnecessary diagnostic disclosure before discussing a practical adjustment. People with the same diagnosis may have different abilities and preferences, and neurodivergence can coexist with a health difficulty. A label alone cannot settle what support is wanted or whether a new difficulty deserves assessment.

Harmful conduct still needs addressing, while accommodations should not depend on proving a moral excuse. You can clarify a shared responsibility and change an inaccessible arrangement. Professionals or relevant services can help with assessment and support when needed; behaviour examples in this lesson do not provide a diagnostic route.

Choose one communication task and name a condition that makes it difficult. What adjustment would the person welcome, without needing to disclose more private information? Take a moment. Pause the player if you would like longer.
