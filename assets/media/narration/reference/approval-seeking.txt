Understanding people. Seeking approval.

Seeking approval means directing attention or action toward being accepted, praised or spared disapproval. It can be an ordinary part of relationships. It becomes a useful concern when an answer repeatedly conflicts with your needs, values or capacity, or when another person’s reaction seems to determine whether you feel allowed to choose.

Remember: Check whether the yes belongs to you.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

How it may arise.

Agreement may feel safer than disappointment, especially when earlier refusals brought criticism or exclusion. Those are possibilities, not a history to invent for someone. Approval may also be genuinely useful in an evaluated role. Distinguish wanting an honest assessment of your work from needing reassurance that you are acceptable as a person.

What the evidence supports.

Richard Ryan and Edward Deci reviewed self-determination theory and evidence concerning autonomy, competence and relatedness in motivation and well-being. The framework suggests distinctions to investigate. It cannot identify the cause of a particular action or refusal. Ryan and Deci (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being.

Roy Baumeister and Mark Leary reviewed evidence for a widespread motivation to form and maintain stable interpersonal attachments. The review does not imply that everyone wants the same amount or form of contact, or must maintain an unsafe relationship. Baumeister and Leary (1995). The need to belong: desire for interpersonal attachments as a fundamental human motivation.

Geraldine Downey and Scott Feldman used several studies to examine anxious expectations of rejection, interpretations of ambiguous behaviour and intimate-relationship outcomes. The findings do not establish that a particular fear is mistaken or allow this entry to score someone’s rejection sensitivity. Downey and Feldman (1996). Implications of rejection sensitivity for intimate relationships.

What else could explain it?

Cooperation, generosity and respect are not automatically approval-seeking. A yes may be freely chosen. Conversely, apparent eagerness may conceal a power imbalance. The question is not whether you care about others’ views, but whether you can consider those views alongside your own judgment and practical limits.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

What to notice and check.

Notice what happens before, during and after agreeing. Did you check your capacity, identify your preference and understand the request? Do you later resent a commitment you never wanted? Compare situations where you can decline safely. If the issue is someone else’s agreement, invite an honest answer without making them responsible for reassuring you.

How to respond.

Use a brief pause before answering, clarify the request and state a limit or alternative you can actually sustain. If retaliation is likely, a more assertive sentence alone may not solve the problem; consider support and available choices. When seeking feedback, ask about a specific action or standard rather than asking the other person to settle your worth.

A brief fictional example: Fictional example: A colleague asks you to cover another shift. You usually agree immediately and then cancel your own plans. This time you check the rota and offer one hour you can genuinely spare. Their disappointment does not by itself establish that your answer was wrong.

Key takeaways.

Caring about approval is not inherently a problem.

An immediate yes may hide an unexamined cost.

Real consequences of refusal need attention alongside communication skills.

Put it into practice.

Recall one agreement and identify your preference, capacity and expected cost of saying no.

Try a pause or a specific limit in an ordinary situation where it is safe.

Review whether the answer was more honest and sustainable, rather than whether everyone approved.
