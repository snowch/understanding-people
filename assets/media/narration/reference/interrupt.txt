Understanding people. Interrupting.

Interrupting means entering another person’s turn before they have finished, but conversational overlap can serve different functions. It can express enthusiasm, clarify a point, warn of a problem or take away someone’s opportunity to contribute. The effect and recurring pattern matter more than applying one meaning to every overlap.

Remember: Notice what the overlap does to the conversation.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

How it may arise.

Pace, excitement, role expectations, language, attention and learned conversation styles can influence turn-taking. Some groups tolerate more overlap than others. An interruption can also reflect unequal power when certain people repeatedly lose the floor. Possible influences do not remove the need to address the effect.

What the evidence supports.

Guy Itzchakov and colleagues manipulated listening quality in four laboratory experiments. Attentive, nonjudgmental listening reduced speakers’ social anxiety and helped them recognise mixed attitudes in the studied tasks. The experiments do not show that listening guarantees agreement or repairs a harmful relationship. Itzchakov, Kluger and Castro (2017). I Am Aware of My Inconsistencies but Can Tolerate Them: The Effect of High Quality Listening on Speakers' Attitude Ambivalence.

Tal Eyal and colleagues tested interpersonal predictions across a series of experiments. Imagining another person’s perspective did not consistently improve accuracy; a final experiment found that obtaining information through conversation helped. Conversation does not guarantee honesty, complete understanding or safety. These were specific prediction tasks, not tests of every relationship. Eyal, Steffel and Epley (2018). Perspective mistaking: Accurately understanding the mind of another requires getting perspective, not taking perspective.

Dacher Keltner, Deborah Gruenfeld and Cameron Anderson reviewed theory and evidence connecting unequal power with opportunities, threats and behavioural tendencies. This is a broad framework, not a universal rule about every powerful or less powerful person. Keltner D, Gruenfeld DH, Anderson C. (2003). Power, approach, and inhibition.

Vivian Vignoles and colleagues conducted two multinational surveys. Ways of being independent and connected varied across several dimensions, beyond a simple East–West division. Group patterns cannot reveal one person’s values, and national averages are not instructions for interacting with an individual. Vignoles and colleagues (2016). Beyond the 'east-west' dichotomy: Global variation in cultural models of selfhood.

What else could explain it?

Not all interruptions are disrespectful, and quietness does not guarantee respectful listening. An urgent warning differs from repeated topic takeover. A person may also need an accessible way to signal a point before it is lost. Avoid inferring ADHD, aggression or a personality type from interrupting alone.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

What to notice and check.

Compare who interrupts, who is interrupted, whether the original point returns and how people respond when the floor is requested. Ask about the speaker’s experience rather than judging only from your own tolerance. Notice your own tendency to finish sentences, offer advice or treat a pause as an invitation to change topics.

How to respond.

For ordinary exchanges, name the practical need: finishing a thought, clarifying turn-taking or making room for a quieter contribution. If you interrupted, return the floor without a lengthy self-defence. In groups, use a structure that fits the task and access needs. If a request repeatedly brings ridicule or retaliation, examine the wider pattern.

A brief fictional example: Fictional example: You start explaining your experience while a friend is still describing theirs. Saying “I cut across you—please finish” repairs the immediate turn. It is more useful than deciding that good intentions mean no interruption occurred.

Key takeaways.

Overlap can have different functions and effects.

Look at whose contribution is repeatedly lost.

A brief repair can return the floor.

Put it into practice.

Notice one interruption, its context and what happened to the original point.

Try a clear turn-taking request or return the floor when you interrupt.

Review whether participation improved and whether the pattern changed across later exchanges.
