Understanding people. Children: ability, learning and expectations.

Children develop abilities in communication, movement, play, learning and responding to others. Expectations should reflect both the child’s current skills and the conditions of a task. A behaviour that frustrates an adult does not, by itself, show deliberate defiance, a stable personality trait or a developmental condition.

Remember: Match the expectation to the skill and setting.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

How it may arise.

A child may not understand the instruction, remember several steps, manage the sensory demands or know what to do instead. They may also want choice, need rest or disagree. These are possibilities to investigate through the child’s behaviour, communication and available guidance. Development varies, but variation is not a reason to ignore concerns or lost skills.

What the evidence supports.

CDC public guidance describes development in play, learning, communication, action and movement, and recommends discussing concerns or lost skills with a clinician. Its milestone resources are not substitutes for validated screening and do not diagnose a child from one missed task. CDC: Developmental milestones.

Susan Michie and colleagues reviewed behaviour-change frameworks and proposed COM-B: capability, opportunity and motivation as interacting conditions for behaviour. They examined its use in tobacco-control and obesity policy. It is a framework for analysing and designing change, not a diagnostic test or proof that one identified barrier caused an action. Michie, van Stralen and West (2011). The behaviour change wheel: a new method for characterising and designing behaviour change interventions.

Richard Ryan and Edward Deci reviewed self-determination theory and evidence concerning autonomy, competence and relatedness in motivation and well-being. The framework suggests distinctions to investigate. It cannot identify the cause of a particular action or refusal. Ryan and Deci (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being.

What else could explain it?

Inability, uncertainty and unwillingness are different questions, and can coexist. An adult’s successful demonstration does not mean a child can reproduce the task independently in another setting. Milestone information is a starting resource rather than a home diagnosis. One missed task does not establish a delay, while recurring concerns deserve discussion.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

What to notice and check.

Identify the exact action expected, how it was explained and what the child can currently do. Compare a shorter instruction, demonstration, quieter setting or different time where appropriate. Seek the child’s view in a suitable way. Include examples of successful participation instead of documenting only difficult moments.

How to respond.

Provide a clear, manageable instruction and an appropriate alternative action. Offer support without shaming or turning the task into a test of affection. For developmental concerns or loss of skills, consult an appropriate clinician or service; do not wait for a theory about the child’s personality. Educational and care arrangements should fit the child’s actual needs and local responsibilities.

Key takeaways.

Check skills and task conditions before inferring intent.

Developmental resources support inquiry, not self-diagnosis.

Concerns and lost skills deserve timely professional discussion.

Put it into practice.

Describe one task and the skill it requires, including how it was explained.

Try an appropriate clarification or support, or arrange advice if development is a concern.

Review what the child could do and what support is still needed, including successes.
