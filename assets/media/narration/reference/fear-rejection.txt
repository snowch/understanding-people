Understanding people. Fear of rejection.

Fear of rejection is concern about being unwanted, excluded or abandoned. It can be proportionate to a real relationship risk, or extend to ambiguous situations where the outcome is unclear. Rejection sensitivity is a research concept about anxious expectations and reactions to rejection; this entry does not measure or diagnose it.

Remember: Check the expectation before acting on it.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

How it may arise.

If rejection is expected, a delay or brief answer may receive more attention as possible evidence. The resulting response—repeated questions, withdrawal, anger or quick agreement—can affect the interaction in turn. That is one possible sequence. It does not show that the first concern was mistaken or that the person caused another’s harmful behaviour.

What the evidence supports.

Geraldine Downey and Scott Feldman used several studies to examine anxious expectations of rejection, interpretations of ambiguous behaviour and intimate-relationship outcomes. The findings do not establish that a particular fear is mistaken or allow this entry to score someone’s rejection sensitivity. Downey and Feldman (1996). Implications of rejection sensitivity for intimate relationships.

Roy Baumeister and Mark Leary reviewed evidence for a widespread motivation to form and maintain stable interpersonal attachments. The review does not imply that everyone wants the same amount or form of contact, or must maintain an unsafe relationship. Baumeister and Leary (1995). The need to belong: desire for interpersonal attachments as a fundamental human motivation.

Sarah Shihata and colleagues reviewed what was known and unresolved about intolerance of uncertainty across emotional disorders. Uncertainty can be difficult without implying a disorder. The review does not establish what level of uncertainty another person should tolerate. Shihata and colleagues (2016). Intolerance of uncertainty in emotional disorders: What uncertainties remain?

What else could explain it?

A relationship may genuinely be unreliable or excluding. A missed reply can also concern workload, communication preferences or a misunderstanding. Fear of rejection is distinct from a diagnosis of borderline personality disorder, and does not justify surveillance or preventing another person from leaving. People need both connection and room for choice.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

What to notice and check.

Write the specific event, the feared meaning and the action you felt urged to take. Compare similar occasions and include messages or actions that complicate the fear. Ask whether a clear expectation about contact exists. Notice when reassurance gives useful information and when the same question returns without new evidence.

How to respond.

Where safe, make a specific request about communication or expectations rather than demanding a guarantee of permanent acceptance. Allow time before responding to ambiguity. If fear repeatedly disrupts life or relationships, support can help you explore it without settling a label yourself. If exclusion or mistreatment is real, address that conduct and consider your options.

A brief fictional example: Fictional example: A friend sends a short reply. You first read it as rejection, then learn they are in a noisy station and will call later. One clarification changes this occasion’s explanation. It does not prove every earlier concern was unfounded.

Key takeaways.

Fear can reflect uncertainty, a learned expectation or a real problem.

Separate the event from the rejection you predict.

Requests and support are more workable than guarantees of acceptance.

Put it into practice.

Record an ambiguous event, the feared meaning and your first impulse.

Choose one fair clarification or a deliberate pause, avoiding repeated checking.

Review the information gained and whether your response made the exchange clearer.
