Understanding people. Feeling capable.

Competence concerns feeling able to act effectively and develop useful skills. It is related to actual ability but not identical to it. A capable person may feel unsure in an unfamiliar setting; someone else may feel confident while missing an important skill. Both confidence and performance deserve a fair check.

Remember: Support progress without taking over.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

How it may arise.

Challenge, feedback and opportunities to practise can shape how capable a task feels. Assistance can make learning possible, or remove the very part someone wants to master. Repeated public correction may make it harder to ask questions even when the correction is accurate. These possibilities need comparing with the task’s real demands rather than assuming encouragement alone is enough.

What the evidence supports.

Richard Ryan and Edward Deci reviewed self-determination theory and evidence concerning autonomy, competence and relatedness in motivation and well-being. The framework suggests distinctions to investigate. It cannot identify the cause of a particular action or refusal. Ryan and Deci (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being.

Susan Michie and colleagues reviewed behaviour-change frameworks and proposed COM-B: capability, opportunity and motivation as interacting conditions for behaviour. They examined its use in tobacco-control and obesity policy. It is a framework for analysing and designing change, not a diagnostic test or proof that one identified barrier caused an action. Michie, van Stralen and West (2011). The behaviour change wheel: a new method for characterising and designing behaviour change interventions.

What else could explain it?

Difficulty starting may involve unclear instructions, missing tools, low interest or too little time rather than low confidence. A task can also be beyond current ability or inaccessible as designed. Distinguish “I do not know how,” “I cannot do this under these conditions” and “I do not want this outcome.” Those answers call for different responses.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

What to notice and check.

Ask what the person can already do, what part is difficult and what useful progress would look like. Use an example of actual work rather than a global judgment such as lazy or gifted. Compare performance with clear instructions and appropriate support. Check whether your feedback tells the person how to improve or only whether you approve.

How to respond.

Agree on a manageable next challenge and the kind of help wanted. Give specific feedback about the task, allow practice where mistakes are tolerable, and retain appropriate safeguards where they are not. If you are learning, make a clear request for information or demonstration instead of treating uncertainty as proof that you are incapable.

A brief fictional example: Fictional example: A daughter declines help cooking because she wants to learn the recipe. Her parent asks whether she wants the timing explained or another person to chop vegetables. Supporting one difficult part can preserve her ownership of the task.

Key takeaways.

Feeling capable and being able are related but different.

Find the actual difficulty before choosing support.

Useful help can leave room to practise and decide.

Put it into practice.

Choose one task and name what is already manageable and what is missing.

Try a specific support or smaller challenge agreed with the learner.

Review progress, understanding and independence of the next attempt.
