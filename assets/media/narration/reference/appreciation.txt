Understanding people. Notice what helps.

Appreciation communicates that you noticed and valued a contribution, quality or effort. It can help make useful behaviour visible. It is different from flattering someone to obtain compliance, evaluating their whole worth or implying that gratitude cancels a concern. Appreciation is most informative when the recipient can recognise what you mean.

Remember: Name the contribution without creating a debt.

How it may arise.

Helpful work often becomes invisible when it is routine. People may notice mistakes more readily than reliable contributions, or offer praise only when they want another favour. Appreciation can then feel vague or conditional. The issue is not whether praise is always good, but whether it is accurate, welcome and consistent with the arrangement.

What the evidence supports.

Cameron Anderson and colleagues reviewed evidence on the desire for respect, admiration and voluntary deference, distinguishing status from power, money and belonging. A broad motive does not explain every interruption, disagreement or wish for recognition. Anderson, Hildreth and Howland (2015). Is the desire for status a fundamental human motive? A review of the empirical literature.

Jason Colquitt and colleagues combined 183 organisational-justice studies. Fair outcomes, procedures, interpersonal treatment and information were related but distinguishable aspects of perceived fairness. Most evidence concerns organisations. Disagreement about fairness is not proof of dishonesty or a single objectively correct allocation. Colquitt and colleagues (2001). Justice at the millennium: a meta-analytic review of 25 years of organizational justice research.

Russell Cropanzano and Marie Mitchell reviewed social-exchange theory in organisational research, including different exchange rules, resources and relationships. The review also identifies conceptual ambiguity. Relationships cannot be reduced to a universal ledger of favours. Cropanzano and Mitchell (2005). Social Exchange Theory: An Interdisciplinary Review.

What else could explain it?

Not everyone enjoys public praise or the same form of recognition. A person may prefer accurate credit, shared workload or payment rather than repeated thanks. Lack of visible enthusiasm does not establish ingratitude. Appreciation also does not compensate for unfair terms, underpayment, unwanted access or continued harmful conduct.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

What to notice and check.

Ask what contribution you actually observed and what effect it had. Notice whose work is routinely expected and whose is celebrated. Consider whether public acknowledgment would reveal something private or uncomfortable. If you are receiving thanks, distinguish a freely given expression from a suggestion that you now owe continued availability.

How to respond.

State the specific contribution and its benefit without attaching an immediate demand. Give appropriate credit in the setting where work is recognised, checking preferences where relevant. Pair thanks with fair responsibilities and resources. If appreciation concerns a behaviour you want to develop yourself, notice its effect without turning every interaction into a performance score.

A brief fictional example: Fictional example: “Your note about the deadline helped us catch the missing approval” gives more useful information than “You are amazing.” It can stand on its own, while any request for further work remains a separate question with room for refusal.

Key takeaways.

Specific acknowledgment makes a contribution visible.

Preferences for public and private recognition differ.

Thanks does not erase unfairness or create an obligation.

Put it into practice.

Identify one contribution and its actual benefit, including work you usually overlook.

Express appreciation in a suitable form without adding a demand.

Review whether recognition was accurate and welcome, and whether responsibilities are fairly shared.
