Understanding people. Make a clear request.

A clear request describes an action you want someone to consider, with enough context to answer usefully. It differs from a complaint, a hint, a demand or a judgment about character. A clear request can still be refused. Clarity improves the information available; it does not create an obligation to agree.

Remember: Name the action and leave room for an answer.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

How it may arise.

People often assume that a need is obvious or that the other person interprets a familiar phrase in the same way. “Help more” might mean taking ownership of a task, doing part of it now or asking before making decisions. Frustration grows when the response is judged against an expectation that was never made explicit.

What the evidence supports.

Tal Eyal and colleagues tested interpersonal predictions across a series of experiments. Imagining another person’s perspective did not consistently improve accuracy; a final experiment found that obtaining information through conversation helped. Conversation does not guarantee honesty, complete understanding or safety. These were specific prediction tasks, not tests of every relationship. Eyal, Steffel and Epley (2018). Perspective mistaking: Accurately understanding the mind of another requires getting perspective, not taking perspective.

Thomas Webb and Paschal Sheeran combined 47 experimental tests in which intentions changed. Changes in behaviour were generally smaller than changes in intention. The result supports separating a promise from follow-through, not deciding why an individual failed to act. Webb and Sheeran (2006). Does changing behavioral intentions engender behavior change? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence.

Martin Hagger and Aleksandra Luszczynska reviewed action-planning interventions in health settings, including differences in results and study quality. A concrete plan may be useful, but health-intervention findings do not validate a family or workplace agreement. Hagger and Luszczynska (2014). Implementation intention and action planning interventions in health contexts: state of the research and proposals for the way forward.

What else could explain it?

A request can be perfectly clear and still impractical, unwanted or unfair. Missing follow-through may concern capacity, priorities or an unspoken disagreement rather than misunderstanding. A polite sentence can also function as a demand if refusal brings punishment. Check both wording and the conditions under which an answer is given.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

What to notice and check.

Specify the action, timing, purpose and relevant constraints. Ask what the person understood and what they can realistically offer. Listen for an actual commitment rather than silence or a vague encouraging response. Include which decisions stay with them and whether they need resources, preparation or a different arrangement.

How to respond.

Use a concrete description of the problem and one proposed change. Explain why it matters without requiring the other person to accept a guessed motive. Invite an alternative that serves the same need. If agreement matters for shared work, record the agreed action and review point. If a direct request could expose you to harm, seek a safer route rather than refining the sentence indefinitely.

A brief fictional example: Fictional example: Instead of “You never support me,” try “Before Friday’s meeting, can you tell me whether you will recommend this proposal, and which concerns remain?” The answer may still be no, but it gives information relevant to your preparation.

Related entries.

These are editorial reading connections, not evidence of a cause or diagnosis in your situation.

Agreeing, then not following through: Check whether both people agreed the same action and timing.

Involving a third person: Clarify whether you are agreeing to mediation, seeking support or bypassing a decision.

Declining help: Ask which kind of help would be useful rather than repeating the same offer.

Interrupting: Ask to finish a point and see whether the pattern changes, where it is safe to do so.

Key takeaways.

A request asks for an action, not a character change.

Clarity does not guarantee agreement or capacity.

Confirm what was actually agreed.

Put it into practice.

Rewrite one vague request as an action, purpose and useful time.

Ask it where appropriate and make room for an honest alternative or refusal.

Review what was understood, agreed and done, including whether the request was realistic.
