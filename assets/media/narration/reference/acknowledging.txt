Understanding people. Acknowledging another perspective.

Acknowledging another perspective means recognising what the person is saying, experiencing or prioritising without necessarily agreeing with their conclusion. It can make an exchange clearer because each person can check whether their concern was understood. It is different from endorsing an unverified fact or taking responsibility for something you did not do.

Remember: Show what you understood before adding your view.

How it may arise.

People can argue past one another when each response addresses the point they expected rather than the one actually raised. A quick reassuring phrase may sound dismissive if it skips the concern. Equally, a careful summary can become a tactic if you use it only to move faster toward your preferred answer.

What the evidence supports.

Tal Eyal and colleagues tested interpersonal predictions across a series of experiments. Imagining another person’s perspective did not consistently improve accuracy; a final experiment found that obtaining information through conversation helped. Conversation does not guarantee honesty, complete understanding or safety. These were specific prediction tasks, not tests of every relationship. Eyal, Steffel and Epley (2018). Perspective mistaking: Accurately understanding the mind of another requires getting perspective, not taking perspective.

Guy Itzchakov and colleagues manipulated listening quality in four laboratory experiments. Attentive, nonjudgmental listening reduced speakers’ social anxiety and helped them recognise mixed attitudes in the studied tasks. The experiments do not show that listening guarantees agreement or repairs a harmful relationship. Itzchakov, Kluger and Castro (2017). I Am Aware of My Inconsistencies but Can Tolerate Them: The Effect of High Quality Listening on Speakers' Attitude Ambivalence.

What else could explain it?

Understanding, validating an experience and agreeing on facts are different actions. You can recognise distress while leaving an explanation uncertain. You can also disagree with an unfair accusation after checking what it means. Acknowledgment does not require accepting abuse, promising a solution or making your own concern disappear.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

What to notice and check.

Identify the point in the person’s own account: a consequence, need, interpretation or request. Ask whether your summary is accurate rather than declaring that you now know how they feel. Notice what they correct. Include your own part in the interaction and whether you have actually left room for an answer.

How to respond.

Use a short, specific summary and then state your view or question. Avoid mechanical phrases that label an emotion the person has not named. If there is a disputed fact, separate it from the effect or concern you can acknowledge. In a harmful exchange, a relevant limit or support route may matter more than continuing the conversation.

A brief fictional example: Fictional example: “You are worried that the new rota removes your only free evening. Have I understood?” acknowledges the practical concern without promising the rota can remain unchanged. The next discussion can address that cost and the other constraints.

Key takeaways.

Acknowledgment is not automatic agreement.

Let the person correct your understanding.

Recognise the concern without erasing your own needs or limits.

Put it into practice.

Choose one disagreement and write the other person’s concern as you understand it.

Offer a brief summary and ask what you missed, if discussion is appropriate.

Review what was corrected and whether the actual issue became easier to discuss.
