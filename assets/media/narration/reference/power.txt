Understanding people. Unequal power.

Unequal power means that people differ in their ability to affect resources, decisions or consequences. It can arise through employment, money, housing, information, social standing, care arrangements or authority. Power is not identical to confidence or status: a quiet person may control crucial resources, while a forceful speaker may have few actual options.

Remember: Look at the real cost of saying no.

How it may arise.

A person’s response depends partly on what disagreement could cost. Agreement in front of an authority figure may not reveal full preference. Someone with power may see a request as optional while the recipient expects a penalty for refusal. Good intentions do not remove that difference, and formal permission to complain does not establish that doing so is safe.

What the evidence supports.

Dacher Keltner, Deborah Gruenfeld and Cameron Anderson reviewed theory and evidence connecting unequal power with opportunities, threats and behavioural tendencies. This is a broad framework, not a universal rule about every powerful or less powerful person. Keltner D, Gruenfeld DH, Anderson C. (2003). Power, approach, and inhibition.

Jason Colquitt and colleagues combined 183 organisational-justice studies. Fair outcomes, procedures, interpersonal treatment and information were related but distinguishable aspects of perceived fairness. Most evidence concerns organisations. Disagreement about fairness is not proof of dishonesty or a single objectively correct allocation. Colquitt and colleagues (2001). Justice at the millennium: a meta-analytic review of 25 years of organizational justice research.

Home Office statutory guidance describes controlling or coercive behaviour as an unfolding pattern and discusses restrictions, threats, economic abuse and effects on everyday freedom. It addresses law and practice in England and Wales. This book does not establish whether a legal offence occurred or replace local advice. What else could explain it?

Legitimate responsibilities and necessary rules can coexist with unequal power. Not every hierarchical decision is abuse. The questions are how authority is used, whether restrictions are relevant and proportionate, and what avenues for challenge exist. Conversely, an informal family arrangement can be controlling without a formal title.

What to notice and check.

Identify who controls the needed resource, whose account is believed and what happens after a disagreement. Compare the written policy with actual practice. Ask whether alternatives are realistic, not merely imaginable. Include your own position and whether someone might be protecting themselves while giving the answer you prefer.

How to respond.

If you hold power, make relevant constraints and decision criteria explicit, reduce avoidable penalties for honest answers and provide a genuine concern route. If your options are constrained, seek advice suited to the setting rather than assuming better wording will remove the imbalance. Where threats or coercion are involved, use appropriate support and avoid testing the limits by provoking a response.

Key takeaways.

Power concerns resources and consequences, not just manner.

A yes can reflect constrained options.

Assess how authority is exercised and challenged.

Put it into practice.

Describe one decision, the relevant resource and the cost of refusal.

Identify a process improvement or support route within your actual options.

Review whether choice and fair challenge became more possible, including any adverse consequences.
