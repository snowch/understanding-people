Unequal power means people differ in their ability to affect resources, decisions or consequences. It can arise through employment, housing, money, information, care arrangements or authority. Power differs from confidence: a quiet person may control something crucial, while a forceful speaker may have few realistic options.

The cue is: look at the real cost of saying no. An answer can reflect preference, constrained alternatives or both. To understand a decision, ask what disagreement could cost rather than relying only on whether the person eventually said yes.

Someone with authority may experience their request as optional, while the recipient expects a penalty for declining. Good intentions do not remove that difference. A policy permitting complaints also does not establish that complaining is safe in practice. Compare the written arrangement with what actually happens after disagreement.

Dacher Keltner, Deborah Gruenfeld and Cameron Anderson reviewed theory and evidence connecting power with opportunities, threats and behavioural tendencies. This is a broad framework. It does not supply a universal rule about how every powerful or less powerful person acts, and cannot explain an individual response by itself.

Necessary rules and legitimate responsibilities can coexist with unequal power. Not every hierarchical decision is abuse. Ask whether restrictions are relevant and proportionate, how authority is exercised and what avenues for challenge exist. Informal family arrangements can also be controlling without anyone holding a formal title.

Jason Colquitt and colleagues combined organisational-justice studies distinguishing fairness of outcomes, processes, treatment and information. Most evidence concerned organisations. These distinctions can inform questions about an arrangement, but disagreement about fairness does not establish dishonesty or one uniquely correct allocation of resources.

Here is a fictional family example. A relative who controls transport insists that an adult attend a gathering. The adult eventually agrees. Their yes needs interpreting alongside the transport options and consequences of refusal. Asking whether they agreed cannot, by itself, show that attendance was freely chosen.

Identify who controls the resource, whose account receives credibility and what follows disagreement. Alternatives need to be realistic, not merely imaginable. If you hold power, consider whether someone might be protecting themselves while giving the answer you prefer. Reassurance from that person does not settle the imbalance.

If you have authority, explain constraints and decision criteria, reduce avoidable penalties for honesty and provide a genuine concern route. If your options are constrained, advice suited to the setting may help more than searching for perfect wording. Do not provoke a response to test how far someone's control extends.
