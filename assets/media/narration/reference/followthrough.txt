Understanding people. Agreeing, then not following through.

Agreeing and then not following through describes a gap between an apparent commitment and later conduct. It can affect trust and transfer work to others. The gap deserves attention, but it does not reveal one cause by itself. First establish what was actually agreed, rather than treating encouragement or silence as a promise.

Remember: Compare the promise with the route to action.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

How it may arise.

Possible contributors include unclear scope, changed capacity, a skill or access problem, competing priorities, avoidance of an uncomfortable no or deliberate disregard. Several can coexist. Wanting an outcome and being able to carry out the next step are different. A repeated gap may also persist because others reliably rescue the work.

What the evidence supports.

Thomas Webb and Paschal Sheeran combined 47 experimental tests in which intentions changed. Changes in behaviour were generally smaller than changes in intention. The result supports separating a promise from follow-through, not deciding why an individual failed to act. Webb and Sheeran (2006). Does changing behavioral intentions engender behavior change? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence.

Susan Michie and colleagues reviewed behaviour-change frameworks and proposed COM-B: capability, opportunity and motivation as interacting conditions for behaviour. They examined its use in tobacco-control and obesity policy. It is a framework for analysing and designing change, not a diagnostic test or proof that one identified barrier caused an action. Michie, van Stralen and West (2011). The behaviour change wheel: a new method for characterising and designing behaviour change interventions.

Martin Hagger and Aleksandra Luszczynska reviewed action-planning interventions in health settings, including differences in results and study quality. A concrete plan may be useful, but health-intervention findings do not validate a family or workplace agreement. Hagger and Luszczynska (2014). Implementation intention and action planning interventions in health contexts: state of the research and proposals for the way forward.

What else could explain it?

A single failure differs from a recurring pattern, while an explanation is not automatically an excuse. Changed conditions can justify renegotiation without making the original impact disappear. Forgetting, unwillingness and inability are different questions. Do not assume that a confident promise guarantees feasibility.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

What to notice and check.

Compare the wording, owner, timing and conditions of the commitment with what happened. Include completed tasks and exceptions, not only failures. Ask when the person knew the plan was at risk and what prevented an early warning. Notice whether your own requests leave room for refusal or rely on unspoken coordination work.

How to respond.

Agree a narrower, realistic action and an early warning point. Clarify what happens if it cannot be completed rather than planning indefinite rescue. You may need a different arrangement when reliability remains insufficient, even without a settled motive. If you are the person missing commitments, acknowledge the effect and change the process rather than offering a larger promise.

A brief fictional example: Fictional example: A friend agrees to book tickets but does not. Asking reveals they expected to be reimbursed first, while you expected payment afterward. Clarifying the process addresses this occasion; later follow-through still needs to show whether the new arrangement works.

Key takeaways.

Establish the commitment before interpreting the gap.

A feasible plan and early warning matter alongside intention.

Repeated unreliability can justify a different arrangement.

Put it into practice.

Compare one missed commitment with its actual wording and conditions.

Agree a feasible next action, owner and warning point.

Review completion and the burden on others, including whether a different arrangement is needed.
