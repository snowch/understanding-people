When someone agrees and does not follow through, the gap can cost you time, money or trust. It deserves attention. But a missed task does not tell you whether the problem was unwillingness, inability, a misunderstanding or something else. First establish the commitment; then examine the route from that commitment to action.

Encouragement, silence and a definite promise are different. So are wanting a result and having a feasible next step. Keeping those distinctions clear lets you address the actual burden without pretending that a confident yes guarantees completion. The example in this lesson is fictional.

Ask what the person agreed to do, who owned each part, when it was due and which conditions applied. Include coordination that may have remained unspoken. If you expected them to arrange transport but they expected you to provide an address first, the apparent task concealed another dependency.

Imagine a friend agreed to book tickets for a shared visit. They did not book, and you lost the cheaper price. Discussion reveals that they expected payment in advance while you expected to reimburse them afterwards. That difference can explain this occasion without removing its cost or establishing their reliability in general.

Capacity can change after an agreement. Someone may lack time, access, information or a skill they expected to have. Another priority may intervene. They may also avoid saying no or disregard the agreement. Several influences can coexist, and this list is a set of questions rather than a verdict.

Look at completed commitments as well as failures. What differed when the person did follow through? Also ask when they realised the plan was at risk. An early warning might have allowed a different arrangement, even when completing the original task was no longer possible.

Thomas Webb and Paschal Sheeran combined forty-seven experiments in which intentions changed. Behaviour generally changed less than intention. This supports separating a promise from subsequent action; it cannot explain one person's missed commitment. A sincere intention and an incomplete task can coexist, while sincerity alone does not solve the practical problem.

Susan Michie and colleagues proposed a framework connecting capability, opportunity and motivation after reviewing approaches to behaviour change. It helps organise questions, rather than diagnose a barrier. Reviews of planning interventions by Martin Hagger and Aleksandra Luszczynska concerned health settings, with varied results and study quality. They do not validate every family or workplace agreement.

A practical response is a smaller action with an owner, a workable time and an early warning point. Agree what happens if it cannot be completed. Otherwise the plan may quietly depend on you rescuing the task every time, while the burden of that rescue remains invisible.

If you missed the commitment, acknowledge its effect and offer a feasible process change rather than a larger promise. If someone else's repeated unreliability continues, you can choose a different arrangement without settling their motive. Check whether your requests leave room for an honest refusal, while keeping shared responsibilities clear.

Think of one ordinary missed commitment. What exactly was agreed, and what first step or condition was necessary? Choose one detail to clarify before the next attempt. Pause the player if you would like longer.

For the fictional tickets, a clearer arrangement could state when payment will arrive, who books and when to warn the group if booking cannot happen. It still needs review. Afterwards, compare completion, early warning and the work carried by others. A clearer process is useful only if later conduct makes it workable.

Keep the cue: compare the promise with the route to action. Understanding a barrier and deciding what reliability you need are related, but different, decisions.
