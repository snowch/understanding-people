Understanding people. Stonewalling, silence and an agreed pause.

Stonewalling is commonly used for a pattern of shutting down communication so that an issue cannot be addressed. An agreed pause is different: it allows space while keeping a workable route to return. Neither silence nor leaving a conversation alone establishes an intention to punish or control.

Remember: Look for a workable return, not forced immediate engagement.

How it may arise.

A person may be overwhelmed, afraid, unsure how to respond, unwilling to discuss the issue or trying to exert pressure. Your own pursuit, criticism or threats may affect the exchange. Those possibilities need examining without making one person responsible for another’s chosen harmful conduct.

What the evidence supports.

George Bonanno and Charles Burton reviewed coping and emotion-regulation research. Their framework emphasises noticing context, having several response options and adjusting to feedback. It is a framework with acknowledged measurement limits, not evidence that one response or this worksheet always works. Bonanno and Burton (2013). Regulatory Flexibility: An Individual Differences Perspective on Coping and Emotion Regulation.

Stefan Hofmann and Angela Hay reviewed avoidance in anxiety treatment, including circumstances in which avoidance can be adaptive. Function and actual danger matter. This is not a reason to remove someone’s safety arrangements or conduct exposure treatment yourself. Hofmann and Hay (2018). Rethinking avoidance: Toward a balanced approach to avoidance in treating anxiety disorders.

NHS public guidance describes emotional, physical, sexual and economic abuse and routes to confidential support. Services are UK-specific. It is guidance, not a test that determines a person’s motive or an individual safety assessment. NHS: Domestic violence and abuse.

What else could explain it?

A request to stop an abusive conversation should not be treated as stonewalling. Privacy and refusal of unwanted contact also matter. At the same time, shared bills, care or decisions may need an alternative route when direct discussion stops. A pause that repeatedly leaves all consequences to someone else is operationally different from a pause followed by follow-through.

What to notice and check.

Notice what precedes the shutdown, whether a return point is offered and whether it is kept. Ask what medium or timing would make the practical issue discussable. Compare calm and heated settings where appropriate. Include what happens when you respect a pause instead of repeatedly seeking an immediate response.

How to respond.

For an ordinary shared problem, agree a time or written route to return and the decision that must be made. Do not demand emotional disclosure as the price of a pause. If no route is workable, consider another arrangement or appropriate support. Where threats or coercion are involved, prioritise support over a communication exercise or informal mediation.

A brief fictional example: Fictional example: After an argument about a payment, one person asks for an hour and later returns to agree the amount. That is different from repeatedly refusing every discussion until the other person pays everything to end the silence.

Key takeaways.

A pause and an indefinite communication barrier are different.

A return arrangement and later conduct are informative.

Safety and shared responsibilities both require attention.

Put it into practice.

Describe one shutdown and any proposed or completed return to the issue.

Where safe, agree a bounded return route or another practical process.

Review whether the issue became discussable and whether support or a changed arrangement is needed.
