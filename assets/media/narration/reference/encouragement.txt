Understanding people. Support someone’s next step.

Encouragement supports someone’s effort or next action while recognising their own goals and choices. It may involve confidence, useful information, practical help or noticing progress. It differs from telling someone that success is guaranteed, dismissing a difficulty or making your approval conditional on an outcome.

Remember: Help the next step belong to the person taking it.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

How it may arise.

A person can value an aim while finding the next step unclear, difficult or inaccessible. Another person’s enthusiasm may help them start, or add pressure to appear confident. Effective support therefore depends on what is missing rather than the intensity of a motivational speech. Enjoyment, personal value and external incentives are distinguishable reasons to act.

What the evidence supports.

Richard Ryan and Edward Deci reviewed self-determination theory and evidence concerning autonomy, competence and relatedness in motivation and well-being. The framework suggests distinctions to investigate. It cannot identify the cause of a particular action or refusal. Ryan and Deci (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being.

Susan Michie and colleagues reviewed behaviour-change frameworks and proposed COM-B: capability, opportunity and motivation as interacting conditions for behaviour. They examined its use in tobacco-control and obesity policy. It is a framework for analysing and designing change, not a diagnostic test or proof that one identified barrier caused an action. Michie, van Stralen and West (2011). The behaviour change wheel: a new method for characterising and designing behaviour change interventions.

Joshua Howard and colleagues combined studies of student motivation and outcomes. Different forms of motivation showed different associations with learning and well-being. Many associations are not causal; applying the findings outside education requires care. Howard and colleagues (2021). Student Motivation and Associated Outcomes: A Meta-Analysis From Self-Determination Theory.

Christopher Cerasoli and colleagues combined research on motivation, incentives and performance. Intrinsic motivation and incentives both mattered, with differences between quality and quantity. Rewards are neither universally helpful nor universally harmful; performance findings cannot identify one person’s values. Cerasoli, Nicklin and Ford (2014). Intrinsic motivation and extrinsic incentives jointly predict performance: a 40-year meta-analysis.

What else could explain it?

Encouragement cannot replace training, time, equipment, accommodations or a realistic plan. A person may also choose a different goal. Rewards can influence performance without addressing every need. Avoid treating a refusal to pursue your preferred ambition as a motivation defect, especially where you control resources or approval.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

What to notice and check.

Ask what the person is trying to achieve, what part is difficult and whether support is wanted. Look for a next step small enough to attempt and informative enough to learn from. Notice actual progress and effort without praising work that has not happened. Compare your hopes with the person’s own priorities.

How to respond.

Offer a specific kind of help and let the person retain the appropriate decisions. Give feedback about what became more effective and what remains to learn. Agree a review point if you share responsibility, and make it possible to report difficulties honestly. A revised plan can be progress when the earlier one was unrealistic.

A brief fictional example: Fictional example: A son struggles to begin revision. Instead of “You can do anything if you try,” ask which topic is unclear and offer to help find practice questions. His answers then show what he needs to learn, while the work remains his.

Key takeaways.

Encouragement should support the person’s goal and agency.

Match help to the actual missing condition.

Notice usable progress without promising a result.

Put it into practice.

Ask about one goal and the specific next-step difficulty, including whether help is wanted.

Offer one suitable support or a small attempt with room for feedback.

Review learning, feasibility and ownership, rather than whether confidence sounded high.
