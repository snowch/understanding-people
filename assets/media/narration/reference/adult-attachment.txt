Adult attachment research examines expectations and responses around closeness, support and separation. Two commonly studied dimensions are attachment anxiety, or concern about rejection and availability, and attachment avoidance, or discomfort with dependence and closeness. These are dimensions, not diagnoses or permanent kinds of person.

Earlier experiences can contribute to expectations, while present relationships and circumstances also matter. You may respond differently to a dependable friend and an unreliable partner. A story about childhood is a possibility to explore through someone’s account, not something established by a refusal or unanswered message.

Zhang and colleagues combined studies relating these dimensions to mental-health measures. The associations do not diagnose anyone or establish a childhood cause. Benjamin Hadden and colleagues reviewed relationships with satisfaction and commitment; differences between longer and shorter relationships in cross-sectional samples do not establish an individual’s future.

In a fictional example, a person worries when a partner repeatedly cancels without warning but feels secure with friends who keep plans. That contrast gives a reason to examine current reliability before declaring the person inherently anxious. Broader expectations could still matter; one explanation need not exclude another.

Ask which situations trigger concern about rejection or loss of choice, and what reassurance or distance actually changes. Does the concern fit current conduct? Compare exceptions and include whether your own inconsistency or repeated pursuit affects the interaction.

Independence is not automatically avoidance. Reassurance may be appropriate in a genuinely inconsistent situation. Workload, safety, communication preferences, cultural practices and trauma may also affect contact or help-seeking. Avoidant attachment is different from avoidant personality disorder.

Where you can, be clear about availability: say when you will reply, ask directly about a need and respect the answer. Support can include reliable contact and chosen independence. Someone should not have to accept a style label to have their concern heard.

You cannot use enough reassurance to guarantee that another person feels secure, or require closeness because you see independence as avoidance. Both people’s boundaries matter. If recurring distress or relationship difficulties impair life, professional support may help; this reference cannot determine a cause or treatment.

Think of a difficulty around closeness or support, and a relationship or occasion where it differs. What do the current facts show, and what remains an attachment explanation? Pause if useful.
