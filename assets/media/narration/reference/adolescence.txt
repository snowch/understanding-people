Understanding people. Adolescence: autonomy, peers and support.

Adolescence involves development alongside changing relationships, responsibilities and opportunities for choice. Wanting more privacy, peer contact or a say in decisions can be ordinary parts of this period. A developmental description should help adults ask better questions, not become a complete explanation for everything a young person does.

Remember: Separate growing agency from a specific difficulty.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

How it may arise.

Learning, sleep, social expectations, stress and practical experience can all affect a task. A young person may be capable in a familiar setting and need support in a new one. Adults may also be changing the responsibilities they expect without making the new arrangement explicit. Avoid treating a broad brain-development account as proof of an individual’s motives or incapacity.

What the evidence supports.

NIMH public guidance describes adolescence as a period of continuing development, learning, changing social experience and sleep needs. Broad developmental patterns do not establish an individual teenager’s judgment, motives or capacity. NIMH: The teen brain — 7 things to know.

Richard Ryan and Edward Deci reviewed self-determination theory and evidence concerning autonomy, competence and relatedness in motivation and well-being. The framework suggests distinctions to investigate. It cannot identify the cause of a particular action or refusal. Ryan and Deci (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being.

Susan Michie and colleagues reviewed behaviour-change frameworks and proposed COM-B: capability, opportunity and motivation as interacting conditions for behaviour. They examined its use in tobacco-control and obesity policy. It is a framework for analysing and designing change, not a diagnostic test or proof that one identified barrier caused an action. Michie, van Stralen and West (2011). The behaviour change wheel: a new method for characterising and designing behaviour change interventions.

What else could explain it?

A difficulty may concern teaching, workload, exclusion, health, accessibility or a genuinely unwanted goal. Disagreement is not automatically rebellion, and willingness to comply does not establish that the arrangement is understood. A marked change in mood, functioning or safety requires attention beyond a general statement about teenagers.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

What to notice and check.

Ask what the young person wants, what the task requires and which decisions they can reasonably own. Compare performance under clearer instructions or different conditions. Include sleep and competing demands without using them as automatic excuses. Notice whether you interpret a similar action more harshly because of age.

How to respond.

Offer appropriate choices within clearly explained responsibilities and safety constraints. Agree a manageable next step, specific support and a review point. Let feedback change the arrangement instead of turning every missed task into a debate about maturity. If difficulties are persistent or impairing, seek suitable educational or health support with the young person involved as appropriate.

Key takeaways.

Development provides context, not a verdict about an individual.

Privacy and agency can coexist with appropriate responsibilities.

Specific support questions are more useful than age-based blame.

Put it into practice.

Choose one task and ask what is difficult from the young person’s perspective.

Agree an appropriate choice, support or small change in conditions.

Review learning and responsibility together; seek support for marked or persistent difficulties.
