Understanding people. Culture: meanings without stereotypes.

Culture includes learned ways of making sense of relationships, duties, communication and everyday life. A gesture, silence or refusal can carry different meanings across settings. These differences are worth exploring, but people also vary within communities and participate in several overlapping social worlds.

Remember: Ask about meaning instead of assigning a national script.

How it may arise.

Family practices, language, education, work, religion, migration and personal experience can all shape meaning. Research using a narrow population may not travel unchanged to another. That is a reason to ask about applicability, not a reason to assume that everyone from a country is independent, collective, direct or deferential.

What the evidence supports.

Vivian Vignoles and colleagues conducted two multinational surveys. Ways of being independent and connected varied across several dimensions, beyond a simple East–West division. Group patterns cannot reveal one person’s values, and national averages are not instructions for interacting with an individual. Vignoles and colleagues (2016). Beyond the 'east-west' dichotomy: Global variation in cultural models of selfhood.

Joseph Henrich, Steven Heine and Ara Norenzayan compared behavioural findings across populations and examined reliance on Western, educated samples. Findings from a narrow sample need checking before they are generalised; the review does not assign a personality to a culture. Henrich, Heine and Norenzayan (2010). The weirdest people in the world?

Sociologist Annette Lareau used ethnographic observation and interviews with US families to examine how class-related practices affected interactions with institutions. The study concerns particular families and settings; it is not a universal parenting typology or an explanation of an individual family. Lareau (2002). Invisible Inequality: Social Class and Childrearing in Black Families and White Families.

What else could explain it?

An unfamiliar action can concern personality, circumstances or a simple misunderstanding rather than culture. Culture should not be used to excuse harm, deny choice or treat someone as a spokesperson for a group. A person may value a tradition, revise it or disagree with others in the same family.

What to notice and check.

Ask what the action or arrangement means to the person and what they expected would happen. Check specific words and practices rather than relying on a broad label. Compare whether each side recognises the same obligation or intention. Notice when you treat your own familiar practice as neutral and the other person’s as cultural.

How to respond.

Explain your understanding, invite theirs and negotiate the practical issue where possible. Use interpretation or accessible information when needed; do not make someone defend an entire culture before you address a concrete concern. Shared decisions can respect different meanings while making consent, responsibilities and limits explicit.

A brief fictional example: Fictional example: One person treats declining a first offer of food as politeness; another hears it as a final refusal. Asking directly and kindly about preferences resolves the immediate uncertainty more usefully than deciding that either person is dishonest or disrespectful.

Key takeaways.

Cultural meanings vary within as well as between groups.

Your own expectations are situated too.

Clarify the particular meaning and negotiate the practical arrangement.

Put it into practice.

Choose one interaction in which you may have assumed a shared meaning.

Ask or explain what the specific action meant, avoiding a group stereotype.

Review whether the difference was cultural, situational or still uncertain, and what arrangement helps.
