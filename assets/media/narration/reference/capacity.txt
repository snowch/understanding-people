Understanding people. Time, resources and capacity.

Capacity concerns what someone can realistically take on under current conditions. Time, money, health, information, tools, access and existing responsibilities all matter. Capacity is not the same as willingness, and a person’s earlier performance does not guarantee the same capacity today.

Remember: A plan needs resources, not just goodwill.

How it may arise.

Overcommitment can arise when each request is considered separately, coordination work is invisible or saying no is costly. A task may also require resources the requester assumes are available. These possibilities need checking. Someone can have more capacity for one activity than another because the demands differ, rather than because they are pretending to be busy.

What the evidence supports.

Susan Michie and colleagues reviewed behaviour-change frameworks and proposed COM-B: capability, opportunity and motivation as interacting conditions for behaviour. They examined its use in tobacco-control and obesity policy. It is a framework for analysing and designing change, not a diagnostic test or proof that one identified barrier caused an action. Michie, van Stralen and West (2011). The behaviour change wheel: a new method for characterising and designing behaviour change interventions.

Thomas Webb and Paschal Sheeran combined 47 experimental tests in which intentions changed. Changes in behaviour were generally smaller than changes in intention. The result supports separating a promise from follow-through, not deciding why an individual failed to act. Webb and Sheeran (2006). Does changing behavioral intentions engender behavior change? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence.

Jason Colquitt and colleagues combined 183 organisational-justice studies. Fair outcomes, procedures, interpersonal treatment and information were related but distinguishable aspects of perceived fairness. Most evidence concerns organisations. Disagreement about fairness is not proof of dishonesty or a single objectively correct allocation. Colquitt and colleagues (2001). Justice at the millennium: a meta-analytic review of 25 years of organizational justice research.

What else could explain it?

A stated capacity limit may be accurate, incomplete or a polite way to decline. That uncertainty does not mean you can demand intrusive proof. Distinguish a skill gap, a temporary overload and a different priority. In shared work, the practical consequence of an unmet responsibility still needs addressing even when the reason is reasonable.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

What to notice and check.

Ask what the task requires, what else is already committed and which dependencies or access barriers exist. Include planning, travel, caregiving and recovery rather than counting only visible task time. Compare an easier occasion or a narrower version of the request. Notice whether someone else has become the automatic backup.

How to respond.

Adjust scope, timing, resources or ownership and make trade-offs explicit. Encourage an early warning when a commitment becomes unrealistic. If you are at capacity, state what you can offer and what must move or stop. A practical capacity conversation should not become a competition over who is most exhausted or most deserving.

Key takeaways.

Willingness and present capacity are different.

Count invisible work and real access barriers.

A limit should lead to a practical trade-off or alternative arrangement.

Put it into practice.

Choose one commitment and list its actual requirements and competing responsibilities.

Reduce scope, move timing or clarify ownership where appropriate.

Review whether the change made the arrangement feasible without silently transferring the burden.
