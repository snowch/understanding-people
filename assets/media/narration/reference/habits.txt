Understanding people. Habits and situations.

A habit is a response that becomes more automatic through repetition in recurring circumstances. It can help daily life run smoothly, but can also continue after a goal changes. Automatic does not mean inevitable, unconscious in every case or outside all responsibility. A repeated action is not necessarily a habit.

Remember: Change the cue and the easy next action.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

How it may arise.

Settings, timing, objects and preceding actions can become cues. A person may check a phone whenever a conversation pauses or take over a task whenever someone hesitates. Deliberate goals and automatic responses can coexist. Simply deciding to stop may leave the familiar cue and convenient response unchanged.

What the evidence supports.

Wendy Wood and Dennis Rünger reviewed habit research. Repetition in recurring contexts can make responses more automatic, alongside deliberate goals. Not every repeated action is a habit. A pattern may also reflect ongoing constraints, decisions or coercion. Wood and Rünger (2016). Psychology of Habit.

Susan Michie and colleagues reviewed behaviour-change frameworks and proposed COM-B: capability, opportunity and motivation as interacting conditions for behaviour. They examined its use in tobacco-control and obesity policy. It is a framework for analysing and designing change, not a diagnostic test or proof that one identified barrier caused an action. Michie, van Stralen and West (2011). The behaviour change wheel: a new method for characterising and designing behaviour change interventions.

Martin Hagger and Aleksandra Luszczynska reviewed action-planning interventions in health settings, including differences in results and study quality. A concrete plan may be useful, but health-intervention findings do not validate a family or workplace agreement. Hagger and Luszczynska (2014). Implementation intention and action planning interventions in health contexts: state of the research and proposals for the way forward.

What else could explain it?

Repetition may reflect a current preference, an ongoing duty, lack of alternatives or a response to real danger. A new pattern can also signal a change in health or circumstances. Distinguish an established cue–response sequence from a one-off lapse or a task that remains genuinely necessary. Do not explain another person’s behaviour as automatic merely because they cannot give a satisfying reason.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

What to notice and check.

Notice when and where the action happens, what precedes it and what immediate outcome follows. Compare times it is easier not to do it. Include fatigue, access and the social arrangement. If you are the observer, ask whether the person experiences the action as habitual rather than diagnosing it from outside.

How to respond.

Choose a specific replacement and adjust the setting to make it easier. You might put the phone out of reach for a short agreed period or place a needed item beside an existing routine. Keep the change small enough to repeat and review. Where the behaviour affects others, agree a useful cue rather than appointing someone to police you indefinitely.

A brief fictional example: Fictional example: You interrupt with advice whenever a friend pauses. You agree to ask “Do you want ideas or just a listener?” before offering solutions. The cue is still present, but there is a clearer alternative response to practise.

Key takeaways.

Habit involves a recurring context and a more automatic response.

A goal change may need an environment change too.

Repeated conduct can have explanations other than habit.

Put it into practice.

Identify one recurring action and a specific cue that precedes it.

Make one replacement action easy in that setting.

Review several occasions, including misses, and adjust the cue or size of the change.
