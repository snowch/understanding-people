Understanding people. Indirect resistance and passive aggression.

Passive aggression is an everyday label for indirect expressions of resistance or hostility, such as pointed comments or obstructive conduct. It is an interpretation, not a validated category this book can identify from a few incidents. Describing the actual action is usually more useful than beginning with the label.

Remember: Name the indirect action without assuming its motive.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

How it may arise.

Indirect resistance may arise when disagreement feels costly, a person does not know how to raise it, or they are angry and avoiding an explicit conversation. It may also be a mistaken interpretation of delay, humour or a practical obstacle. The effect can still need addressing even when intent is uncertain.

What the evidence supports.

Tal Eyal and colleagues tested interpersonal predictions across a series of experiments. Imagining another person’s perspective did not consistently improve accuracy; a final experiment found that obtaining information through conversation helped. Conversation does not guarantee honesty, complete understanding or safety. These were specific prediction tasks, not tests of every relationship. Eyal, Steffel and Epley (2018). Perspective mistaking: Accurately understanding the mind of another requires getting perspective, not taking perspective.

Thomas Webb and Paschal Sheeran combined 47 experimental tests in which intentions changed. Changes in behaviour were generally smaller than changes in intention. The result supports separating a promise from follow-through, not deciding why an individual failed to act. Webb and Sheeran (2006). Does changing behavioral intentions engender behavior change? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence.

Dacher Keltner, Deborah Gruenfeld and Cameron Anderson reviewed theory and evidence connecting unequal power with opportunities, threats and behavioural tendencies. This is a broad framework, not a universal rule about every powerful or less powerful person. Keltner D, Gruenfeld DH, Anderson C. (2003). Power, approach, and inhibition.

What else could explain it?

Forgetting, a capacity limit, a need for privacy and legitimate refusal are not automatically covert hostility. An indirect complaint may contain a real issue. Conversely, repeated obstruction can make an arrangement unworkable without a confession of anger. Do not confuse an ability to remain polite with an absence of harm.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

What to notice and check.

Identify what happened, the agreed task or meaning at issue and whether similar events recur under comparable conditions. Ask a direct, non-accusatory question about the practical difficulty where safe. Notice whether you allow explicit disagreement or punish it, then criticise the person for expressing it indirectly.

How to respond.

For an ordinary problem, invite a clear answer and address the specific consequence. “Do you still agree to this task?” can be more useful than “Why are you passive-aggressive?” Make the next agreement realistic. If the pattern continues, change the arrangement or use a suitable process rather than repeatedly debating an inferred attitude.

A brief fictional example: Fictional example: A housemate agrees to a chore but repeatedly leaves it unfinished while making jokes about your standards. Clarifying the required outcome, whether they agree and what alternative is workable addresses the task without assuming one explanation for every delay.

Key takeaways.

The label interprets conduct rather than proving hidden anger.

Make explicit disagreement possible.

Address recurring consequences even when motive remains uncertain.

Put it into practice.

Describe one indirect action and the concrete problem it creates.

Ask a clear question about agreement or barriers where appropriate.

Review follow-through and whether the arrangement needs changing, not whether you obtained a motive admission.
