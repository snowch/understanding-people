Understanding people. Trying to punish.

Trying to punish is a possible intention to impose a cost after an unwanted action. The cost may be explicit, such as a stated sanction, or suspected from withdrawal, humiliation or loss of access. An unpleasant consequence alone does not prove punishment was intended. It does tell you something about the arrangement that needs examining.

Remember: Look at the imposed cost, not a guessed motive.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

How it may arise.

Consequences can be used to deter an action, express anger, enforce a rule or regain control. A person might describe the same action as teaching a lesson, fairness or self-protection. These explanations require checking against what was agreed, the proportionality of the response and the freedom to disagree. Good intentions do not erase an imposed harm.

What the evidence supports.

Home Office statutory guidance describes controlling or coercive behaviour as an unfolding pattern and discusses restrictions, threats, economic abuse and effects on everyday freedom. It addresses law and practice in England and Wales. This book does not establish whether a legal offence occurred or replace local advice. Jason Colquitt and colleagues combined 183 organisational-justice studies. Fair outcomes, procedures, interpersonal treatment and information were related but distinguishable aspects of perceived fairness. Most evidence concerns organisations. Disagreement about fairness is not proof of dishonesty or a single objectively correct allocation. Colquitt and colleagues (2001). Justice at the millennium: a meta-analytic review of 25 years of organizational justice research.

The US Equal Employment Opportunity Commission describes harassment linked to protected characteristics and retaliation within the laws it administers. Legal definitions, coverage and procedures vary by jurisdiction. Unfair or harmful conduct can still matter when it does not meet this legal definition. US EEOC: Harassment.

What else could explain it?

A boundary about your own participation differs from a penalty designed to force another person’s compliance. Stopping a conversation because of shouting can be a limit; threatening unrelated financial harm because someone disagrees is a different action. Natural consequences, formal role responsibilities and retaliation also need distinguishing.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

What to notice and check.

Identify the triggering event, the consequence, who controls it and how it ends. Was the rule clear, relevant and legitimately applied? Can the affected person challenge it safely? Compare whether similar situations receive similar responses. In your own conduct, ask whether you are protecting a reasonable limit or trying to make another person suffer for disappointing you.

How to respond.

For shared agreements, make consequences relevant, proportionate and transparent, with a route to repair or review. Avoid humiliation and unrelated deprivation. If threats or dependence make refusal unsafe, seek appropriate support instead of testing whether the person will carry out the penalty. You do not need to settle their inner motive before taking the consequences seriously.

A brief fictional example: Fictional example: A colleague is removed from a necessary email chain after raising a concern. The timing is worth documenting, but does not alone prove retaliation. Ask about access through a suitable channel and consider other evidence about the decision.

Key takeaways.

An imposed consequence and an inferred intention are different.

Boundaries and coercive penalties should not be treated as synonyms.

Relevance, proportionality and safe challenge matter.

Put it into practice.

Write one triggering action and the concrete consequence without assigning intent.

Check an applicable agreement or seek advice about a proportionate response.

Review access, fairness and practical effects; do not provoke retaliation to obtain proof.
