Thinking and interpretation.

How we form explanations, check beliefs and judge other people.

Confirmation bias.

Why we favour evidence that fits a belief, how to notice it, and how to check fairly.

Correspondence bias.

Why we explain behaviour through character and overlook the situation.

Identity and beliefs.

A belief can connect to someone’s experience, commitments and sense of who they are.

Observation and interpretation.

Separate what someone said or did from your explanation of it.

Truth, lies and uncertainty.

Check claims through relevant evidence. Eye contact, nervousness, confidence or a compelling story do not reliably establish honesty.

Uncertainty.

Not knowing what is happening or what will happen next can be relevant to an interaction.

Behaviour and relationships.

Recognise actions and patterns, compare possible explanations and examine your own contribution.

Declining help.

Compare reasons for turning down help, change the offer and review what happens.

Acknowledging another perspective.

Recognising the concern or experience someone expresses without claiming you agree with every conclusion.

Agreeing, then not following through.

Someone agrees to an action, but it does not happen as expected.

Avoiding vulnerability.

Holding back feelings, uncertainty or needs that would expose something personally important.

Contempt and belittling.

Communicating that another person is inferior or not worth taking seriously, for example through ridicule or degrading remarks.

Control of money and resources.

Restricting access to money, information, work or essentials in a way that limits another person’s choices.

Deflecting or changing the subject.

Moving a discussion away from the issue being raised, so it remains unresolved.

Denying an event or agreement.

Rejecting an account of what was said, done or agreed. The denial itself does not establish whether the person is mistaken or being deceptive.

Difficulty trusting.

Finding it hard to rely on another person’s word, intentions or handling of something important.

Dismissing another person’s needs.

Responding to a stated need or feeling as though it does not deserve consideration. This describes a response, not a measurement of someone’s capacity for empathy.

Emotional blackmail and conditional threats.

An everyday term for pressure that makes avoiding a threatened emotional or relational consequence conditional on compliance.

Entitlement, admiration and others’ needs.

Behaviours often described as narcissistic include demanding special treatment, seeking admiration or repeatedly discounting others’ needs. These descriptions do not establish narcissistic personality disorder.

Favours that create unwanted obligation.

Giving help or gifts that are later treated as creating obligations the recipient did not agree to.

Gaslighting and undermining your reality.

A pattern of undermining someone’s confidence in their memory, perception or account of events, often through denial, distortion or attacks on their credibility.

Guilt-tripping and pressure through obligation.

Using guilt or a claimed duty to press someone toward a choice, especially when their refusal is not accepted.

Indirect resistance and passive aggression.

An everyday description of opposition or resentment expressed indirectly rather than through a clear disagreement.

Interrupting.

Beginning to speak before another person has finished.

Intimidation and threats.

Words or actions that create fear of consequences, such as threats, menacing conduct or destruction of belongings.

Involving a third person.

Bringing another person into a disagreement, whether for help, mediation or influence.

Listening and checking understanding.

Giving someone room to speak, then checking whether you understood what matters to them.

Minimising harm or impact.

Treating an effect on someone as too small or unreasonable to deserve attention.

Monitoring and demands for access.

Checking messages, movements or accounts, or demanding access to them. Consent and the ability to withdraw it matter.

People-pleasing and automatic agreement.

Agreeing or accommodating before checking one’s own wishes, limits or capacity, often to avoid disappointing someone.

Possessiveness and demands for exclusivity.

Treating another person’s attention, time or relationships as something one is entitled to control.

Repair after a misunderstanding.

Returning to an exchange to clarify, take responsibility for your actions and discuss what could work differently.

Repeated criticism.

Repeated negative comments about someone’s actions, choices or work. Describe the comments before interpreting the pattern.

Repeatedly asking for reassurance.

Returning to a question or request for reassurance after receiving an answer.

Repeatedly pushing a stated limit.

Continuing to press, bypass or test a limit after it has been communicated.

Responding defensively.

Pushing back against perceived criticism, for example by justifying, denying or counter-criticising.

Restricting contact with other people.

Interfering with someone’s access to friends, family, work or other support.

Restriction, monitoring and coercive patterns.

Repeated restrictions, threats, monitoring or control of money and contact can limit someone’s freedom. The impact matters even when intent is disputed.

Retaliation after a refusal or complaint.

An adverse action following a refusal, disagreement or complaint that may be intended to impose a cost.

Shifting responsibility.

Moving responsibility for an action onto someone else instead of addressing the part the speaker chose or controlled.

Stonewalling, silence and an agreed pause.

A term used for shutting down or refusing engagement in a discussion. Distinguish an indefinite refusal from a pause or a decision not to engage.

Taking or withholding credit.

Claiming a contribution one did not make, or leaving another person’s contribution unrecognised.

Taking too much responsibility.

Taking on responsibility for outcomes or other people’s reactions beyond what one can reasonably control or has agreed to do.

Withdrawing from a conversation.

Someone reduces contact or stops taking part in a conversation.

Needs, goals and intentions.

What matters to people, competing priorities and the limits of inferring intentions.

Belonging.

Wanting connection, acceptance or a place in a group.

Capability, opportunity and motivation.

Explore whether a change needs knowledge or skill, different circumstances, or attention to competing motives and habits.

Competing goals.

Two desired outcomes can call for different actions at the same time.

Fair treatment.

Wanting an arrangement or process that feels fair. People can disagree about what fairness requires.

Feeling capable.

Wanting to be effective and able to meet a challenge.

Feeling safe.

Wanting safety, predictability or protection from a feared loss.

Having a say.

Wanting meaningful choice or ownership over what you do.

Motivation.

Explore what someone wants, what competes with it and what makes action possible.

Recognition and status.

Wanting respect, recognition or influence in a group.

Seeking approval.

Giving substantial weight to others’ favourable reactions when making choices or judging one’s own worth.

Trying to avoid conflict.

A possible intention to prevent an argument or its consequences.

Trying to persuade.

A possible intention to change someone’s view or decision. It must be examined in the particular situation.

Trying to protect.

A possible intention to prevent harm or loss to someone.

Trying to punish.

A possible intention to impose a cost in response to a perceived wrong. This is a substantial claim about purpose.

Trying to retain a role.

A possible intention to preserve a familiar role or involvement in decisions.

Personality and individual differences.

Understand broad traits, variation across situations and the limits of labels.

Agreeableness.

A broad personality dimension involving tendencies such as cooperation and consideration.

Conscientiousness.

A broad personality dimension including tendencies toward organisation and dependability.

Extraversion.

A broad personality dimension related to tendencies such as sociability and assertiveness.

Negative emotionality.

A broad personality dimension involving tendencies to experience distress, worry or emotional volatility. It is also often called neuroticism.

Open-mindedness / openness.

A broad personality dimension involving tendencies such as intellectual curiosity, imagination and aesthetic sensitivity.

Emotion, habits and learning.

Stress, expectations and patterns that can develop or change over time.

Consequences that sustain a pattern.

Consider whether what follows a behaviour makes its repetition worthwhile or relieves a difficulty.

Fear of rejection.

Concern about being unwanted, excluded or left. It can shape how ambiguous interactions are interpreted.

Habits and situations.

Repeated responses may be tied to familiar cues and circumstances.

Jealousy and what follows.

Concern about losing a valued relationship or position. The feeling and the actions taken in response need separate consideration.

Learned expectations.

Past experience can shape what a person expects from an interaction. Do not invent that history from present behaviour.

Perfectionism and inflexible standards.

Holding standards so tightly that errors, stopping or accepting an adequate result become difficult.

Stress and overload.

Demands may exceed the time, energy or resources someone feels they have.

Context, relationships and development.

Family, culture, life stages, resources and circumstances around an interaction.

Adolescence: autonomy, peers and support.

A young person’s wish for privacy or independence sits alongside learning, relationships, sleep, responsibilities and real constraints.

Changing roles and life transitions.

Moving, becoming a parent, changing work, retirement or taking on care can alter what people need from one another.

Children: ability, learning and expectations.

Before interpreting a child’s action as unwillingness, consider what they understand, can do and can manage in that setting.

Culture and power.

Consider the expectations, roles and unequal choices around an interaction.

Culture: meanings without stereotypes.

People can use different rules for respect, advice, privacy, duty and disagreement—even within the same family or community.

Family expectations.

Families differ in what they treat as ordinary involvement, duty, privacy and disagreement.

Grief, loss and changing needs.

Loss can change energy, attention, feelings and what kind of contact is welcome. It need not follow a fixed sequence or timetable.

Language, accent and communication context.

Fluency, speed, directness and pauses are imperfect guides to understanding, competence or honesty.

Later life: change without age stereotypes.

Age alone does not tell you someone’s competence, openness, motives or preferred amount of help.

Low mood, withdrawal and support.

Persistent low mood or loss of interest with difficulty in daily life deserves attention. Withdrawal alone does not establish depression.

Neurodiversity and communication fit.

People differ in how they process information, communicate and manage sensory demands. Ask about useful conditions without inferring a diagnosis.

New changes in behaviour or functioning.

A substantial change from someone’s usual behaviour can call for a health conversation rather than a personality explanation.

Relationship history.

Earlier agreements, disappointments or repairs can matter to a present interaction.

Rules, exclusion and unequal access.

A person’s options can be constrained by rules, costs, discrimination or inaccessible arrangements, rather than their motivation.

Time, resources and capacity.

Whether a person can carry out an action with the resources available.

Trauma, reminders and present safety.

Past frightening experiences can matter to current reactions, but a strong reaction does not reveal someone’s history.

Unequal power.

People differ in their ability to refuse, disagree or leave without serious consequences.

Who else is present.

The people watching an interaction may change its meaning or consequences.

Worry, avoidance and reassurance.

Worry can be a response to a real problem. Persistent fear or avoidance that restricts life may also call for professional support.

Responding and collaborating.

Listen, make requests, agree responsibilities, repair and review.

Boundaries.

Decide what you will do when a behaviour affects you, without needing certainty about its motive.

Compare explanations.

Ask what would look different if another explanation were true.

Getting support without needing a label.

You can seek help for distress, changes in functioning or harmful treatment before knowing their cause.

Make a clear request.

Specify the action, timing and scope of what you are asking, while allowing an answer.

Make a workable agreement.

Specify responsibilities, constraints, timing and how either person can raise a problem.

Notice what helps.

Describe a helpful action and its effect so that good experiences can be understood and remembered.

Reciprocity and mutual consideration.

Making room for each person’s needs and contributions over time, without requiring every exchange to be identical.

Review what happened.

Return to the outcome of an action and revise your explanation where appropriate.

Support someone’s next step.

Ask what support would help and offer a manageable contribution while preserving choice.

Taking responsibility and making corrections.

Acknowledging one’s specific actions and their consequences, correcting errors and making realistic changes.

Clinical vocabulary and support.

Understand terminology and distinctions without diagnosing yourself or another person.

Antisocial personality disorder.

A clinical condition involving a pervasive disregard for others’ rights and consequences, assessed with the person’s developmental history and broader behaviour.

Avoidant personality disorder.

A clinical condition involving pervasive social inhibition, feelings of inadequacy and sensitivity to negative evaluation, with significant effects on life.

Borderline personality disorder (BPD).

A clinical condition involving persistent difficulties with emotional regulation, self-image, relationships and often impulsivity; assessment considers the full pattern and impact.

Dependent personality disorder.

A clinical condition involving a pervasive, excessive need to be cared for, associated with difficulty acting independently and fear of separation.

Histrionic personality disorder.

A clinical condition involving a pervasive pattern of excessive emotional expression and attention-seeking, assessed in context and in relation to functioning.

Narcissistic personality disorder (NPD).

A clinical condition involving a pervasive pattern of grandiosity, need for admiration and difficulties with empathy, assessed in the context of functioning and history.

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD).

A condition involving recurring obsessions, compulsions or both, often taking time, causing distress or interfering with daily life.

Obsessive-compulsive personality disorder (OCPD).

A clinical condition involving a pervasive preoccupation with order, perfectionism and control that reduces flexibility or interferes with functioning.

Paranoid personality disorder.

A clinical condition involving pervasive, unwarranted distrust and suspicion, including interpreting others’ motives as hostile or harmful.

Personality disorders: what the term means.

Clinical terms for enduring, pervasive difficulties in patterns of thinking, feeling, relating or responding, associated with significant distress or problems functioning.

Schizoid personality disorder.

A clinical condition involving pervasive detachment from social relationships and limited outward emotional expression, assessed within the broader pattern of functioning.

Schizotypal personality disorder.

A clinical condition involving persistent difficulties with close relationships alongside unusual thinking, perceptions or behaviour, requiring assessment of the wider context.
