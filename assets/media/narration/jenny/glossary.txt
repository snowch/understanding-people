The glossary gives plain-English meanings for terms used in the book. This spoken tour shows how those ideas fit together. The written glossary keeps each definition, a fictional example and links to the relevant chapter and reference entry. A definition does not replace the qualifications in those explanations.

Use a term when it helps you notice a useful difference. Learning the word is not the same as establishing that it applies to a person. The comparisons in this tour are fictional illustrations, not tests of you or somebody else.

Confirmation bias concerns an uneven way of looking for or interpreting information. You notice what supports a belief while giving contrary details too little attention. Correspondence bias concerns an explanation: treating an action as evidence of character while allowing too little for the circumstances.

Motivated reasoning describes how what you want to be true can influence your judgment. These ideas can overlap without being interchangeable. A preferred explanation may lead you to collect matching examples, but an explanation you dislike can also guide what you notice.

Corroboration means additional supporting evidence, especially from an independent source. Repeating the same account is not automatically an independent observation. A base rate concerns how common something is in the relevant group before considering a particular clue. None of these terms supplies a precise probability about a person without appropriate information.

Autonomy means experiencing an action as your own choice and agreeing with doing it. It can include choosing assistance. In self-determination theory, competence concerns feeling able to handle a task and make progress. Relatedness concerns connection and feeling cared about. An offer can support one of these while frustrating another.

Intrinsic motivation means doing something because the activity itself is interesting or enjoyable. Extrinsic motivation concerns a result beyond the activity. That result may be personally valued rather than merely imposed. A gardener may enjoy growing plants, value improving the neighbourhood, or experience both reasons together.

A personality trait is a tendency across occasions, not a description of everything somebody does. The Big Five describes five broad dimensions; it does not divide everyone into five types. Being more outgoing does not determine where someone falls on organisation or another dimension.

A habit is a learned tendency to respond fairly automatically in a familiar situation. It is different from a whole personality. Narrative identity is the changing story a person tells about how their past, present and future fit together. A person's account of independence can matter without establishing why they acted on this occasion.

Working memory holds a small amount of information in mind while you use it. Cognitive flexibility means being able to change your approach when needed. Remembering two possible dates while checking a calendar involves one kind of work; considering a different arrangement when neither date fits involves another.

An if–then plan connects a particular situation to an action. If a visit ends by ten, I will take the next bus is a concrete example. Such a plan still depends on opportunity and a realistic action. Naming a plan does not guarantee that somebody can carry it out.

A descriptive norm concerns what people commonly do, or believe others do. A prescriptive norm concerns what people believe the group expects or approves of. What usually happens and what people think should happen can differ. Silence at a meeting does not establish agreement with either.

An open question invites someone to explain in their own words. A leading question suggests the answer the questioner expects. What do you still need to decide leaves a different opening from you promised on Tuesday, didn't you? Questions can guide an answer as well as gather one.

In this book, manipulation means influence that makes informed, free choice harder. A boundary concerns what you will accept or participate in, backed by an action you can take. You may decline to organise an event you did not agree to organise, without controlling what everyone else decides.

Gaslighting concerns psychological abuse that undermines trust in one's memory, perceptions or judgment. One disagreement about a memory is not enough to establish it. A harmful pattern can deserve support while its precise explanation remains uncertain; a glossary is not a safety assessment or a diagnostic tool.

When a word feels useful, return to the event. Which distinction does the term help you see, what information supports it, and what practical response follows? Use the chapter or reference for the fuller explanation. The aim is more precise understanding, not a larger collection of labels.
