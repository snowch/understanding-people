Negotiation is an attempt to reach an arrangement when interests or preferences differ. A position is the outcome requested; an interest is a concern that outcome serves. Understanding the concern may reveal options beyond one fixed demand, but it cannot guarantee a mutually acceptable bargain exists.

Mediation involves a third person helping a process rather than imposing a decision. Its usefulness depends on the role, authority, consent and freedom to participate. Mediation can be unsuitable when threats or coercion prevent a genuine choice.

Erica Boothby, Gus Cooney and Maurice Schweitzer reviewed negotiation research, emphasising context, interaction and power. The work does not supply a universally effective script. Cooperation and competition can coexist: both parties may benefit from agreement while disagreeing about its terms.

Ask what each proposal would solve, what constraints are real and which commitments depend on someone else. What is the practical alternative if no agreement is reached? Employment, money, housing and care can make a nominal choice costly. Do not assume everyone can walk away equally.

In a fictional example, a client insists on Friday delivery, but a supplier cannot safely complete the whole order. The actual concern is a Monday demonstration. A smaller tested delivery might meet that need if both agree what it includes. Pretending to agree when no feasible option exists creates a later failure.

Compromise is not automatically fair. Splitting a difference can preserve an unreasonable starting point. A person may lack authority or resources to promise something, and bargaining over someone else’s consent or safety is not like choosing a visit time.

Prepare a clear request, the concern it serves and more than one feasible option. Check who can decide and what each person would need to do. Use a facilitator only where the process fits. A respectful refusal or suitable support may be constructive when no acceptable agreement exists.

Repair also needs follow-through. An apology can acknowledge an earlier problem without showing that a new promise will be kept. Separate explanation, responsibility, correction and later conduct rather than searching for wording that should make someone forgive.

Roy Lewicki and colleagues studied judgments of hypothetical apologies. Perceived effectiveness varied with content and the violation described. Those judgments are not evidence of rebuilt trust after real harm. No apology formula obliges forgiveness or renewed access.

Choose a disagreement. What outcome do you want, what concern does it serve, and what is your real alternative without agreement? What has the other person actually said they need? Pause if useful.

For the delivery example, a useful option specifies the tested portion and timing rather than using the word compromise without defining the work. It also acknowledges what cannot safely be promised.

When you can write, offer one feasible option or ask a question that could reveal another. Review clarity, fairness, feasibility and freedom to decline, not just whether an agreement was signed. Later conduct matters more than a reassuring promise alone.
