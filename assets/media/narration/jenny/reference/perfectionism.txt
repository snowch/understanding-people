Perfectionism concerns demanding standards and how people judge themselves or others when those standards are not met. Striving for quality and being preoccupied with mistakes are distinguishable, though they can coexist. Careful work is not inherently a problem. Rigidity, distress and the cost of the standard deserve attention.

Our cue is: define what good enough must achieve. This means deciding what the task actually needs to do. It is not an invitation to remove necessary safeguards or lower every standard. The appropriate endpoint depends on the purpose and consequences of the work.

A bounded task can start to feel like a test of personal worth, safety or acceptance. Rechecking may reduce doubt briefly while delaying completion. But the environment may also genuinely punish small mistakes, or the brief may keep changing. Examine personal and environmental influences together before assuming an internal defect.

Karina Limburg and colleagues combined two hundred and eighty-four studies concerning perfectionism and mental-health outcomes. Perfectionistic concerns and strivings were associated with several difficulties. These associations do not diagnose a disorder or establish that all high standards and careful work are harmful.

Conscientiousness, professional standards, obsessive-compulsive disorder and obsessive-compulsive personality disorder are distinct concepts. A tidy home or a preference for a particular method cannot establish a disorder. Christopher Soto and Oliver John's personality research describes broad dimensions and narrower aspects, rather than fixed types or clinical diagnoses.

Sarah Shihata and colleagues reviewed unresolved questions about intolerance of uncertainty across emotional disorders. Uncertainty can be difficult without implying a disorder. Their review cannot tell you how much uncertainty another person should tolerate. Someone doing safety-critical work may have sound reasons for a rigorous checking process.

Here is a fictional example. You rewrite an ordinary meeting summary five times because it could always be better. Agreeing that it must accurately record decisions, owners and dates gives the document a concrete job. It separates useful accuracy from an impossible requirement to eliminate every possible criticism.

Ask who set the standard, whether it serves the purpose and what would count as completion. Compare the value of another revision with time, rest and other responsibilities. Notice whether an error leads to a specific correction or a judgment that you are a failure. Those reactions call for different kinds of attention.

For a low-risk task, agree completion criteria, a proportionate checking process and a stopping point. Try finishing at that point and then review the actual result. Judge the method by quality and cost as well as your feelings. Feeling entirely certain is not a necessary condition for adequate work.

Include your effect on others. Delegating a task while rejecting every acceptable method can leave someone carrying responsibility without real choice. If standards and doubt cause substantial distress or interfere with daily life, professional support may be more useful than another productivity rule. Support need not wait for a self-diagnosis.

Think of one ordinary, low-risk task. What must it achieve, what check is proportionate, and when could you reasonably stop? Choose an endpoint you could explain to another person. Take a moment, and pause the player for longer if useful.
