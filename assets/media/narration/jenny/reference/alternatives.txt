Comparing explanations means holding more than one plausible account of an event while deciding what information would distinguish them. You are trying to prevent the first convincing story from becoming the only one you investigate. You do not need endless possibilities or an assumption that every account is equally credible.

Begin with what actually happened. An alternative should explain those details rather than erase them. If a task was missed, maybe nothing happened is not an explanation. A capacity problem, a misunderstanding and a competing priority might be alternatives, and more than one cause may apply.

For your first explanation and one or two credible alternatives, ask what you would expect to notice if each were important. Then find a comparison where the predictions differ. Another event that fits every explanation may add little. Include something that would weaken your preferred account, rather than only seeking support.

Here is a fictional example. A colleague misses tasks sent through a new system but completes comparable tasks received another way. Checking whether notifications are accessible can distinguish an access problem from the broad claim that they refuse all work. Improving access provides information, while later follow-through still needs review.

Daniel Gilbert and Patrick Malone reviewed research showing how people can underweight circumstances when explaining another person’s behaviour. Their review does not establish that your particular character judgment is wrong. It suggests a place to check: what conditions have received less attention than the person’s supposed disposition?

Ziva Kunda’s review describes how desired conclusions can shape the reasons people seek and find convincing. A wanted conclusion can still be correct. Ask whether your own explanation protects your pride or settles uncertainty in a way you find appealing, without assuming that this possibility proves your reasoning is biased.

Tal Eyal and colleagues studied interpersonal predictions. Imagining someone else’s perspective did not consistently improve accuracy, while obtaining information through conversation helped in a final experiment. These were specific prediction tasks, not a test of every relationship. Conversation cannot guarantee honesty, complete understanding or safety.

In an ordinary situation, you might clarify an agreement, compare similar occasions or adjust a practical condition with consent. Keep the original observation and add what the check showed. A person’s answer is information to assess alongside conduct and circumstances. Avoid treating one explanation from either person as automatic proof.

Sometimes two explanations remain hard to distinguish. Say what is unknown and choose a practical response based on the consequences. You can change an unreliable arrangement without proving whether the unreliability comes from overload or low priority. A narrower conclusion may be enough for the decision you face.

Alternative explanations should not delay responding to serious harm. Do not provoke, surveil or set traps to collect evidence. Where there is danger, use safer support. Including your own contribution is useful, but it does not require assuming responsibility for someone else’s harmful conduct or finding a reassuring explanation.
