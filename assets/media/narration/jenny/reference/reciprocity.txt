Reciprocity concerns how people respond to one another’s contributions in a relationship. Help, attention, information and care can all be involved. A reciprocal relationship does not need identical contributions on every occasion. Capacity, need and the kind of relationship affect what mutual consideration can reasonably look like.

The useful cue is to look for mutual care over time rather than identical exchanges. That does not require ignoring a persistent one-sided arrangement. It means choosing a fair comparison before deciding what the imbalance tells you, and asking what each person understood the arrangement to be.

One person may offer a gift while another assumes a trade. Help may be part of a role responsibility, or something both people explicitly agree to return later. Trouble grows when an unspoken condition appears after the help has been accepted. The size of the favour cannot tell you its agreed meaning.

Include your own expectations. Have you offered more than you can sustain while hoping someone will infer what you need? Or assumed someone else will always compensate without a conversation? Before substantial help, clarify relevant terms. Gratitude is different from consent to future requests, private access or unrelated obligations.

Russell Cropanzano and Marie Mitchell reviewed social-exchange theory in organisational research. They considered different rules, resources and relationships, while also identifying conceptual ambiguity. Their review does not establish a universal ledger of favours. An idea about exchange cannot tell you exactly what a friend owes you.

Jason Colquitt and colleagues combined organisational-justice studies distinguishing outcomes, procedures, respectful treatment and information. Most evidence concerns organisations. These distinctions can help clarify what people find unfair, but they do not determine a single correct balance for a caring relationship or make every disagreement evidence of selfishness.

Here is a fictional example. You drive a friend to appointments, and they regularly help with forms you find difficult. The time totals may differ, yet both contributions can be valued. If either becomes unmanageable, discuss the arrangement instead of assuming the other person should already know what needs changing.

Consider a reasonable period and include less visible work. A temporary imbalance may reflect illness, care responsibilities or changed resources. A continuing imbalance may still need action. Counting only money, hours or the contribution easiest for you to measure could leave an important part of the relationship out.

You can reduce a contribution you cannot maintain without presenting a moral invoice for everything previously offered. Discuss what is sustainable and what each person can genuinely contribute. A caring relationship can include generosity and limits without requiring every act to be exchanged for the same thing.

If an obligation is enforced through threats or dependence, consider the wider controlling pattern and support options. Think of one ongoing exchange in an ordinary safe relationship. What contributions and constraints might be less visible? Identify one expectation you have not checked, or one contribution you cannot sustain. Take a moment, and pause the player if you would like longer.

A useful next step might be clarifying whether help is a gift or an agreed exchange, or asking to change its frequency. Later, review whether the arrangement is understood, voluntary and workable for both people. Receiving care does not give away your agency, and mutual care need not look identical on each occasion.
