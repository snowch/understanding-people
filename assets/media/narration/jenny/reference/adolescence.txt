Adolescence involves continuing development alongside changing relationships, responsibilities and opportunities for choice. Wanting more privacy, contact with peers or a say in decisions can be ordinary parts of this period. Those changes should help adults ask better questions, rather than become a complete explanation of everything a young person does.

The cue is: separate growing agency from a specific difficulty. A teenager can want more independence and need help with a particular task. Neither fact cancels the other. The useful question concerns what this task requires and what support or choice would make the arrangement workable.

Public guidance from the US National Institute of Mental Health describes adolescence as a period of continuing learning, changing social experience and sleep needs. Broad developmental patterns cannot establish a particular teenager's judgment, motives or capacity. Saying that the brain is still developing does not tell you why this homework remains unfinished.

Richard Ryan and Edward Deci reviewed theory and evidence concerning autonomy, competence and connection in motivation and well-being. Their framework offers distinctions to investigate. It cannot identify the cause of a refusal. Wanting choice, finding a task difficult and feeling disconnected can lead to different practical conversations.

Learning, sleep, stress, social expectations and practical experience can all affect performance. A young person may manage something familiar while needing help in a new setting. Adults may also change responsibilities without explaining the new arrangement. Check whether the expectation was clear before treating a missed task as evidence about maturity.

Susan Michie and colleagues reviewed behaviour-change frameworks and proposed considering capability, opportunity and motivation together. This organises possible questions; it does not prove a cause. Teaching, accessibility, exclusion, workload, health or an unwanted goal may matter, and more than one influence may be present.

Here is a fictional example. A son repeatedly puts off studying. Asking him to show one difficult question might reveal a topic he does not understand. Looking at the timetable might instead reveal more work than the available time allows. A general statement about teenage brains settles neither possibility.

Ask what the young person wants, what they can currently do and which decisions they can reasonably own. Compare clearer instructions or different conditions where appropriate. Disagreement is not automatically rebellion, and apparent compliance does not guarantee understanding. Notice whether you would interpret the same action more harshly because of age.

Offer appropriate choices within explained responsibilities and safety constraints. Agree a manageable next step, the support available and a time to review it. Let feedback change the arrangement instead of turning every difficulty into another argument about whether someone is grown up enough.

A marked change in mood, functioning or safety needs attention beyond a general account of adolescence. Seek suitable educational or health support for persistent or impairing difficulties, involving the young person as appropriate. Developing agency does not mean being left alone with a problem that needs help.
