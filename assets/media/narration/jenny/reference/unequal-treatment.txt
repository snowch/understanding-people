Unequal treatment means people receive different resources, attention, opportunities or rules. A double standard means judging comparable conduct differently without a relevant justification. Unequal and unfair are not always the same: people can have different needs or responsibilities.

Compare the conduct, the circumstances, the rule and the consequence. A younger child needing more help differs from one child being praised for conduct another is punished for. A temporary illness may justify a changed duty without making every later difference necessary.

Jensen and Thomsen combined research involving children and adolescents. Differences in parental treatment were associated with emotional and behavioural difficulties. The largely White samples do not establish an adult in-law’s motive, and an association does not identify a single cause.

In a fictional family event, two siblings are expected to contribute. One is excused for a medical appointment, while another was refused a similar exception. Comparing the circumstances and the stated rule is more useful than immediately declaring one the golden child.

Ask whether there is a relevant difference, whether the explanation is consistently applied and who can question it. Include an exception that challenges your account. You need not dismiss a concern because people are not identical, or count every welcome difference as favouritism.

Jason Colquitt and colleagues combined organisational-justice studies. Outcomes, procedures, interpersonal treatment and information were related but distinguishable aspects of perceived fairness. This helps explain why a reasonable allocation can still be poorly explained. Workplace evidence cannot supply a uniquely correct answer to every family dispute.

Raise the allocation or standard itself. We were both late; why was only one person excluded? Ask for a transparent future rule or a relevant adjustment. You can seek fair treatment without demanding identical affection.

Popular labels such as golden child and scapegoat can name a painful experience, but they can also make a family seem trapped in a permanent script. Describe what someone receives or is blamed for instead. A favoured child need not welcome the role or be responsible for another adult’s conduct.

If you make the rules, explain relevant differences and be willing to correct inconsistencies. A single serious discriminatory or threatening incident can warrant action. A long pattern is useful for understanding recurrence, not a prerequisite for asking for help.
