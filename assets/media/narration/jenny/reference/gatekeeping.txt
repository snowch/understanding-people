Gatekeeping describes influencing access to information, participation, people or resources. It can help involvement or restrict it. Coordinating care and protecting privacy may be legitimate responsibilities. The same broad word can also describe exclusion or control, so start with the actual access decision.

An organiser may hold key information, have an agreed responsibility or have gradually become the default coordinator. They might be managing risk, avoiding conflict, preserving a role or excluding someone. These possibilities require checking; the label does not establish a strategy.

Reis and colleagues reviewed studies of parental gatekeeping and parenting. They describe complex relationships and disagreement about concepts and measurement. The literature largely concerns coparenting and father involvement. It cannot directly establish a grandmother’s or in-law’s intentions.

What access was requested, whose consent matters and who can grant it? Is the rule explained and consistent? Does the organiser hold the information, or are they waiting for it too? Include whether your own request assumes a right that has not been agreed.

In a fictional example, one sibling receives all updates about a parent’s care while others feel excluded. The parent wants some medical details private but welcomes a shared schedule. Sharing the schedule with consent can solve a coordination problem without making all medical information a family entitlement.

Family-role research distinguishes uncertainty about membership or duties from personal limits. Being the organiser does not automatically confer the right to decide everything. Equally, family membership does not create unrestricted rights to another adult’s information or contact.

Clarify the role and the actual need. An agreed update process can sometimes reduce dependence on one messenger. Seek the least intrusive route that meets the need while respecting the relevant person’s wishes and legitimate refusals.

Children’s needs, safeguarding and legal arrangements may constrain access. Do not bypass someone covertly to test a theory. If restrictions involve threats, financial control or coercion, appropriate qualified support may matter more than negotiating an information channel.

Think of one access decision. What was requested, whose consent was needed and what reason was given? Can you identify a legitimate limit as well as a concerning possibility? Pause if useful.

For the care example, a useful answer separates access to scheduling information from access to private clinical details. A shared schedule approved by the parent may improve participation without overriding privacy.

When you can write, ask for one clear role or agreed information route. Review whether participation improved while privacy and choice were retained. Record unresolved concerns separately instead of treating every remaining limit as evidence of exclusion.
