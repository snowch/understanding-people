Dismissing someone's needs means treating their reported concern as unworthy of consideration. That differs from saying you cannot meet a request, or disagreeing about how to solve the problem. You can recognise a difficulty while leaving responsibility, constraints and available options open for discussion.

This distinction protects both people. Naming a need does not settle the method of meeting it; calling it inconvenient does not make it disappear. This material is general and has not received specialist review or assessed your safety. Our brief example is fictional.

Ask whether the disagreement concerns the existence of a difficulty, its importance, who is responsible or one proposed solution. Those are different questions. If someone cannot provide a particular solution, that does not necessarily mean they deny the need. Equally, sympathetic words can leave a shared duty entirely unaddressed.

Imagine two people sharing a room at home. One needs quiet to work; the other needs space for a call. Naming both requirements allows discussion of timing or another space. Saying the first person's work does not matter changes the issue: their concern is being dismissed rather than the schedule negotiated.

Someone may be overloaded, lack information, see conflicting needs or think a request is unfair. They might also avoid responsibility or exercise control. These possibilities can coexist, and the response alone does not identify which applies. Ask about the practical difficulty instead of supplying an inner explanation such as a complete absence of empathy.

Look at whose needs become the default and whose require repeated justification. Include your own habits of treating your preferred method as the only acceptable solution. A preference, an access requirement, a shared duty and an immediate safety concern need different responses. A respectful no and dismissal are not the same thing.

Richard Ryan and Edward Deci reviewed theory and evidence about autonomy, competence and connection in motivation and well-being. The framework offers questions about choice, capability and relationships; it cannot establish the cause of one refusal. It also does not decide who must meet every request another person makes.

Jason Colquitt and colleagues combined organisational fairness studies, distinguishing outcomes, procedures, interpersonal treatment and information. Most evidence concerns organisations, so it does not determine the correct arrangement in our fictional room. Tal Eyal and colleagues' interpersonal prediction experiments support asking for information, while leaving honesty, completeness and safety uncertain. These are aids to inquiry, not verdicts.

For an ordinary conflict, state the concern you understand, name real constraints and discuss feasible options. Check whether each person can describe the other's difficulty accurately. Make shared duties explicit. Access or rights questions may need appropriate advice rather than being settled by whichever person argues more forcefully.

An accommodation can itself create pressure if it is offered only in exchange for unrelated compliance. Where dismissal occurs with threats, deprivation or control, seek support for the actual pattern. NHS guidance describes forms of abuse and UK support routes, but cannot assess your circumstances. Understanding does not require endless debate before you protect a legitimate limit.

For the fictional room conflict, quiet for a particular period is different from a demand that the other person never use the room. A useful discussion could recognise both requirements and agree a workable time. Try one clarification or seek relevant advice, then review whether a practical decision was made.

The review is not simply whether you got your first request. Ask whether both concerns and constraints were acknowledged, and whether responsibilities became clearer. Recognise the need without assuming that recognition settles every solution.
