An ordinary disagreement, an imposed obligation and a harmful controlling pattern need different responses. Describe what happened, its effects and the options you actually have before deciding what to call the person. Support or protection need not wait for certainty about motive or diagnosis.

When you are pressured to accept something harmful or unwanted, ask what you can safely do next. If threats or essential resources are involved, begin with appropriate support. This chapter provides general educational guidance and has not received external specialist review. It cannot establish which action would be safe in your circumstances.

People can want different things or remember an agreement differently. They can also distort information, use pressure or make threats that narrow another person’s options. A serious incident matters even before you can establish a recurring pattern.

The book uses manipulation to mean influence that makes it harder to choose freely with the information you need. This is a working description of conduct, not a personality diagnosis. Ask what was agreed, what information was withheld or distorted, and what happens after a refusal.

Here is a fictional example. You agree to bring food to a gathering. A relative knowingly tells others that you will organise the whole event, then uses their expectations to pressure you into that extra work. The created obligation matters. You do not have to explain the relative’s entire personality to address it.

Gaslighting refers to psychological abuse that undermines trust in your own memory, perceptions or judgment. One disagreement about a memory does not establish it. That qualification should not be used to minimise harmful conduct or delay support while you search for the perfect term.

Paige Sweet used domestic-violence accounts in a sociological analysis of how power and social inequalities can undermine someone’s credibility. Her work describes processes. It does not provide a validated detector for every disputed recollection.

Consider a fictional contrast. In one situation, a person checks a message, corrects their account and repairs the effect. In another, someone repeatedly dismisses evidence, attacks your ability to remember and restricts your options. Those developments call for different responses; they are not a diagnostic scoring system. Abuse does not require a personality diagnosis.

Where useful and safe, keep what you witnessed separate from what was reported and what remains uncertain. Relevant records may clarify an agreement. But do not assume that your device or account is private, or that collecting information is harmless in a controlling situation.

Confrontation or discovery of records can increase risk. You do not need perfect documentation before asking for help, and you do not need to provoke a reaction to prove a pattern. The support page offers routes to consider rather than a test you must complete.

NHS public guidance recognises nonphysical as well as physical domestic abuse and describes support options. Those services are specific to the United Kingdom; elsewhere, use appropriate local support. Seeking help can begin with the restrictions and practical difficulties already known.

A request asks another person to act. An agreement sets shared terms. A boundary is a limit on what you will accept or participate in, backed by an action you can take. In an ordinary safe exchange, you might clarify that you will bring food but will not organise an event you never agreed to organise.

Shared legal or care duties still matter. If dependence or threats make a limit difficult to act on, that does not mean you consent or lack courage. Practical support may be needed first. A general exercise cannot decide that announcing or enforcing a limit will be safe.

An explanation, an apology, a correction and sustained change provide different information. Roy Lewicki and colleagues studied judgments of apologies after hypothetical trust violations. Those judgments do not prove that a real relationship is repaired or oblige forgiveness. Look for relevant changes; warmth alone does not establish safety or reliability.

Include your own contribution without assigning equal blame. You can correct an inaccurate account while the other person remains responsible for a threat. If your own conduct is threatening or controlling, stop imposing it and seek appropriate help. Your mistakes do not make you responsible for someone else’s abuse.
