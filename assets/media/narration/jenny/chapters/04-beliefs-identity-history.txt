A disagreement can concern a fact, a practical cost, a value, or what a choice seems to say about someone. Before explaining a reaction through identity or personal history, ask what the issue means to the person. Include yourself: wanting to appear fair, competent or right can influence how you hear an answer.

The useful question for this chapter is, what do I want to be true? Use it when admitting a mistake feels uncomfortable or when you find yourself dismissing an answer unusually quickly. Wanting a conclusion does not make it false. It gives you a reason to check how you reached it.

A practical offer can mean support to the person making it and interference to the person receiving it. It might raise concerns about debt, privacy or competence. These are possibilities to ask about, not motives you can read directly from a refusal.

Here is a fictional example. A parent offers to organise an adult child’s appointments. The child welcomes a lift but wants to make the bookings. The child may be protecting decision ownership while still accepting care. Treating the refusal as rejection of the whole relationship would go beyond what happened.

Ask which part of the offer is useful, what the person wants to retain, and what concerns them. Understanding your good intention does not oblige them to accept your proposed arrangement. A narrower offer can be a useful result.

Motivated reasoning means that what you want to be true influences how you judge information. Ziva Kunda reviewed research on how desired conclusions can shape the reasons people search for and find persuasive. This does not show that every strongly held belief is wrong or that disagreement reveals a concealed motive.

Imagine a separate fictional situation. You see yourself as a considerate friend. Someone says that your public offer of help embarrassed them, and you immediately dismiss the complaint. Your wish to remain considerate in your own eyes may affect how seriously you consider their account.

Ask whether you would judge the same information differently if it made you look good. Then identify a relevant fact that could weaken your explanation. Use a comparable standard for welcome and unwelcome information, rather than testing only the claims that threaten your preferred account.

Past experience can shape expectations, but present conduct does not reveal a biography. A refusal of money could involve a previous unwanted condition, a current budget preference or something else. Similar behaviour in relatives does not establish a shared history or a single cause.

People also tell changing stories about how their past, present and hopes fit together. This is called narrative identity. Dan McAdams and Kate McLean reviewed research on these life stories while identifying unanswered causal and cultural questions. A person’s story helps you understand its meaning to them; it does not independently verify every event.

You can ask what accepting an offer would mean while allowing an incomplete or private answer. Someone does not have to disclose a painful history to justify a reasonable limit. Curiosity is useful only when it leaves the other person room to choose what they share.

Acknowledging an effect is different from settling a disputed fact. In a separate fictional example, a friend says your public offer of financial help exposed something private. You can acknowledge the disclosure and ask what privacy they need, while separately clarifying whether repayment was agreed. Empathy does not settle the terms of a loan.

Keep descriptions specific enough to change. Preferring to manage one task yourself need not become never accepting help. Making an unreliable commitment need not become being an unreliable person. You can take responsibility for a particular action without turning it into a permanent account of who you are.

Separate the content of the criticism, your interpretation of the colleague’s motive, and your own stake in success. Ask which part seems unworkable and check it. A useful objection can arrive awkwardly, and an accurate correction need not decide your worth.

This week, notice one conclusion you feel driven to defend. Ask what you want to be true and make one fair check. Afterwards, ask whether the check changed the factual claim, the meaning you attached to it, or neither. The written chapter offers further comparisons and optional notes.
