Understanding people is about forming better explanations and choosing useful responses. You do not need a background in psychology. You need a willingness to separate what happened from the meaning you gave it, and to let new information change that meaning.

The questions apply to you as well as to other people. Why do you keep postponing something you care about? Why does a friend seem distant? Why might someone agree to a plan, then do something different? A confident explanation can arrive before you have enough information to support it.

The book offers a simple sequence. Observe what happened. Consider alternatives. Ask and check. Respond. Then review what happened next. This is a way to organise your thinking, with room to return to an earlier question when you learn something new.

Here is a brief fictional example. A friend has not replied for two days, and you think they are annoyed with you. The unanswered message describes an event. Annoyance is one possible explanation. Keeping those separate lets you consider what to ask without pretending that your disappointment is unimportant.

Use the same care with your own behaviour. If you miss a commitment, ask what made it difficult, what you were choosing between, and what you actually did. Compare that explanation with the one you would give someone else in similar circumstances.

Wanting an outcome, having an opportunity, and carrying out an action are different things. A person may want to help but lack transport. They may have time but prefer a different commitment. Those possibilities call for different responses, so encouragement alone may miss the difficulty.

The chapters bring several perspectives to these questions. Psychology helps us ask about beliefs, feelings, motivation and habits. Sociology and anthropology widen the picture to groups, shared meanings, institutions and power. Decision research helps us ask what the evidence supports and what remains uncertain.

No single perspective supplies a complete explanation of an individual. A useful perspective changes the question you ask. It might help you notice a practical barrier, ask about a competing goal, or recognise that someone cannot safely disagree.

You can follow the twelve chapters in order or begin with a question that matters now. If you are unsure about an interpretation, begin with the chapter on misreading people. If a valued intention repeatedly stalls, turn to motivation. Questions about trust belong with the chapter on truth, lies and uncertainty.

The worksheet helps you work through an ordinary situation. The support page helps when understanding motives is not enough. A need for safety, professional help or a clear boundary should not be delayed while you search for a perfect explanation.

This is a complete first draft. The book uses research to inform its explanations, and distinguishes findings from possibilities and practical suggestions. Its framework is a learning tool, and the edition has not undergone external subject-matter review.

You might have a clear description and only a tentative explanation. That is a useful beginning. Keep the event in mind as you listen, including your own contribution and what you still do not know.

One better question or one workable adjustment is enough to start. Later, return to the event and ask what changed in your understanding and what you will do next. The written pages offer research links, additional examples and optional space for notes.
