Return to the situation you wanted to understand when you began this book. What do you know now? What are you still guessing? And what would you do next? Progress might mean a better question or a clearer limit, even when someone’s reasons remain uncertain.

The aim has been to improve how you explain behaviour and how you respond, including to yourself. Keep the useful distinctions close enough to use them in an ordinary moment. You do not need a complete theory of another person before choosing a reasonable next step.

First, keep what happened separate from why you think it happened. An unanswered message is an event you can describe. Not caring is a possible explanation. Consider alternatives and ask what the evidence actually supports, without inventing a comforting story or assuming the harshest one.

Second, wanting and being able are different. Check practical difficulties, competing goals and what the activity means to each person. A useful offer can leave decisions with its recipient. Include the explanation you want to be true, especially when a correction threatens how you see yourself.

Third, consider the occasion as well as the person. Stress, familiar routines and surroundings may affect what is manageable. Look for patterns and exceptions. A label does not explain every action, and an understandable difficulty can still need a practical change.

Fourth, ask what taking part or disagreeing costs. Group expectations, access to information and unequal power can shape behaviour. Silence need not mean agreement or lack of interest. Your own ease in a setting does not show that everyone has the same opportunity.

Fifth, listen and check what matters. Let the other person correct your understanding. A confident voice, eye contact or a persuasive story does not establish that an account is true. Ask which independent information would actually address the claim, and keep accuracy separate from intention.

Sixth, choose a useful response and review it. Agree what improvement means, respect other people’s choices and check the practical effects. You can set a limit or seek support without establishing a hidden motive. Understanding someone does not require accepting harm.

The short questions are these. What did they say or do? Why do I think they did it? How could I check? Then add your own part, and ask what you can usefully and safely do next. If fear, coercion or retaliation is involved, use suitable support rather than turning the situation into an exercise.

Here is a new fictional example. Your neighbour agreed to water your balcony plants while you were away. You return to dry soil and think they did not bother. What do you know, what else is possible, and what could you ask? Take a moment, and pause the player if you would like longer.

You know what was agreed and that the soil is dry now. That alone does not establish whether, when or how much your neighbour watered. Hot weather or unclear instructions are possibilities to check, not established excuses.

You might ask how the watering went, which pots they watered and when. Include whether your own instructions were clear. Depending on the answer, agree a more specific arrangement or make another plan next time. You do not need a verdict about their character to respond to what the plants need.

Choose one ordinary, safe situation this week. Use a relevant question, take one useful step, and decide when to review what happened. The worksheet or your Chapter Twelve practice notes can help if writing is useful and safe.

A week later, try recalling the questions before looking back. Notice whether you actually used one, rather than simply finding it familiar. Return to whichever chapter would improve your next decision. One thoughtful use is enough; you do not have to keep assessing everyone around you.
