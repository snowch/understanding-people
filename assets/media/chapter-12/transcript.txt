0:00 — What does a usable kitchen mean?
Three housemates agree to keep the kitchen usable. Asha expects washing up immediately. Ben thinks morning is soon enough. Morgan clears the sink but leaves food on the worktop. After a week, each has a theory about the others’ character. They may need a clearer agreement more urgently than a complete explanation of one another.

0:22 — Start with the decision
Asha can replace why are they inconsiderate with how can we keep space clear for breakfast. Pans were in the sink at seven on Monday and Thursday is a concrete observation. You always leave everything to me makes several claims at once. A few relevant examples and everyone’s account may be enough; they do not need to monitor every plate or movement.

0:44 — Choose a question that changes your next step
Different questions bring different information. What does each person need the kitchen for? What did usable mean to them? Are discussions happening only when someone is rushing? Where are cleaning materials kept? Who made the rule and who carries the work? Use a question if the answer could change what you do, rather than collecting more labels.

1:07 — A practical guide, not a diagnosis
These questions bring together the book’s ideas about motivation, meaning, emotions, habits, power and evidence. They have not been tested as one combined method. Include your own role: what you may have misunderstood, what you did, or what you can change. The aim is a useful next step, not a complete psychological profile of your housemates.

1:31 — Make the agreement specific
Asha asks whether the sink and one section of worktop can be clear by seven, and what would make that workable. Ben explains his late shift. Morgan says nobody discussed the worktop. They agree an area for items awaiting washing and a rota for shared cleaning. The arrangement needs to fit their schedules and share the work fairly.

1:53 — Agree what to review
They try it for a week and ask whether breakfast preparation was possible, whether the work was fair, and whether a new problem appeared. Harkin and colleagues combined experiments on progress monitoring and found benefits on average for reaching goals. That supports checking progress, but does not prove this household arrangement will work or justify secret monitoring.

2:15 — A useful result is not proof of a motive
If the kitchen becomes easier to use, the arrangement helped that week. It may still be unclear why dishes were left out before. If nothing changes, check whether everyone understood and could follow the agreement. Repeated refusal of reasonable terms may call for a firmer limit. A later work-schedule change can also make a previously useful plan need revision.
Chapter explanation: https://snowch.github.io/understanding-people/chapters/12-putting-understanding-to-work.html#make-the-review-small-and-informative

2:39 — Know when to stop analysing
More checking takes time and can intrude on privacy or safety. You may know enough for a small reversible change. Further information may not change the decision, or the other person may decline more discussion. Harm or a formal dispute may require other support. These are practical considerations, not validated cut-off points or a reason to ignore important new evidence.
Chapter explanation: https://snowch.github.io/understanding-people/chapters/12-putting-understanding-to-work.html#decide-when-to-stop-analysing

3:04 — One question to take forward
When several explanations remain but you still need to act, ask: What useful step can I take with what I know? Describe the event, choose a question or check, agree a step where appropriate, and review what changed. People can understand one another and still want different outcomes. You do not need to solve every motive before making a considerate, workable decision.
