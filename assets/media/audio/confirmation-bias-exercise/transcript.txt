Confirmation bias: what did you notice?
Fictional example · Synthetic narration

Narrator: Try a small listening experiment. Confirmation bias means looking for information, or reading it in a way, that supports what you already believe. This exercise lets you practise checking both sides. It is not a test of whether you are biased.

Imagine you suspect that your friend Alex only contacts you when they need something. That is a belief to examine, not a fact we have established. You are about to hear a fictional voice message. Listen once. Afterwards, write down the words that support your belief, and the words that make it less certain. Here is Alex.

Alex: Hi. How did your appointment go? You sounded worried on Sunday, and I've been wondering how you got on. Also, could I borrow your drill this weekend? Mine has stopped working. If you need it, no problem. And are you free for a walk next week? I'd like to catch up properly. Tuesday or Thursday would work for me. Let me know about the appointment when you feel like talking.

Narrator: Before the replay, make two short notes. What supports your belief? What challenges it? Use Alex's actual words. If you remember nothing for one side, write that. Leave your first answers in place so you can compare them later. There will be fifteen seconds of quiet. Pause the recording if you need longer.

[Pause: 15 seconds. Pause the player for longer if needed.]

Narrator: Now listen to exactly the same message. Try to notice anything you missed, or explained away, the first time.

[Same message replayed]
Hi. How did your appointment go? You sounded worried on Sunday, and I've been wondering how you got on. Also, could I borrow your drill this weekend? Mine has stopped working. If you need it, no problem. And are you free for a walk next week? I'd like to catch up properly. Tuesday or Thursday would work for me. Let me know about the appointment when you feel like talking.

Narrator: What changed in what you noticed? Add a note without rewriting your first answers. Take another fifteen seconds, or pause for longer.

[Pause: 15 seconds. Pause the player for longer if needed.]

Narrator: The drill request supports the narrower statement: Alex is asking for a favour today. The appointment question and the invitation to walk challenge the word only. They do not prove Alex's sincerity or settle the history of your friendship.

Perhaps you remembered the request but dismissed the interest as politeness. Ask whether you would have accepted those same words as genuine interest from someone you trusted. That difference in how you judge evidence is worth examining. Missing a detail by itself could simply be a lapse of attention or memory.

Would you keep your original belief, narrow it, or change it? What would help you decide? You could compare several recent conversations: contact with and without requests, follow-through on invitations, and how each of you responds when the other needs help. Include exceptions.

The useful habit is not forcing a positive interpretation. It is giving information that challenges your belief a fair hearing. Next time you feel certain about someone's motive, ask: what have I noticed, and what might I have dismissed?
