0:00 — You agreed to bring food
Martin volunteers you to organise a family gathering, although you agreed only to bring food. When you object, he says you remembered it wrong and calls you unreliable to others. You wonder what to call this. Start with the agreement and what he did. You can correct the claim and decide what work you accept without knowing every reason behind his behaviour.

0:22 — Check safety before wording
The conversations in this example assume it is reasonably safe to disagree. If there are threats, fear, monitoring, or control over housing, money or care, start with support and a safer next step. Abuse is not simply a communication problem both people must solve. You do not need to confront someone or establish a diagnosis before seeking help.

0:46 — Describe conduct before a label
In this book, manipulation means influencing someone in a way that makes it harder to choose freely with the information they need. If Martin knowingly tells others you agreed to organise, he may use their expectations to pressure you. That describes a particular action. It does not diagnose his personality or show that every disputed agreement is manipulation.

1:09 — What gaslighting means here
Gaslighting is psychological abuse that undermines someone’s trust in their memory, perceptions or judgment. One disagreement about memory is not enough to establish it. Paige Sweet’s research explains how unequal power and other people’s authority can help make a victim’s account seem untrustworthy. It does not provide a checklist for diagnosing a person. Repeated denial, fear or attempts to discredit you can warrant support without settling the label.
Chapter explanation: https://snowch.github.io/understanding-people/chapters/11-conflict-manipulation-boundaries.html#use-gaslighting-carefully-without-minimising-harm

1:39 — Records only when safe
A message saying you can bring food may help clarify the agreement. If safe and useful, keep that record or a brief note, separating recorded words from memory. But a monitored device or a threat changes the decision. Collecting or storing evidence could increase risk. You do not need perfect records before getting help, and you do not have to make the person admit what happened.
Chapter explanation: https://snowch.github.io/understanding-people/chapters/11-conflict-manipulation-boundaries.html#keep-the-evidence-proportionate-and-safe

2:04 — Request, agreement and boundary
A request asks Martin to check before offering your time. An agreement states that you bring food and someone else organises the venue. A boundary is a limit backed by an action you can take: I will not take on tasks assigned without my agreement. Choose something you can act on, rather than a threat you cannot or do not intend to carry out.
Chapter explanation: https://snowch.github.io/understanding-people/chapters/11-conflict-manipulation-boundaries.html#make-a-boundary-about-what-you-can-do

2:26 — A clear response in a safe exchange
You might say: I agreed to bring food, not organise the gathering. I can still bring food. Please correct the message saying I am organising. If pressure continues, you can repeat the limit without arguing endlessly about your character. Dependence on someone may limit what is safe or possible. Difficulty enforcing a limit does not mean you are weak or consent to harm.
Chapter explanation: https://snowch.github.io/understanding-people/chapters/11-conflict-manipulation-boundaries.html#make-a-boundary-about-what-you-can-do

2:51 — Look for repair in behaviour
An apology can be followed by correcting the message, finding another organiser and asking before offering your time again. Those changes address the problem. If it repeats, fewer commitments or outside advice may be needed. You can correct your own unclear wording without taking responsibility for someone else’s coercion or abuse. Check whether you also accept other people’s no.
Chapter explanation: https://snowch.github.io/understanding-people/chapters/11-conflict-manipulation-boundaries.html#judge-repair-by-what-changes

3:16 — One question to remember
When pressured to accept something harmful or unwanted, ask: What can I safely do next? In an ordinary safe disagreement, describe the conduct and choose a limit you can act on. Where there is fear or control, choose a support step instead. Do not keep notes if discovery could put you at risk. Understanding should help you respond, not keep you in harm’s way.
