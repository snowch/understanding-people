# How to use this book

Read with a situation in mind, including one involving your own behaviour. The aim is to leave with a better question or a more considered action, rather than a label for a person.

## Follow the explanation, then try it

Each chapter starts with **One habit to practise**: a question to remember and a moment when it could help. The text then explains the idea through research and examples. Green panels headed **Try this** or **Put it into practice** give you a small task. Their wording and checklist icon identify them in print and for readers who do not distinguish colours.

You can read straight through without doing every exercise. When a question fits your situation, pause and use it. Avoid turning ordinary interactions into a running assessment of everyone around you.

<section class="reader-action" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="choose-example">

### Try this: choose an example you can revisit {#choose-example}

Pick a recent event from your own life that you feel comfortable thinking about. Write three things: **what you saw or heard**, **why you think it happened**, and **what you still do not know**. After reading a chapter, return to those notes: what would you now ask or do differently?

If someone else is involved, include what you said or did and how that might have affected their response. If you are thinking about yourself, include what was happening around you as well as what you meant to do.

## Check what you can use, then try it during the week

Before reading the key takeaways, try the chapter’s **Can you use it?** questions. Each uses a new fictional situation. Say or write your answer before opening **Compare your answer**. You do not need to copy the wording; look at whether you separated facts from guesses, asked a useful question or recognised a limit. Each explanation links back to the passage if you get stuck.

These are self-checks, not scored tests or proof that you will remember later. Some later chapters bring back an earlier idea in a new situation. Try answering before looking back. You can also return a week later and attempt the end-of-chapter check again without opening the answers.

Choose **one** weekly exercise. Chapter 1 offers separate options for noticing confirmation bias and correspondence bias in your own thinking. You do not need to track everyone around you or complete every exercise.

### Keep notes if they help

Open **My practice notes** beneath an exercise. You can choose a date to return, record what happened and describe what you learned. With JavaScript available, notes save automatically in this browser on this device. The book does not send notes to the author, sync them between devices or send reminders.

Anyone using the same browser may be able to read the notes. Avoid names and sensitive details, and do not keep records if discovery could put you at risk. Private browsing or clearing browser data can remove them. Use **Download these notes** to keep a text copy, or **Delete these notes** to remove that exercise’s saved notes from this browser. Deleting here does not delete downloaded copies.

If saving is blocked, a message will say so; download or copy the notes before leaving. Without JavaScript, the fields do not save. You can use your own notebook instead. Printing the page includes space for handwriting and, when JavaScript is available, the full text of any notes you have entered.

## Know what each element means

Outlined boxes labelled **Definition** explain unfamiliar terms in plain English and give an example. Select the term to find it in the alphabetical [glossary](glossary.md). Each glossary entry links back to its explanation in a chapter.

Research claims have nearby source links and a description of the relevant limits. Possible explanations are presented as possibilities. Suggested conversations and action panels are practical applications unless a passage explicitly describes a tested intervention. See the [evidence standards](evidence.md) for more detail.

All named scenarios are fictional teaching examples. They show how an explanation might develop; they are not case-study evidence that an approach succeeds.

We brought together the five-step method and worksheet from the ideas in the book. Researchers have not tested them as a complete method. They are learning tools, not a way to diagnose or treat a condition, test personality or establish guilt. If someone is struggling with daily life or may be unsafe, use the [support page](support.md) to consider what help is needed.

## Use uncertainty without getting stuck

You can choose a reasonable next step before you fully understand why someone acted as they did. A repeated missed commitment may justify changing an arrangement. You can acknowledge that an explanation is uncertain while being clear about the effect of the behaviour.

Likewise, an alternative explanation does not have to be a reassuring one. Consider explanations that could fit what happened, and check them fairly, including facts you would rather not accept. You do not have to keep looking for an innocent explanation of behaviour that harms you.

## Read the current edition as a draft

All [twelve chapters](contents.md) are available as a complete first draft. Each ends with a **Can you use it?** check, **Key takeaways**, and practice to try during the week. This edition has not undergone external subject-matter review, and its source list is selective rather than a systematic review of all relevant research.

The most useful feedback identifies a passage: what claim seems unsupported, what explanation needs clarification, or what question the example leaves unanswered. Sources and revision history are available through the page-source links.
