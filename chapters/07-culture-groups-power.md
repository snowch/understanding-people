# Culture, groups and power

Behaviour happens within arrangements that make some actions easier, expected or costly. **Before judging participation, examine meaning, access and the consequences of disagreement.** These questions apply to families and organisations as well as wider cultures—and to your own position in them.

<section class="remember-cue" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="remember-this">

## One habit to practise {#remember-this}

When someone stays quiet or does not join in, ask: **“What makes taking part easy or risky here?”**

</section>

## Culture supplies meanings, not a script for each person {#culture-supplies-meanings-not-a-script-for-each-person}

Culture includes learned meanings and practices: how an invitation is understood, what counts as respectful disagreement and who is expected to decide. People also differ within groups and belong to several overlapping communities.

Vivian Vignoles and colleagues’ 2016 multinational surveys found variation in ways of being independent and connected beyond a simple East–West division. The patterns do not identify an individual’s values. [Research record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27359126/).

**Brief fictional example:** A family invitation means “come if you can” to one person and “attendance is expected” to another. Clarifying the expectation is more useful than assigning either person a national or family personality.

## Distinguish usual behaviour from expected behaviour {#distinguish-what-people-do-from-what-they-are-expected-to-do}

<section class="definition" markdown="1" aria-label="Definitions: Descriptive norm, Prescriptive norm">
<p class="definition-label">Definitions</p>
<dl>
<dt id="definition-descriptive-norm"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#descriptive-norm">Descriptive norm</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>What people in a group commonly do, or believe others commonly do.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> At the residents’ meeting, newcomers usually stay quiet. Staying quiet is the usual behaviour, whether or not anyone thinks it should be.</p></dd>
<dt id="definition-prescriptive-norm"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#prescriptive-norm">Prescriptive norm</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>What people in a group believe others expect or approve of.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> A newcomer believes that members will disapprove if they interrupt the chair. This is about what the group expects, rather than how often people actually interrupt.</p></dd>
</dl>
</section>

Compare the stated rule with what actually happens. “Everyone may ask questions” offers limited reassurance if newcomers are mocked when they do. Ask whose contribution receives a different response, including when you are the person setting the tone.

## Access depends on more than interest {#institutions-can-reward-familiar-ways-of-participating}

Knowing the right contact, vocabulary or procedure can make participation easier. Time, transport, language, money and care responsibilities matter too. An invitation alone does not create an accessible opportunity.

For her 2002 study, Annette Lareau observed and interviewed US families. She examined how practices linked to social class affected encounters with schools and other institutions. It describes possible processes of advantage in those settings, not a universal parenting classification. [Read the study](https://doi.org/10.1177/000312240206700507).

<span id="anthropology-widens-what-seems-like-ordinary-behaviour"></span>

Anthropology also asks what an activity means locally. In 2005, Joseph Henrich and colleagues studied decisions in economic games, such as dividing resources, across 15 small-scale societies. They examined those decisions alongside local social life. It challenges easy generalisation; it does not rank cultures or show that one institutional change will cause cooperation. [Research record](https://henrich.fas.harvard.edu/publications/economic-man-cross-cultural-perspective-behavioral-experiments-15-small-scale).

## Map power through practical choices {#map-power-through-practical-questions}

Ask who sets the options, controls resources, can refuse and can obtain a review. Confidence and formal titles do not fully answer those questions.

**A separate brief example:** A worker agrees to extra hours in front of the manager who allocates shifts. The yes may be sincere, constrained or both. Its meaning depends partly on what refusal would cost.

Real role responsibilities and necessary rules can coexist with unequal power. Examine whether restrictions are relevant, proportionate and open to appropriate challenge. Do not pressure someone to speak publicly when their housing, work or safety could be affected.

## Change a process, then check who can use it {#improve-one-arrangement-then-check-whose-voice-it-reaches}

Where you have influence, clarify rights, provide information early, offer suitable contribution routes and explain how decisions are made. These are editorial options, not interventions proved by the cited studies.

Check actual access and outcomes rather than counting how many invitations were sent. If one person uses a new route successfully, that does not establish that every barrier has disappeared. Ask whose needs or work remain invisible—and whether your own comfort made the arrangement seem equally easy for everyone.

<section class="key-takeaways" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="key-takeaways">

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Culture gives context without determining individual beliefs.
- Usual behaviour, expected behaviour and stated rules can differ.
- Check practical access, power and the real costs of disagreement.

</section>

## Put it into practice {#put-it-into-practice}

### Recall {#can-you-use-it}

If someone stays quiet in a group, what should you check before concluding that they lack interest?

<details class="answer-check" markdown="1"><summary>Show the useful distinction</summary>

Meaning, access, familiarity with the process and the consequences of speaking. Ask **“What makes taking part easy or risky here?”**

</details>

### Try it {#try-it}

**A new fictional situation:** A committee emails an online form to everyone and says its consultation is inclusive. Several carers do not submit responses.

What is missing from the claim, and what change might supply useful information? Say or write your answer before opening the example.

<details class="answer-check" markdown="1"><summary>Show an example answer</summary>

Delivery of a form does not establish usable access. Ask about timing, format and available time; offer a feasible alternative where appropriate. Review who can actually contribute and whether input influences decisions, rather than assuming a lack of interest.

</details>

</section>

### Use it this week {#map-participation}

This week, examine one ordinary group arrangement. Identify an unwritten expectation or access barrier, including your own position. Ask or make one appropriate change, then review who could use it and who still could not. Do not test whether retaliation will occur.

#### Another comparison

**A separate fictional situation:** A meeting accepts written questions but only certain members can vote. Has making questions accessible made decision power equal?

<details class="answer-check" markdown="1"><summary>Show what to consider</summary>

No. Better access to questions can help while voting rights remain unequal. Explain both clearly so people know what participation can and cannot influence.
