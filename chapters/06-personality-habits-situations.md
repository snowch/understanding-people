# Personality, habits and situations

“I am just an introvert,” says Alex, declining a crowded birthday party. The next afternoon, Alex spends hours talking at a small repair club. A friend sees a contradiction: surely an introvert would dislike both.

The contradiction comes partly from treating a label as a rule. Size, familiarity, noise, purpose and choice differ between the two settings. Alex might also have a broad preference for quieter company. A useful account can include a tendency and the conditions under which it changes.

## Use traits as dimensions, not compartments

<p class="definition-label">Definitions</p>
<dl>
<dt id="definition-personality-trait"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#personality-trait">Personality trait</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>A tendency in how someone thinks, feels or behaves across occasions. It does not describe everything they do.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> Alex often prefers quieter gatherings but sometimes enjoys a large party.</p></dd>
<dt id="definition-big-five"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#big-five">Big Five</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>A way of describing personality using five broad dimensions, with people varying along each one. These are dimensions, not five types of person.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> Alex can be less outgoing than a friend while being similarly organised. One dimension does not decide the others.</p></dd>
</dl>
</section>

In one measure, the BFI-2, the five dimensions are extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, negative emotionality and open-mindedness, with narrower facets within them. Soto and John's development and validation studies support that particular measurement approach; they do not make a casual impression equivalent to a measured score. [Soto and John, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27055049/).

Think of a dimension as “more or less of a tendency across relevant observations”, rather than membership of a sealed category. A broad score also compresses distinctions: enjoying company, being assertive and feeling energetic need not show up identically in one person's life.

The practical question is what a description helps you anticipate. “Alex prefers small groups with a shared activity” offers more guidance for an invitation than “Alex is an introvert.” It also leaves Alex free to accept a large event without having to defend a personality change.

This chapter therefore uses dimensions and concrete patterns rather than a type quiz. A familiar type label may start a conversation, but it should not decide someone's competence, honesty or eligibility for an opportunity.

## Look for both consistency and variation

Fleeson's three experience-sampling studies recorded people's trait-relevant states repeatedly over two to three weeks. They found substantial variation within people alongside stable differences in their average patterns. A tendency and varied behaviour can coexist. The studies' timescale and self-reported states do not establish exactly how Alex behaves over years or explain every change. [Fleeson, 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11414368/).

To examine a pattern fairly, include occasions when the expected behaviour did not occur. If you remember only the parties Alex declined, the repair club disappears from the account. If you only see a colleague in competitive meetings, you have a narrow window on their behaviour.

| Initial label | More useful description | What remains to learn? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Unsociable” | Often declines large gatherings; enjoys familiar small groups | Which features make an invitation appealing? |
| “Disorganised” | Misses appointments changed at short notice | Are changes reaching the same calendar? |
| “Controlling” | Insists on checking shared spending | What authority was agreed, and can others disagree freely? |

A more precise description does not automatically make conduct acceptable. The last example could involve an ordinary budgeting agreement or harmful control. Context helps distinguish them; a trait label alone does not.

<section class="reader-action" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="replace-label">

### Try this: replace a label with a conditional pattern {#replace-label}

Take a label you use about yourself. Write: **“In these situations, I often…”** Then describe one exception and what differed.

Use the pattern to identify a workable adjustment. Do not turn a short record into a personality test or quietly score someone else.

</section>

## Ask whether repetition is being mistaken for intention

Alex also wants to read in the evening, yet reaches for the phone whenever sitting on the sofa. This may involve a habit rather than a fresh decision that reading is unimportant.

<section class="definition" markdown="1" aria-label="Definition: Habit">
<p class="definition-label">Definition</p>
<dl>
<dt id="definition-habit"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#habit">Habit</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>A learned tendency to respond fairly automatically when a familiar situation occurs.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> Alex sits on the sofa and reaches for the phone before deciding how to spend the evening.</p></dd>
</dl>
</section>

Wood and Rünger’s review explains how habits develop through repetition and interact with deliberate goals. Repeating an action does not, on its own, show that the person currently endorses its outcome. [Wood and Rünger, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26361052/).

For our example, inspect the sequence: arrive home, sit down, see the phone, open an app. Then ask what happens on evenings when Alex reads. Perhaps the book is already open on a table, or the phone is charging elsewhere. These observations suggest a practical change to try; they do not prove the mechanism.

A habit explanation also has limits. If Alex is waiting for an important message, checking the phone may be a purposeful response. If reading is difficult because the lighting is poor, removing the phone will not solve that problem. Ask what the action is doing in this setting before prescribing a cue change.

## Change the arrangement, then observe

Alex chooses to leave the phone on a nearby shelf and put a book beside the sofa for three evenings. Essential calls remain audible. This is an agreed, modest experiment in their own routine, not a test imposed by a friend.

The outcome might be more reading. It might instead be discovering that Alex is too tired to read at that time. Either result supplies information for another decision: keep the arrangement, choose a different time or reconsider the goal.

Avoid promising a fixed number of days after which a routine will become automatic. The useful target here is a feasible next repetition and a record of what made it easier or harder. A missed evening is an observation to examine, not proof that Alex lacks the right personality.

## Make room for responsibility without making it a character verdict

Suppose a housemate repeatedly leaves the front door unlocked. “I am forgetful” may describe a tendency; it does not settle what everyone else should tolerate. The household still needs a reliable arrangement, perhaps a visible check or suitable self-locking hardware, with practical and safety implications considered together.

Conversely, “If you cared, you would remember” treats a failure as direct access to a motive. Ask whether the proposed solution works, who bears its burden and what happens if failures continue. You can require a dependable outcome without diagnosing the person's character.

The same applies to your own self-description. A trait can inform how you organise life; it need not become a ceiling on what you attempt. Choose a setting and support that make the desired behaviour more feasible, then judge the experience on its own terms.

<section class="key-takeaways" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="key-takeaways">

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Personality dimensions describe tendencies; a label does not dictate every action.
- Look for patterns across occasions and for the conditions behind exceptions.
- Repeated behaviour can involve contextual habits as well as current goals.
- Replace your own fixed self-labels with descriptions you can examine and act on.
- Adjusting a routine and expecting responsible conduct can go together.

</section>

<section class="reader-action" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="test-arrangement">

### Put it into practice: try a small change to a routine {#test-arrangement}

Choose one action of your own that you want to make easier. Identify **the usual setting**, **what happens just before it**, and **one feasible change to the arrangement**.

Try the change on a few agreed occasions and note what happened, including exceptions. Keep it only if it helps the goal without creating an unacceptable cost elsewhere.

</section>
