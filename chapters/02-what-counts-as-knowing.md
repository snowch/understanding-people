A neighbourhood organiser notices that people who reply quickly in the group chat are more likely to turn up for events. She proposes a rule: anyone who has not replied within a day loses their place. “Fast replies show commitment,” she says.

The pattern might be real, but it does not tell her why people attend. People with predictable working hours might find both replying and attending easier. Regular participants might check the chat because they already feel involved. Requiring faster replies could exclude people who cannot check their phones at work.

Before using a pattern to judge someone, ask what it actually tells you.

<section class="remember-cue" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="remember-this">

## One habit to practise {#remember-this}

When a pattern makes you want to change a rule, ask: **“What else could explain this pattern?”**

</section>

## Are you asking what happened, what will happen, or why? {#separate-description-prediction-and-explanation}

These questions look similar but ask for different things:

| Question | What would help answer it? |
| --- | --- |
| What happened? | A clear record of invitations, replies and attendance, including missing information. |
| What is likely next time? | A pattern that also appears on later occasions, including how often its predictions are wrong. |
| Why did it happen? | Information that helps you choose between possible reasons, such as a work schedule affecting both replying and attending. |
| What should we do? | What each option might lead to, what is possible and what the people involved consider fair. |

A record showing that prompt responders attended more often does not tell the organiser what would happen if she made everyone respond faster. The rule might change attendance, change who gets a place, or simply exclude people who work without access to their phones.

In a 2018 paper, Julia Rohrer explains how researchers can investigate causes when they cannot run an experiment. Two things can happen together because a third thing affects both. Statistical adjustments can help, but they depend on assumptions about those connections. They do not automatically prove a cause. [Read Rohrer’s paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/2515245917745629).

In the organiser’s example, predictable working hours might make it easier both to reply and to attend. Replying quickly would then be a clue about attendance, without making attendance happen. The organiser needs to check that possibility before making a rule.

## Who or what is missing from the record? {#ask-what-is-missing-from-the-record}

The organiser sees messages from people who joined the chat. She does not see residents who never received an invitation, could not use the platform or left after an unwelcoming first visit. A conclusion about “the neighbourhood” would therefore go beyond her observations.

The records may be incomplete too. An absent tick beside a name might mean that someone did not attend, did not sign in or was never added to the record. Check what the tick means before judging the person.

Now consider a separate example: a customer has not bought from you. Do you know that they rejected the offer, or only that your system has no completed purchase? They might be waiting for approval, comparing options or having trouble paying. Asking what is holding up the purchase could help more than calling them indecisive.

## How often does the warning point to the wrong person? {#count-the-ordinary-cases-as-well-as-the-striking-ones}

Here is a separate, invented example to show why the starting numbers matter. Imagine a screening system that warns when someone may have deliberately broken a rule. Of 1,000 people, 10 really did break it. The warning correctly flags 8 of those 10. It also wrongly flags 99 of the other 990 people.

| In this invented example | Flagged | Not flagged |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deliberately broke the rule: 10 people | 8 | 2 |
| Did not deliberately break it: 990 people | 99 | 891 |

<section class="definition" markdown="1" aria-label="Definition: Base rate">
<p class="definition-label">Definition</p>
<dl>
<dt id="definition-base-rate"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#base-rate">Base rate</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>How common something is in the relevant group before you consider a particular clue or test result.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> In Chapter 2’s invented group, 10 of 1,000 people deliberately broke the rule. The base rate is 1%.</p></dd>
</dl>
</section>

There are 107 flagged people, only 8 of whom deliberately broke the rule: about 7.5%. Detecting 80% of actual cases does **not** mean that 80% of flagged people are cases. The base rate and false alarms both matter. These numbers illustrate arithmetic; they describe no real screening tool or population.

In experiments reported in 1995, Gigerenzer and Hoffrage tested how people solved probability problems. Participants often did better when information was presented as counts. This is why “8 out of 107 people” may be easier to follow than several percentages. Their results do not tell us how common dishonesty is in your own situation. [Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.102.4.684).

If you do not know how common something is, say so. Do not turn a guess into a precise percentage about a person. And do not dismiss specific evidence of harm just because that kind of harm is uncommon in a wider population.

## Change your mind when you learn more {#let-confidence-move-without-swinging-between-certainties}

Now return to the neighbourhood organiser. She learns that several slow responders work shifts and attend when given longer to book. She now has less reason to think slow replies mean a lack of commitment. Other slow responders may still have different reasons.

You can change your mind a little without adopting a new certainty. The organiser could say, “I no longer think reply speed is a good way to judge commitment.” In the same situation, a resident might say, “I know my place was cancelled. I do not know whether anyone meant to exclude me.”

Ask what would make you reconsider your explanation. If every possible answer seems to prove you right, you may be overlooking information that challenges your view. [Chapter 1](01-before-you-explain.md) explains confirmation bias with Rowan’s example.

## You still have to decide what is fair {#evidence-informs-a-decision-it-does-not-choose-your-values}

The organiser may still need a deadline to arrange enough staff. She can explain that need and allow a reasonable time to reply. Missing a deadline does not, by itself, tell her how much someone cares.

Two people can agree about the facts and still disagree about what is fair. One wants every place filled. Another wants people with unpredictable schedules to have a chance to attend. More information can show the costs of each choice, but the group still has to choose.

Use the amount of checking that fits the decision. Trying a different booking reminder is reversible and inexpensive. Publicly accusing someone of dishonesty carries much greater consequences. Where safety is at stake, seeking protection need not wait for certainty about motive; see [Chapter 11](11-conflict-manipulation-boundaries.md).

<section class="key-takeaways" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="key-takeaways">

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Knowing what happened does not always tell you why it happened or what to do next.
- Check what a record actually shows and what it leaves out.
- A warning can be wrong. Count the false alarms as well as the cases it finds.
- Ask what information would make you reconsider your own explanation.
- Check more carefully when being wrong could cause serious harm; the facts still leave choices about fairness.

</section>

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="put-it-into-practice">

## Put it into practice {#put-it-into-practice}

<section class="practice-check" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="can-you-use-it">

### Recall {#can-you-use-it}

Without looking back, what should you ask when you notice that two things happen together?

<details class="answer-check" markdown="1">
<summary>Show the question to remember</summary>

**“What else could explain this pattern?”** Your wording can differ; keep the same idea.

</details>

### Try it {#try-it}

**A new fictional situation:** A café owner notices that customers who use a loyalty card spend more. She says, “Giving everyone a card will make everyone spend more.”

What does the owner know? What else could explain the pattern, and what could she check? Say or write your answer before opening the example.

<details class="answer-check" markdown="1">
<summary>Show an example answer</summary>

She has observed a link, not established that the card causes spending. Regular customers may be more likely to get a card. Checking their spending before they got one would help assess that possibility without settling every possible cause.

</details>

</section>

### Use it this week {#make-claim-checkable}

Notice one claim that something causes a change. Ask what else could explain the pattern, then check one relevant fact. Afterwards, note whether it changed your view. Choose an ordinary decision, not an experiment imposed on someone else.

#### Another question

**Here is a separate invented example. A warning flags 20 bookings: 2 really contain an error and 18 do not. If a booking is flagged, is it probably wrong?**

<details class="answer-check" markdown="1">
<summary>Show an example answer</summary>

No. In these invented numbers, only 2 of the 20 flagged bookings contain an error: 10%. Count false alarms as well as correct warnings. This says nothing about a real booking system. [Revisit the explanation](#count-the-ordinary-cases-as-well-as-the-striking-ones).

</details>

<section class="deeper-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="audit-evidence">

#### Check a fact you are relying on {#audit-evidence}

Choose a statement you believe about someone in your own life. Write **how you know it**, **what you saw or heard**, and **what you still do not know**.

Be specific about who and when. Instead of “Everyone ignores my messages”, try “Three people did not reply to my invitation last week.” Does the more precise description change what you think?
