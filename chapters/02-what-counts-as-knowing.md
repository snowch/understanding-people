# What counts as knowing?

A neighbourhood organiser notices that people who reply quickly in the group chat are more likely to turn up for events. She proposes a rule: anyone who has not replied within a day loses their place. “Fast replies show commitment,” she says.

Her observation might be accurate. The explanation and the proposed rule still need examining. People with predictable hours might find both replying and attending easier. Frequent participants might check the chat because they already feel involved. A quick reply could be a useful forecast without being the cause of attendance—or a fair basis for allocating places.

Understanding people requires more than a plausible story. It requires knowing what your evidence can carry.

## Separate description, prediction and explanation

These questions look similar but ask for different things:

| Question | What would help answer it? |
| --- | --- |
| What happened? | A clear record of invitations, replies and attendance, including missing information. |
| What is likely next time? | A pattern that holds in relevant new cases, with an account of its errors. |
| Why did it happen? | Evidence that distinguishes competing causes, including circumstances affecting both events. |
| What should we do? | Likely consequences, practical constraints and a judgment about what is fair. |

A record showing that prompt responders attended more often does not tell the organiser what would happen if she made everyone respond faster. The rule might change attendance, change who gets a place, or simply exclude people who work without access to their phones.

Rohrer's methodological account explains why causal conclusions from observational data depend on assumptions about how variables relate. A common cause can generate an association, and adding statistical controls does not automatically remove bias. The practical lesson is to make your causal assumptions visible, rather than treating “we accounted for several factors” as a guarantee. [Rohrer, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/2515245917745629).

For this example, draw three possible arrows in words: predictable hours may affect replies; predictable hours may affect attendance; feeling involved may affect both. These are hypotheses for inquiry, not a completed causal model.

## Ask what is missing from the record

The organiser sees messages from people who joined the chat. She does not see residents who never received an invitation, could not use the platform or left after an unwelcoming first visit. A conclusion about “the neighbourhood” would therefore go beyond her observations.

Missingness can matter within the chat too. An absent tick beside a name might mean no attendance, no sign-in or a record that was never updated. Before explaining a person, check what the measure actually represents.

The same discipline applies to a customer who has not purchased. Do you know that they rejected the offer, or only that your system has no completed transaction? They might be awaiting approval, comparing alternatives or unable to complete payment. Asking which stage they reached could be more informative than classifying them as indecisive.

Write a claim you are relying on. Under it, record **where the information came from**, **what it directly establishes**, and **who or what is missing**.

Replace “Everyone does this” with the actual scope: “Three people in this group did this on these occasions.” Does your explanation still fit that narrower statement?

## Count the ordinary cases as well as the striking ones

Suppose a fictional screening rule flags behaviour thought to indicate deliberate rule-breaking. In a group of 1,000 people, assume 10 have deliberately broken the rule. The screen flags 8 of those 10, but also flags 99 of the other 990.

| In this invented example | Flagged | Not flagged |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deliberately broke the rule: 10 people | 8 | 2 |
| Did not deliberately break it: 990 people | 99 | 891 |

There are 107 flagged people, only 8 of whom deliberately broke the rule: about 7.5%. Detecting 80% of actual cases does **not** mean that 80% of flagged people are cases. The starting frequency—often called the **base rate**—and false alarms both matter. These numbers illustrate arithmetic; they describe no real screening tool or population.

Gigerenzer and Hoffrage's experiments found that presenting information in frequency formats could improve performance on Bayesian reasoning problems. That supports trying counts such as “8 out of 107” when probabilities become confusing. It does not supply a base rate for suspicious behaviour in your own life. [Gigerenzer and Hoffrage, 1995](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.102.4.684).

When the relevant frequencies are unknown, acknowledge that. Do not turn a guess about how common dishonesty is into a precise-looking probability about a person. Nor does a low population frequency require you to dismiss specific evidence of harm.

## Let confidence move without swinging between certainties

Return to the organiser. She discovers that several slow responders work shifts, and that they attend when given a longer booking window. This weakens “slow replies mean low commitment”. It does not establish that every slow reply has the same cause.

Useful updating can be modest: “I now have less reason to use reply speed as a character judgment.” You can also separate confidence in an event from confidence in its explanation: “I am sure the place was cancelled; I am unsure whether the cancellation was intended to exclude me.”

Before collecting more information, name something that would change your view. If every answer can be absorbed into your existing explanation, the inquiry has stopped doing useful work. [Chapter 1](01-before-you-explain.md) discusses this confirmation problem and the research behind considering alternatives.

## Evidence informs a decision; it does not choose your values

The organiser may still need a deadline because staffing depends on numbers. She can explain that practical need, offer a reasonable response window and distinguish a missed booking from a verdict about commitment.

Two people may accept the same evidence yet disagree about an acceptable trade-off. One prioritises filling every place; another prioritises access for residents with unpredictable schedules. More data may clarify the costs, but the group must still decide how to share them.

Use the amount of checking that fits the decision. Trying a different booking reminder is reversible and inexpensive. Publicly accusing someone of dishonesty carries much greater consequences. Where safety is at stake, seeking protection need not wait for certainty about motive; see [Chapter 11](11-conflict-manipulation-boundaries.md).

<section class="key-takeaways" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="key-takeaways">

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Description, prediction, causal explanation and a decision require different kinds of reasoning.
- Check what a record measures and which people or events it leaves out.
- A signal's usefulness depends on ordinary cases and false alarms, as well as successful detections.
- Identify evidence that could weaken your own preferred account.
- Match the depth of checking to the consequences, while recognising that values still shape the decision.

</section>

Choose one proportionate source of new information. Decide in advance how it could change your next step, rather than collecting evidence indefinitely.
