# Before you explain someone

*Draft chapter · Fictional examples; the practical framework is an editorial synthesis.*

On Tuesday, Rowan tells you that your proposal could solve a recurring problem. In Thursday's team meeting, Rowan says the proposal is not ready. You leave thinking: “Rowan undermined me to look good.”

That explanation may be possible. But the same sequence could also follow a new budget constraint, a concern Rowan did not know how to express privately, a different understanding of “could work”, or pressure from someone else. Your first task is to separate the event from the explanation.

## Start with what happened

“Rowan praised the proposal privately and questioned its readiness in the meeting” describes a sequence you can discuss. “Rowan is two-faced” adds a judgment about character and motive.

This distinction does not require you to suppress how the exchange affected you. You can be embarrassed or frustrated while remaining uncertain about why it happened.

| Layer | In this example |
| --- | --- |
| Observation | Rowan described the proposal as promising, then said it was not ready. |
| Impact | I felt exposed and left unclear about whether I had support. |
| Interpretation | Rowan wanted to gain status at my expense. |
| Missing information | What changed? What did “promising” mean? Which part was not ready? |

The observation is also fallible: you may remember the wording imperfectly. Use an existing message or document when it matters, and distinguish exact wording from your recollection.

## Keep several explanations alive

Choose a few plausible alternatives, rather than an unlimited list. Include the person's goals, the situation and the possibility that your own understanding was incomplete.

For Rowan, you might consider a substantive objection, a change in external constraints and a concern about social standing. Then ask what each would lead you to expect. A substantive objection should be discussable in concrete terms. A new constraint may have a traceable source. A recurring pattern of private encouragement and public distancing would make the relationship itself more relevant.

None of these observations alone proves a motive. Their value is that they suggest a next question and make it possible for your first interpretation to change.

The same reasoning travels. If a volunteer misses two sessions, ask about transport, scheduling and expectations as well as commitment. If a friend declines an invitation, the refusal does not tell you whether the barrier was money, energy, preference or your relationship. These are possibilities to investigate, not hidden needs to assign.

## Add the social setting

Consider what someone can say safely, who makes decisions, what resources are available and which norms govern the interaction. A private conversation and a public meeting may create different pressures.

Culture also affects the questions worth asking. Research on the narrow cultural coverage of behavioural science cautions against treating findings from familiar populations as universal. It does not tell you what Rowan thinks; it gives you a reason to ask how disagreement normally works in this team. [Henrich, Heine and Norenzayan, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20550733/).

## Ask something that could change your mind

An opening might be:

> “On Tuesday I understood you to support the direction. In the meeting you said it wasn't ready. What concerns should I understand?”

This is a suggested conversation, not a tested script. It names the discrepancy and leaves room for an explanation. Listen for specific information, then check your understanding: “So you support the aim, but want the staffing cost resolved before we present it again?”

A response is evidence about what Rowan says, not guaranteed access to Rowan's private motives. You can compare it with relevant documents and later behaviour without trying to turn the conversation into an interrogation.

## Choose an action that fits what you know

If the concern is concrete, agree on the next revision. If expectations differed, clarify what support means before the next meeting. If the pattern repeats, you might stop representing private encouragement as a public commitment and ask for decisions to be recorded.

You can also address the impact directly: “If you see a major issue before a meeting, please raise it with me beforehand.” You do not have to establish malicious intent to make that request.

Understanding and protection can proceed together. Where direct discussion would expose you to retaliation or other harm, seeking support or limiting contact may be more useful than further questioning.

## Review the explanation

After the next interaction, record what happened and what it changes. Did a clear objection emerge? Did the agreed behaviour occur? Does your interpretation now explain more of the evidence, or have you been treating every response as confirmation?
