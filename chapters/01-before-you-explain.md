# Before you explain someone

On Tuesday, Rowan tells you that your proposal could solve a recurring problem. In Thursday's team meeting, Rowan says it is not ready. You leave thinking: “Rowan undermined me to look good.”

That explanation may be possible. But the same sequence could follow a new budget constraint, a concern Rowan did not express privately, or a different understanding of “could work”. Before deciding how to respond, separate the event from the explanation.

## What happened, and what have you added?

“Rowan praised the proposal privately and questioned its readiness in the meeting” describes a sequence you can discuss. “Rowan is two-faced” adds a judgment about character and motive.

Psychologists use *attribution* to describe explanations of behaviour. Gilbert and Malone's review examines **correspondence bias**: inferring an enduring disposition from behaviour that the situation could explain. The useful warning is that observing an action does not isolate its cause. It does not follow that character never matters, or that every unfavourable judgment is mistaken. [Gilbert and Malone, 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7870861/).

Keep four layers separate:

| Layer | In this example |
| --- | --- |
| Observation | Rowan described the proposal as promising, then said it was not ready. |
| Impact | I felt exposed and left unsure whether I had support. |
| Interpretation | Rowan wanted to gain status at my expense. |
| Missing information | What changed? What did “promising” mean? Which part was not ready? |

Your observation is fallible too. You may remember the wording imperfectly or have heard enthusiasm where Rowan intended polite interest. A relevant message can help distinguish exact wording from recollection.

<section class="reader-action" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="separate-layers">

### Try this: separate the layers {#separate-layers}

Take a recent exchange and write **what happened**, **its effect on you**, and **your explanation** on separate lines. Then write one question whose answer could change that explanation.

Keep the impact in the account. Being uncertain about a motive does not require you to pretend an interaction did not hurt.

</section>

## Notice how an explanation starts defending itself

Once “Rowan wants to embarrass me” becomes your working account, later events can seem to fit automatically. A quick reply looks calculated; a slow reply looks dismissive. If opposite behaviours both confirm the same explanation, ask what could possibly count against it.

Nickerson's review describes **confirmation bias** as seeking or interpreting information in ways that favour existing beliefs or a hypothesis. That includes how evidence is read, not only which facts are collected. Our Rowan example illustrates this risk; the review does not establish that it explains any particular disagreement. [Nickerson, 1998](https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.2.2.175).

“Be objective” is a good intention but gives you little to do. Try constructing a plausible alternative and asking what would distinguish it from your first account.

In two experiments on social judgments, Lord, Lepper and Preston found that instructions to consider the opposite reduced the studied biases more than instructions to be fair and unbiased. These were bounded experimental tasks, not evidence that a brief exercise reliably resolves real relationships or removes bias permanently. They provide a reason to make the alternative explicit. [Lord, Lepper and Preston, 1984](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6527215/).

## Make the alternatives useful

For Rowan, consider a substantive objection, a changed constraint and a concern about public standing. Each suggests different information to seek.

| Possible explanation | Useful information | What would leave it unresolved? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rowan sees a problem with the proposal | A concrete objection and the conditions under which it would be addressed | A vague assurance that “it just needs work” |
| Something changed after Tuesday | What changed, when, and how it affects the decision | A new explanation that does not fit the timing |
| Rowan distances themselves in public | A pattern across comparable interactions, including counterexamples | A single awkward meeting |

These are prompts for inquiry, not tests with guaranteed interpretations. An explanation can be partly right, and several causes can operate together. A real objection could coexist with a wish to protect one's reputation.

Culture and group norms also shape which questions are worth asking. Henrich and colleagues' review challenges broad generalisations from culturally narrow research samples. For this example, the practical question is local: how does this team normally express disagreement? Do not substitute an assumption about Rowan's nationality for that inquiry. [Henrich, Heine and Norenzayan, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20550733/).

## Put yourself inside the explanation

You might have presented Tuesday's conversation as a firm endorsement. You might also be interpreting a public objection as disrespect because being seen as competent matters to you. Those are possibilities to examine, not conclusions that you caused the problem.

A familiar slogan says we explain our own actions through circumstances and other people's through character. It is too broad. Malle's meta-analysis of 173 studies found no general actor–observer asymmetry across the evidence, with patterns depending on conditions such as whether the event was positive or negative. Self-scrutiny remains useful without treating that slogan as a universal law. [Malle, 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17073526/).

<section class="reader-action" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="check-own-account">

### Try this: check your own account {#check-own-account}

Recall a time when **you** encouraged an idea but later raised an objection. What happened between those moments? Which details would an observer have missed?

Now return to Rowan. Ask whether you have considered comparable possibilities, while keeping any evidence of a repeated harmful pattern in view. The exercise opens questions; it does not establish innocence or require self-blame.

</section>

## Ask something that could change your mind

An opening might be:

> “On Tuesday I understood you to support the direction. In the meeting you said it wasn't ready. What concerns should I understand?”

Listen for specific information, then check your interpretation: “So you support the aim, but want the staffing cost resolved before we present it again?”

Rowan might explain that a finance discussion happened on Wednesday. That suggests checking the staffing issue. Or Rowan might say Tuesday's conversation was only encouragement to explore the idea. That suggests clarifying expectations. A response is information to consider alongside relevant documents and later behaviour, not guaranteed access to a private motive.

## Respond to what you know

If an objection is concrete, agree on the next revision. If expectations differed, clarify what support means before the next meeting. If private encouragement and public distancing repeatedly diverge, stop presenting the former as a public commitment and ask for decisions to be recorded.

You can address the impact too: “If you identify a major issue before a meeting, please raise it with me beforehand.” You do not have to prove malicious intent to make that request.

Where a conversation could expose you to retaliation or harm, use the [support page](../support.md) to consider a safer route. Understanding and protection can proceed together.

<section class="key-takeaways" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="key-takeaways">

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Separate what happened from what you think it means.
- Consider several explanations, including circumstances and constraints.
- Check how your own expectations shape your interpretation.
- You can respond to harmful behaviour without proving someone's motives.
- Revise your explanation when new evidence appears.

</section>

<section class="reader-action" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="choose-response">

### Put it into practice: choose a response and a review point {#choose-response}

Choose one action supported by the evidence you have: a question, a clarification, a change to an arrangement or a boundary. Write down **what you expect it to clarify** and **when you will review what happened**.

At that point, record what actually changed—including anything that weakens your first account. Use the [worksheet](../worksheet.md) if you want to keep the steps together.
