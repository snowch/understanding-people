# Needs, motives and competing goals

Understanding motivation starts with three distinctions: **wanting an outcome, having a workable opportunity and carrying out the action**. Someone can care and still be unable to act under current conditions. Someone can also have the opportunity and honestly prefer a different goal. Ask what is missing before adding encouragement or pressure.

<section class="remember-cue" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="remember-this">

## One habit to practise {#remember-this}

When you or someone else wants to act but keeps missing it, ask: **“What is making it hard to do?”**

</section>

## Intention is not the same as action {#wanting-choosing-and-doing-are-different-questions}

Separate “Do I want this?”, “Can it fit my circumstances?” and “What will I do when the moment arrives?” Each can reveal a different problem.

Thomas Webb and Paschal Sheeran’s 2006 meta-analysis combined 47 experiments that changed intentions. Behaviour generally changed less than intentions did. It supports looking beyond commitment, not assigning a cause to one missed task. [Research record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16536643/).

**Brief fictional example:** A son wants to revise but cannot tell which topics he understands. Practice questions may reveal a learning problem that another speech about effort would miss. A different student may need time, access or a different agreement.

## Reasons for acting can coexist {#what-kind-of-reason-is-moving-you}

Enjoyment, personal value, rewards and avoiding disapproval are different reasons. A valued outcome can matter even when its daily tasks are not enjoyable.

The definition examples below use a separate fictional gardening setting.

<section class="definition" markdown="1" aria-label="Definitions: Intrinsic motivation, Extrinsic motivation">
<p class="definition-label">Definitions</p>
<dl>
<dt id="definition-intrinsic-motivation"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#intrinsic-motivation">Intrinsic motivation</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>Doing an activity because you enjoy it or find the activity itself interesting.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> Leila gardens because she enjoys growing plants.</p></dd>
<dt id="definition-extrinsic-motivation"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#extrinsic-motivation">Extrinsic motivation</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>Doing an activity for a result beyond the activity itself. The result may matter to you personally, or you may feel pressure to obtain it.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> Leila gardens because she values improving the neighbourhood, even on days when she does not enjoy the work.</p></dd>
</dl>
</section>

Richard Ryan and Edward Deci’s 2020 education review distinguishes these forms of motivation. Joshua Howard and colleagues’ 2021 meta-analysis found different associations between student motivation, persistence and well-being. These associations do not prove that changing someone’s stated reasons will produce the desired action. [Ryan and Deci](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2020.101860) · [Howard and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33593153/).

## Choice, capability and connection matter {#does-the-activity-offer-choice-confidence-and-connection}

Ask whether the person has a meaningful say, feels able to progress and experiences welcome or support. These questions concern distinct needs; they do not identify a hidden motive in advance.

<section class="definition" markdown="1" aria-label="Definitions: Autonomy, Competence, Relatedness">
<p class="definition-label">Definitions</p>
<dl>
<dt id="definition-autonomy"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#autonomy">Autonomy</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>Feeling that an action is your own choice and that you agree with doing it. This can include choosing to accept help.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> Leila chooses an occasional gardening session because it fits what she wants to contribute.</p></dd>
<dt id="definition-competence"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#competence">Competence</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>In self-determination theory, feeling able to handle a task and make progress.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> After someone shows Leila how to plant a seedling, she feels able to do the next one herself.</p></dd>
<dt id="definition-relatedness"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#relatedness">Relatedness</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>Feeling connected to other people and cared about by them.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> Leila feels welcome when another gardener remembers her name and invites her to work alongside them.</p></dd>
</dl>
</section>

Ryan and Deci discuss these needs in education; applying them to another setting produces questions to ask, not a proven explanation of that person.

**A separate brief example:** An adult daughter declines help organising a move but welcomes help carrying boxes. Receiving support and retaining decisions can coexist.

## Examine opportunity and competing demands {#who-gets-a-workable-opportunity}

<span id="what-else-would-this-choice-displace"></span>

Check time, skills, transport, money, access and other responsibilities. Ask what taking on this action would displace. The same person may want both connection and rest, or a promotion and time for care.

Susan Michie and colleagues developed COM-B around capability, opportunity and motivation after reviewing behaviour-change frameworks. It helps structure inquiry; it does not diagnose the cause of one action. [Read the paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21513547/).

Offer support matched to the obstacle: an explanation for an unclear task, access for a practical barrier, or a changed commitment when priorities conflict. Accept that a realistic answer may be no. A shared responsibility still needs a workable owner; understanding a barrier does not make its consequences vanish.

## Plan after making the action feasible {#make-the-next-step-specificafter-it-becomes-feasible}

A plan connects an action to a useful cue and includes what happens if circumstances change.

<section class="definition" markdown="1" aria-label="Definition: If–then plan">
<p class="definition-label">Definition</p>
<dl>
<dt id="definition-if-then-plan"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#if-then-plan">If–then plan</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>A plan that connects a particular situation to an action: if that situation occurs, you will do the planned action.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> Leila plans: “If the care visit finishes by ten, I will take the ten-twenty bus.”</p></dd>
</dl>
</section>

Martin Hagger and Aleksandra Luszczynska reviewed planning interventions in health contexts in 2014. Results varied, and the findings do not establish the effectiveness of every everyday plan. An if–then sentence cannot create missing resources. [Research record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24591064/).

## Review both action and feasibility {#review-what-the-change-actually-tells-you}

One success shows what worked on that occasion, not the only cause of earlier difficulty. One failure calls for checking what happened, not automatically increasing pressure. Review whether the person understood the plan, had the conditions to act and still chose the goal.

<section class="key-takeaways" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="key-takeaways">

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Wanting, being able and acting are different questions.
- Enjoyment, personal value and pressure can coexist but have different implications.
- Match help to the obstacle, keep choice meaningful and review an achievable agreement.

</section>

## Put it into practice {#put-it-into-practice}

<section class="practice-check" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="can-you-use-it">

### Recall {#can-you-use-it}

If someone says they want to act but repeatedly does not, what should you check before trying harder to motivate them?

<details class="answer-check" markdown="1"><summary>Show the useful distinction</summary>

Ask what makes action difficult: the next step, skills, resources, opportunity and competing aims. Also check whether the apparent commitment was genuinely chosen.

</details>

### Try it {#try-it}

**A new fictional situation:** A volunteer values a project but misses the early start because a care visit is unpredictable. The organiser needs dependable cover.

What arrangement could respect both concerns, and what would it let you learn? Say or write your answer before opening the example.

<details class="answer-check" markdown="1"><summary>Show an example answer</summary>

An occasional role without essential early cover, or a later task, might fit better if available. Clarify what each person can commit to and review it. Attendance once would establish feasibility on that occasion, not prove that care duties explained every absence.

</details>

</section>

### Use it this week {#plan-one-step}

This week, choose one stalled action of your own. Ask **“What is making it hard to do?”** Check one suspected barrier, try a feasible adjustment and review what happened. If another person is involved, agree the change rather than designing their behaviour without them.

#### Another comparison

**A separate fictional situation:** You complete a disliked administrative task because you value keeping a service available. Must the task become enjoyable for your motivation to be genuine?
