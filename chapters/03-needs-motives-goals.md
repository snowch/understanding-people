# Needs, motives and competing goals

On Sunday evening, Leila signs up to help at a community garden. She wants to meet people and spend more time outdoors. By Saturday morning she is sorting out a care visit for her father. The bus leaves before she is ready, and she stays home. Two weeks later, she misses another session.

The organiser sees two empty spaces on the rota. Leila sees another promise she has failed to keep. Both reach for the same explanation: she must not be motivated enough.

Before trying to encourage Leila, ask what is making attendance difficult. Does she want to come? Does she have enough time? Can she get there? Does she know she would be welcome if she arrived late?

## Wanting to do something does not make it happen {#wanting-choosing-and-doing-are-different-questions}

In a 2006 paper, Webb and Sheeran combined results from 47 experiments that changed how strongly people intended to act. On average, behaviour changed less than intentions did. Wanting something more strongly did not lead to an equally large change in what people did. That helps explain why we should look beyond Leila’s wish to attend, but it does not tell us what stopped her. [Read the research summary](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16536643/).

Separate three questions:

| Question | What it asks in Leila's case |
| --- | --- |
| Do I want this? | Does taking part still matter to me, and why? |
| Can this fit my circumstances? | Can I manage the care visit, journey and session together? |
| What do I need to do when the time comes? | Do I know when to leave, whom to meet and what to do if I am delayed? |

A motivational speech addresses only some possibilities. It cannot change a bus timetable. Equally, arranging transport will not create an interest that Leila does not have.

## Why do you want to do it? {#what-kind-of-reason-is-moving-you}

<section class="definition" markdown="1" aria-label="Definitions: Intrinsic motivation, Extrinsic motivation">
<p class="definition-label">Definitions</p>
<dl>
<dt id="definition-intrinsic-motivation"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#intrinsic-motivation">Intrinsic motivation</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>Doing an activity because you enjoy it or find the activity itself interesting.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> Leila gardens because she enjoys growing plants.</p></dd>
<dt id="definition-extrinsic-motivation"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#extrinsic-motivation">Extrinsic motivation</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>Doing an activity for a result beyond the activity itself. The result may matter to you personally, or you may feel pressure to obtain it.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> Leila gardens because she values improving the neighbourhood, even on days when she does not enjoy the work.</p></dd>
</dl>
</section>

A theory called **self-determination theory** examines why people act and whether their reasons feel like their own choice. In a 2020 paper about education, Ryan and Deci explain the difference between enjoying an activity and doing it for another result. Both can matter: a personally valued goal and pressure from others are different reasons, even though both are called extrinsic motivation. [Read their paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2020.101860).

Leila might enjoy growing things, value contributing to her neighbourhood, want companionship and feel guilty about disappointing the organiser. These reasons can coexist. A useful conversation asks which ones matter to her, rather than assigning a hidden motive.

In a 2021 paper, Howard and colleagues combined findings from studies of students. Enjoyment was linked with success and wellbeing; seeing personal value in the work was particularly linked with keeping at it. These links do not prove that one caused the other. They also do not show that changing Leila’s reasons would make her attend the garden. [Read the research summary](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33593153/).

<section class="reader-action" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="name-reasons">

### Try this: name your reasons {#name-reasons}

Choose something you keep intending to do. Complete these sentences:

- **I enjoy…**
- **It matters to me because…**
- **I feel pressure to…**
- **If nobody judged my decision, I would…**

You may want to do it for several reasons and also have reasons to avoid it. If you are helping someone else, ask for their answers instead of deciding for them.

</section>

## Does Leila feel free to choose, able to help and welcome? {#does-the-activity-offer-choice-confidence-and-connection}

<section class="definition" markdown="1" aria-label="Definitions: Autonomy, Competence, Relatedness">
<p class="definition-label">Definitions</p>
<dl>
<dt id="definition-autonomy"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#autonomy">Autonomy</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>Feeling that an action is your own choice and that you agree with doing it. This can include choosing to accept help.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> Leila chooses an occasional gardening session because it fits what she wants to contribute.</p></dd>
<dt id="definition-competence"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#competence">Competence</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>In self-determination theory, feeling able to handle a task and make progress.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> After someone shows Leila how to plant a seedling, she feels able to do the next one herself.</p></dd>
<dt id="definition-relatedness"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#relatedness">Relatedness</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>Feeling connected to other people and cared about by them.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> Leila feels welcome when another gardener remembers her name and invites her to work alongside them.</p></dd>
</dl>
</section>

In the same 2020 paper, Ryan and Deci explain autonomy, competence and relatedness as three psychological needs. The paper focuses on education. Here we are using the ideas to suggest questions about Leila’s gardening experience. These questions do not tell us in advance what her answer will be. [Read their paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2020.101860).

Perhaps Leila signed up for a regular slot because she thought that was the only acceptable contribution. Perhaps she worries that everyone else knows more about gardening. Perhaps “just come along” leaves her unsure who will greet her. Asking about these possibilities leads to different responses: a choice of commitments, a clear introductory task or a named person to meet.

The organiser could ask: “Would an occasional session suit you better? Is there anything about the first visit that is unclear?” Leila must also be free to say that the activity no longer fits. Supporting choice includes accepting an answer other than attendance.

## Do the garden’s arrangements make it possible to join? {#who-gets-a-workable-opportunity}

Now look at how the garden is run. It opens on Saturday mornings because that suits existing members. The rota assumes everyone can arrive on time. Newcomers may not yet be in the group chat. Any of those arrangements could affect whether Leila can take part.

Sociologist John Wilson brought together research on volunteering in papers published in 2000 and 2012. He examined people’s reasons for volunteering alongside their resources, relationships and links to organisations. Much of that research observed existing behaviour rather than testing changes in experiments. It suggests questions about access and relationships, but cannot prove that changing a particular arrangement will improve attendance. [Read the 2000 paper](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1312130); [read the 2012 paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/0899764011434558).

In our example, Leila already spends time helping someone: her father. A rota records attendance at the garden, not all the care she provides. The organiser has a legitimate need for reliable numbers, but calling her uncommitted would turn a narrow observation into a broad judgment.

Notice who can change what. The organiser may be able to offer a later start or a shorter session. Leila cannot change the timetable simply by wanting to attend. The organiser also needs enough people to run the activity safely.

<section class="reader-action" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="look-beyond-person">

### Try this: check what makes taking part difficult {#look-beyond-person}

For an activity you help organise, ask what makes joining difficult. Consider **time, cost, transport, caring responsibilities, accessibility and rules that newcomers may not know**. Which could you change?

Ask, “What makes taking part difficult?” rather than requiring someone to disclose private circumstances to prove their commitment. Offer feasible options and let them decide what to share.

</section>

## What would Leila have to give up to attend? {#what-else-would-this-choice-displace}

Leila already uses Saturday mornings for care, errands and rest. Time at the garden would take time away from something else she values. Ask what she would have to give up before treating attendance as an easy choice.

List the other options. Leila could attend a shorter session, come occasionally, help with a different task or decide she cannot take on another commitment now. These are suggestions for this example, rather than evidence that one arrangement is best for everyone.

The organiser also has goals: sufficient help, fair workloads and less uncertainty. A negotiated arrangement should account for both sides. “An occasional visit with no essential rota duty” may fit better than repeated promises about a fixed slot.

## Agree on a plan that Leila can actually carry out {#make-the-next-step-specificafter-it-becomes-feasible}

Once Leila chooses a workable arrangement, a plan can connect an intention to a particular moment: “If the care visit finishes by ten, I will take the ten-twenty bus and meet Sam at the gate. If it runs late, I will message Sam and use the next agreed date.”

<section class="definition" markdown="1" aria-label="Definition: If–then plan">
<p class="definition-label">Definition</p>
<dl>
<dt id="definition-if-then-plan"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#if-then-plan">If–then plan</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>A plan that connects a particular situation to an action: if that situation occurs, you will do the planned action.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> Leila plans: “If the care visit finishes by ten, I will take the ten-twenty bus.”</p></dd>
</dl>
</section>

In a 2014 paper, Hagger and Luszczynska examined studies of planning and health behaviour. Planning helped in some settings, but the size of the benefit varied and the studies had limitations. Leila’s bus plan is an everyday use of the idea; it cannot solve a problem such as having no available transport. [Read their paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/aphw.12017).

## Did the new arrangement help? {#review-what-the-change-actually-tells-you}

Suppose Leila joins one shorter session. That shows this arrangement was possible on that occasion. It does not prove that transport was the only barrier, that she now has a permanent habit or that every newcomer needs the same help.

Suppose she still does not attend. Ask what happened before increasing the pressure. Perhaps care demands changed again; perhaps she realised she prefers another activity. The next useful decision might be to stop reserving a rota place while keeping the invitation open.

Leila can replace “I am unreliable” with a clearer description: “I cannot promise a fixed arrival time while I am arranging care.” The organiser can offer a commitment that fits that limit. Both still need to tell the other if the plan changes.

You can use these questions in other situations, such as studying, exercising or sharing housework: **Do I want to do this? Can it fit my life? What do I need to do when the time comes?** The answers may point towards more practice, a different arrangement, a clearer plan or a decision to decline.

<section class="key-takeaways" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="key-takeaways">

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Wanting to do something, having a workable opportunity and following through are different questions.
- Enjoyment, personal value and pressure can coexist; ask which reasons matter to the person.
- Look at time, resources, relationships and group arrangements alongside individual motivation.
- For your own goals, ask what you would have to give up and whether you still want to do it.
- Make a plan you can carry out, then check whether it helped. One result does not prove why it worked or failed.

</section>

<section class="reader-action" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="plan-one-step">

### Put it into practice: agree on one step you can take {#plan-one-step}

Choose a goal you still want to pursue. Write **when you will act, what you will do first, and what you will do if your main difficulty gets in the way**. Your backup plan can include postponing or declining.

If you are supporting someone else, agree on any reminder rather than imposing one. Set a time to review whether the arrangement helped, and whose effort it required.

</section>
