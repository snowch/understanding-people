# Questions, listening and disagreement

Understanding another person usually requires new information, not a more elaborate guess. **Ask a useful question, listen for correction and locate the actual disagreement.** A conversation can improve understanding without guaranteeing honesty, agreement or a solution.

<section class="remember-cue" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="remember-this">

## One habit to practise {#remember-this}

After someone explains a concern, pause before replying. **“Have I understood you correctly?”**

</section>

## Get information rather than only imagining an answer {#get-information-instead-of-refining-a-guess}

Your experience can suggest possibilities, but it does not reveal another person’s mind. Ask what matters to them and what would change the practical decision.

Tal Eyal and colleagues’ 2018 experiments asked people to predict others’ thoughts, feelings or preferences. Imagining the other person’s perspective did not consistently improve accuracy. A final experiment found benefit from obtaining information through conversation. These tasks do not establish that every direct question is safe or sufficient. [Research record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29620401/).

**Brief fictional example:** A friend delays a holiday decision. Asking what remains to be decided can distinguish cost, dates and another person’s approval from the assumption that they do not want to travel with you.

## Start open, then clarify the relevant detail {#start-open-then-make-the-uncertainty-specific}

<section class="definition" markdown="1" aria-label="Definition: Open question">
<p class="definition-label">Definition</p>
<dl>
<dt id="definition-open-question"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#open-question">Open question</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>A question that invites someone to explain in their own words, rather than giving a yes-or-no answer.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> Imani asks Rosa, “What do you still need to decide about the holiday?”</p></dd>
</dl>
</section>

An open question lets the person describe the issue. A precise question can then clarify a date, term or commitment. Yes-or-no questions can be appropriate for a specific decision; the purpose is useful information, not making every sentence open-ended.

Avoid asking several questions at once or embedding an accusation: “Why do you never care?” already assumes both the pattern and its meaning.

## Listen for correction {#listen-for-correction-not-just-your-turn-to-speak}

Summarise the concern briefly and ask what you missed. Understanding is different from endorsing a disputed fact or agreeing to a request.

Guy Itzchakov and colleagues’ 2017 laboratory experiments found that high-quality listening could reduce speakers’ social anxiety and help them recognise contradictions in their views on the topics studied. The findings do not promise agreement or relationship repair. [Research record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27856728/).

**A separate brief example:** “You want us to meet, but the proposed cost is too high. Have I understood?” creates room for a correction before you propose another plan.

## Avoid supplying the remembered answer {#keep-memory-questions-free-of-unnecessary-suggestion}

<section class="definition" markdown="1" aria-label="Definition: Leading question">
<p class="definition-label">Definition</p>
<dl>
<dt id="definition-leading-question"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#leading-question">Leading question</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>A question worded in a way that suggests the answer the questioner expects.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> “You promised on Tuesday, didn’t you?” suggests both that there was a promise and when it was made.</p></dd>
</dl>
</section>

Ask what someone remembers before asserting the wording or timing you expect. Keep a direct record separate from an approximate recollection.

Elizabeth Loftus’s 2005 review examines how later misinformation can affect memory reports. It is a reason for care with questioning, not proof that a particular memory or report of harm is false. [Research record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16027179/).

## Identify the layer of disagreement {#find-the-layer-of-disagreement}

| The disagreement concerns… | A useful next question |
| --- | --- |
| What happened | Which detail or relevant record could clarify it? |
| What it meant | What did each person understand or fear? |
| What should matter | Which values or priorities conflict? |
| What can be done | What constraints and responsibilities apply? |

More evidence about a fact may not settle a value dispute. Acknowledging a feeling may not decide who owns a task.

## Know when to stop asking

A person can decline disclosure. You may also need to pause, seek support or use a formal process when conversation is repetitive, unsafe or outside your role. Curiosity does not justify interrogation, surveillance or a demand for someone’s history. Explain your own need and decide what action is reasonable with the information available.

<section class="key-takeaways" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="key-takeaways">

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- New information is more useful than confident mind-reading.
- Use open and precise questions for different purposes, and invite correction.
- Separate facts, meanings, values and arrangements; respect privacy and limits.

</section>

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="put-it-into-practice">

## Put it into practice {#put-it-into-practice}

<section class="practice-check" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="can-you-use-it">

### Recall {#can-you-use-it}

What makes a follow-up question useful after someone explains a concern?

<details class="answer-check" markdown="1"><summary>Show the useful distinction</summary>

It checks an uncertainty that could change your understanding or next action, rather than merely inviting more talk or steering toward your preferred answer.

</details>

### Try it {#try-it}

**A new fictional situation:** A teammate says a proposed deadline is difficult. You assume they dislike the project and start explaining its importance.

What could you ask before giving that explanation, and how would you check the answer? Say or write your answer before opening the example.

<details class="answer-check" markdown="1"><summary>Show an example answer</summary>

Ask which part of the deadline or work is difficult. Summarise the answer and clarify the relevant constraint. The person may value the project while lacking capacity; if that is the issue, a speech about importance will not resolve it.

</details>

</section>

### Use it this week {#listen-check}

This week, choose one ordinary conversation. Ask a question whose answer could change your next step, then use **“Have I understood you correctly?”** Record the correction you received and what you did with it. Respect a refusal or an unsafe setting.
