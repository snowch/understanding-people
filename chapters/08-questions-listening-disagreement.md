# Questions, listening and disagreement

Two friends are organising a shared holiday. Imani wants to book early; Rosa keeps saying she needs more time. Imani prepares a speech about indecision. Before delivering it, she asks, “What do you still need to decide?” Rosa replies, “Whether I can afford the total once food and transport are included.”

That answer changes the problem. It also shows the limit of imagining someone else's perspective: Imani had imagined Rosa choosing between destinations, while Rosa was deciding whether she could go at all.

## Get information instead of refining a guess

Across 25 experiments, Eyal, Steffel and Epley found no consistent accuracy benefit from instructions to take another person's perspective. In a final experiment, obtaining the person's perspective through conversation improved accuracy. The tasks and comparisons do not establish that asking always produces a complete or truthful account; they do challenge the assumption that imagining more carefully is enough. [Eyal and colleagues, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29620401/).

Perspective-taking can generate possibilities to ask about. It should not replace the answer. Imani's first useful move is to let Rosa supply information that was absent from her own story.

A question also needs a purpose. “Tell me everything going on in your life” is a large demand. “What would you need to know before deciding about the holiday?” keeps the conversation connected to the decision and allows Rosa to choose how much to disclose.

## Start open, then make the uncertainty specific

<dl>
<dt id="definition-open-question"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#open-question">Open question</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>A question that invites someone to explain in their own words, rather than giving a yes-or-no answer.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> Imani asks Rosa, “What do you still need to decide about the holiday?”</p></dd>
</dl>
</section>

Start by giving the person room to explain. Then use focused follow-up questions to check the details. Both can help:

| Less useful opening | A question that leaves more room |
| --- | --- |
| “Why are you being difficult?” | “What is making this decision difficult?” |
| “You don't like the place, do you?” | “How are you feeling about the options?” |
| “Is it the hotel, flights or dates?” | “What have we not accounted for?” |
| “So you'll agree if it's cheaper?” | “Would a lower total change your decision, or is there something else?” |

These wordings are suggestions, not magic phrases. Tone, timing and the relationship matter. A calm question followed by punishment for an unwelcome answer does not create a meaningful choice.

Once Rosa identifies cost, Imani can ask whether a total budget would help. Rosa need not disclose her income or debts. “I can spend this amount” provides what the plan requires without opening her finances to inspection.

<section class="reader-action" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="remove-answer">

### Try this: remove the answer from your question {#remove-answer}

Write the question you were about to ask. Underline any accusation, assumed motive or suggested answer. Rewrite it so the person can describe what you have missed.

Then add one focused follow-up about the actual decision. Check that you would be willing to hear “No” or “I would rather not discuss that.”

</section>

## Listen for correction, not just your turn to speak

A summary can reveal whether you understood: “You like the idea, but the listed price leaves out costs that could make it unaffordable. Is that right?” Rosa can correct both the content and the emphasis.

Itzchakov, Kluger and Castro's four laboratory experiments examined high-quality listening, characterised by empathy and a non-judgmental approach. Results supported changes in speakers' anxiety and attitude structure, including greater awareness of conflicting considerations. These findings concern the studied conversations; they do not mean listening guarantees agreement, accuracy or a repaired relationship. [Itzchakov and colleagues, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27856728/).

A practical implication is to allow an answer to be mixed. Rosa may want to go and worry about spending. She should not have to eliminate one side to make the other intelligible. Imani can hear both and still explain that a booking deadline exists.

Avoid using a summary as a disguised rebuttal: “So you want everyone to wait because you didn't plan.” That adds a judgment Rosa did not supply. If you have a concern about the delay, state it separately and own it.

## Keep memory questions free of unnecessary suggestion

<section class="definition" markdown="1" aria-label="Definition: Leading question">
<p class="definition-label">Definition</p>
<dl>
<dt id="definition-leading-question"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#leading-question">Leading question</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>A question worded in a way that suggests the answer the questioner expects.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> “You promised on Tuesday, didn’t you?” suggests both that there was a promise and when it was made.</p></dd>
</dl>
</section>

A question can be both open and leading. “Why did you break your promise?” invites an explanation, but it also assumes a promise was broken.

If the friends disagree about an earlier promise, begin with what each remembers. “What do you remember us agreeing?” leaves more room than “You promised on Tuesday, didn't you?” Separate remembered wording from the meaning each person took from it.

Loftus's review describes how exposure to misleading information after an event can affect later memory reports. This supports caution about supplying details inside a question. It does not show that a particular memory is false, nor justify dismissing someone's account of harm because memory can be fallible. [Loftus, 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16027179/).

Where a message or document exists, examine it together if appropriate. “I'd love to come” may be an expression of interest rather than a booking commitment. If the old agreement remains unclear, make the next one specific. Avoid repeatedly questioning someone in an attempt to extract the version you prefer.

## Find the layer of disagreement

Some disagreements concern facts: what the quoted price includes. Others concern forecasts: whether booking later will cost more. Others concern values or terms: whether everyone must stay in the same accommodation, and who pays for cancellation.

Address the layer you can actually resolve. Obtain the full quote for a factual gap. Discuss acceptable uncertainty for a forecast. Negotiate the arrangement when preferences differ. No amount of explaining the hotel price can decide how much Rosa should be willing to spend.

Imani might say, “I need to book by Friday. Could we compare the full costs tonight? If it doesn't fit your budget, I can book my trip and we can plan something else together.” This is a clear proposal with an alternative, rather than a claim to know what Rosa really wants.

## Know when to stop asking

Rosa may decide not to go. Once the relevant information is understood, further questions can become pressure. The goal is a defensible decision, not an answer that matches your preference.

If the conversation becomes threatening or carries a risk of retaliation, the methods here are not a requirement to continue. Use the [support page](../support.md). In an ordinary disagreement, a pause or written clarification may be enough; in a coercive situation, independent support may matter more than better phrasing.

Review your contribution afterwards. Did you learn something new, or merely secure agreement? Did you let the answer change your plan? Those questions are useful checks on whether your questions helped you understand.

<section class="key-takeaways" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="key-takeaways">

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Imagining another perspective can generate questions; obtaining new information can correct the guess.
- Begin with room for an unexpected answer, then clarify the details needed for the decision.
- Summarise for correction and allow someone to hold mixed views.
- Check your own questions for suggested answers, especially when discussing memory.
- Distinguish factual gaps from different preferences, and stop when further questioning becomes pressure.

</section>

<section class="reader-action" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="listen-check">

### Put it into practice: ask, summarise and check {#listen-check}

In a welcome, low-stakes conversation, ask one open question. Let the answer finish. Say **“What I understand is…”** and invite a correction before explaining your own view.

Record one thing you learned and one way it changes—or fails to change—the next decision. A useful conversation can end with respectful disagreement.

</section>
