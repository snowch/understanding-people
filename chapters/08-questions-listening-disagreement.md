Two friends are organising a shared holiday. Imani wants to book early; Rosa keeps saying she needs more time. Imani prepares a speech about indecision. Before delivering it, she asks, “What do you still need to decide?” Rosa replies, “Whether I can afford the total once food and transport are included.”

That answer changes the problem. It also shows the limit of imagining someone else's perspective: Imani had imagined Rosa choosing between destinations, while Rosa was deciding whether she could go at all.

<section class="remember-cue" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="remember-this">

## One habit to practise {#remember-this}

After someone explains a concern, pause before replying. **“Have I understood you correctly?”**

</section>

## Ask the person instead of only imagining their answer {#get-information-instead-of-refining-a-guess}

In a 2018 paper, Eyal, Steffel and Epley reported experiments testing whether imagining another person’s point of view helped people understand them accurately. Across 25 experiments, that instruction did not consistently improve accuracy. In a final experiment, talking to the person did help. This does not mean every answer will be complete or truthful; it shows the value of obtaining information you did not already have. [Read the paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29620401/).

Imagining Rosa’s reasons could help Imani think of questions. But Rosa’s answer gives her something new: the worry is total cost, not choosing a destination.

A question also needs a purpose. “Tell me everything going on in your life” is a large demand. “What would you need to know before deciding about the holiday?” keeps the conversation connected to the decision and allows Rosa to choose how much to disclose.

## Let them explain, then ask about the details {#start-open-then-make-the-uncertainty-specific}

<section class="definition" markdown="1" aria-label="Definition: Open question">
<p class="definition-label">Definition</p>
<dl>
<dt id="definition-open-question"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#open-question">Open question</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>A question that invites someone to explain in their own words, rather than giving a yes-or-no answer.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> Imani asks Rosa, “What do you still need to decide about the holiday?”</p></dd>
</dl>
</section>

Start by giving the person room to explain. Then use focused follow-up questions to check the details. Both can help:

| Less useful opening | A question that leaves more room |
| --- | --- |
| “Why are you being difficult?” | “What is making this decision difficult?” |
| “You don't like the place, do you?” | “How are you feeling about the options?” |
| “Is it the hotel, flights or dates?” | “Is there a cost or difficulty we have missed?” |
| “So you'll agree if it's cheaper?” | “Would a lower total change your decision, or is there something else?” |

These are suggested questions. They will not help much if the person is punished for an answer you dislike. Let the answer affect the decision, including the possibility that they will decline.

Once Rosa identifies cost, Imani can ask whether a total budget would help. Rosa need not disclose her income or debts. “I can spend this amount” provides what the plan requires without opening her finances to inspection.

## Check that you understood their answer {#listen-for-correction-not-just-your-turn-to-speak}

Return to the holiday conversation. Imani could say, “You like the idea, but food and transport might make it too expensive. Is that right?” Rosa can tell her whether she has understood.

In four laboratory experiments reported in 2017, Itzchakov, Kluger and Castro studied what happened when people felt listened to with understanding and without judgment. Their findings supported changes in anxiety and in how people understood their own views, including noticing conflicting feelings. The experiments do not show that careful listening always produces agreement or repairs a relationship. [Read the paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27856728/).

Rosa can want to go and worry about spending at the same time. Imani can recognise both feelings while explaining that she needs to book by a certain date.

“So you want everyone to wait because you didn’t plan” adds blame to Rosa’s answer. If Imani is worried about the delay, she can say so directly: “I’m worried the price will rise if we wait.”

## When asking about the past, avoid supplying the answer {#keep-memory-questions-free-of-unnecessary-suggestion}

<section class="definition" markdown="1" aria-label="Definition: Leading question">
<p class="definition-label">Definition</p>
<dl>
<dt id="definition-leading-question"><dfn><a href="../glossary.md#leading-question">Leading question</a></dfn></dt>
<dd><p>A question worded in a way that suggests the answer the questioner expects.</p><p class="definition-example"><strong>Example:</strong> “You promised on Tuesday, didn’t you?” suggests both that there was a promise and when it was made.</p></dd>
</dl>
</section>

A question can be both open and leading. “Why did you break your promise?” invites an explanation, but it also assumes a promise was broken.

If the friends disagree about an earlier promise, begin with what each remembers. “What do you remember us agreeing?” leaves more room than “You promised on Tuesday, didn't you?” Separate remembered wording from the meaning each person took from it.

In a 2005 paper, Elizabeth Loftus examined research on how misleading information received after an event can affect what people later remember. That is a reason to avoid putting unverified details into your questions. It does not prove that a particular memory is false or justify dismissing someone’s account of harm. [Read the paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16027179/).

Where a message or document exists, examine it together if appropriate. “I'd love to come” may be an expression of interest rather than a booking commitment. If the old agreement remains unclear, make the next one specific. Avoid repeatedly questioning someone in an attempt to extract the version you prefer.

## What exactly do you disagree about? {#find-the-layer-of-disagreement}

Imani and Rosa might disagree about what the hotel price includes. They might be unsure whether waiting will make it more expensive. Or they might want different things: one wants everyone in the same hotel; the other wants the cheapest trip. Each problem needs a different discussion.

Check the full quote to settle what the price includes. Discuss how much risk each person is willing to take about booking later. If their preferences differ, look for an arrangement both can accept. Explaining the price cannot decide how much Rosa should spend.

Imani might say, “I need to book by Friday. Could we compare the full costs tonight? If it doesn't fit your budget, I can book my trip and we can plan something else together.” This is a clear proposal with an alternative, rather than a claim to know what Rosa really wants.

## Know when to stop asking

Rosa may decide not to go. Once she understands the options and has given an answer, more questions can become pressure. Imani needs to decide what to do with Rosa’s answer, not keep asking until it changes.

If asking questions could lead to threats or harm, you do not have to continue. Use the [support page](../support.md). Better wording cannot make an unsafe relationship safe on its own.

Review your contribution afterwards. Did you learn something new, or merely secure agreement? Did you let the answer change your plan? Those questions are useful checks on whether your questions helped you understand.

<section class="key-takeaways" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="key-takeaways">

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Imagining someone’s reasons can help you think of questions. Their answer may reveal what you missed.
- Begin with room for an unexpected answer, then clarify the details needed for the decision.
- Say what you understood and let the person correct you. They can feel two ways about the same choice.
- Check your own questions for suggested answers, especially when discussing memory.
- Check whether you need more facts or simply want different things. Stop asking when it becomes pressure.

</section>

### Recall {#can-you-use-it}

Without looking back, what should you do after someone explains a concern, before giving your answer?

<details class="answer-check" markdown="1">
<summary>Show the question to remember</summary>

**“Have I understood you correctly?”** Your wording can differ; keep the same idea.

### Try it {#try-it}

**A new fictional situation:** Tess wants her brother to help clear a storage room. He says, “Not this weekend.” Tess thinks he is avoiding the work. He later explains that he cannot lift heavy boxes while his wrist heals.

What could Tess ask without assuming a motive? How could she check her understanding of the reply? Say or write your answer before opening the example.

<details class="answer-check" markdown="1">
<summary>Show an example answer</summary>

“What makes this weekend difficult?” leaves room to explain. After hearing about the wrist, Tess could say, “Your wrist makes lifting difficult. Have I understood that correctly?” She should not turn that answer into a promise to help next weekend.

</details>

</section>

### Use it this week {#listen-check}

Let someone finish in one ordinary conversation. Summarise what you understood and invite correction before giving your view. Note what you learned and whether it changed your response. Respect a decision not to discuss something.

#### Ask without suggesting the answer {#remove-answer}

Choose a question you want to ask someone in your own life. Does it already assume what they meant? For example, “Why did you ignore me?” assumes deliberate ignoring. “Did you see my message?” checks something you do not yet know.

Rewrite your question so it allows an answer you did not expect. Be ready to hear “No” or “I would rather not discuss that.”
