# Motivation, influence and cooperation

Helping people act is different from getting them to comply once. **Start with a useful shared outcome, identify what makes action possible and check the costs to everyone involved.** This chapter applies the motivation questions from Chapter 3 to cooperation, learning and buying decisions.

<section class="remember-cue" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="remember-this">

## One habit to practise {#remember-this}

Before adding a reward, reminder or pressure, ask: **“What improvement do we actually want?”**

</section>

## Agree the aim and make an honest request {#start-with-a-shared-aim-and-an-honest-request}

State what needs to improve and why. Ask whether others share that aim, what they can contribute and which constraints matter. A shared interest does not guarantee agreement about the method.

**Brief fictional example:** A group wants meetings to finish on time. Before demanding faster answers, clarify which decisions need preparation and whether members receive the information early enough.

A request leaves room for an honest answer. A consequence hidden behind friendly wording can make apparent agreement misleading. Explain relevant terms and responsibilities rather than using personal disclosures as pressure points.

## Find the obstacle before increasing pressure {#diagnose-the-obstacle-before-increasing-the-pressure}

Distinguish an unclear task, missing skill, lack of access, insufficient capacity and a conflicting priority. These are starting questions, not an exhaustive classification or a diagnostic method. An agreed responsibility still needs a practical owner when a barrier is real.

Use one small change matched to the reported difficulty: clearer instructions, a suitable tool, a different time or a narrower commitment. Review whether it helped. Chapter 3 and the [capability, opportunity and motivation reference](../reference/change-conditions.md) explain the framework behind these questions.

## Choose a measure that matches the improvement {#incentives-and-interest-do-not-have-one-simple-relationship}

Rewards can influence the action you count while leaving another outcome unchanged. More messages are not necessarily better support; more completed tasks are not necessarily higher-quality work.

Christopher Cerasoli and colleagues’ 2014 meta-analysis examined intrinsic motivation, incentives and performance. Relationships differed for quality, quantity and how incentives were tied to performance. The results do not support either “rewards always help” or “rewards always harm.” [Research record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24491020/).

Ask what the incentive encourages, what it may displace and whether people can report a problem honestly. Recognition, payment, meaningful work and practical support do different jobs.

## Support a useful method, not only more effort {#support-effective-action-not-just-more-effort}

**A separate brief example:** A student spends hours rereading notes but cannot answer questions without them. More hours of the same activity may not address the problem.

John Dunlosky and colleagues’ 2013 review evaluated ten study techniques, finding broad support for practice testing and spacing study across sessions. That supports trying relevant recall questions and returning to material later; it does not guarantee grades or establish one timetable for every learner. [Research record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173288/).

For other skills, ask what an effective method looks like in that domain. Evidence about studying does not automatically validate a rehearsal, sales or relationship technique.

## Make cooperation feasible for both sides {#make-cooperation-possible-by-comparing-constraints}

Clarify the outcome, ownership, timing, resources and early warning if a promise becomes unrealistic. Equal responsibility need not mean identical actions, but the division should be understood and fair. Count coordination work and the burden of rescuing missed tasks.

## A buying decision belongs to the buyer {#a-buying-decision-is-also-the-buyers-decision}

A client may need evidence of fit, budget approval, access support or time to compare options. Ask which uncertainty matters. Explain benefits, costs and limitations honestly; a trial should have a clear purpose and suitable terms.

These are editorial standards for a fair decision, not a guaranteed closing script. Do not invent urgency or hide material conditions. Better understanding can appropriately lead to no purchase.

## Review cooperation from both sides {#review-cooperation-from-both-sides}

Check whether the useful outcome improved, whether the work was sustainable and whether a new cost appeared. Include your own follow-through. Agreement, gratitude or one successful attempt alone does not establish lasting cooperation.

<section class="key-takeaways" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="key-takeaways">

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Define a useful shared outcome before choosing pressure, rewards or reminders.
- Match support to the task and distinguish quality from quantity.
- Review sustainability, fair workload and informed choice as well as immediate results.

</section>

## Put it into practice {#put-it-into-practice}

### Recall {#can-you-use-it}

Before adding a reward or reminder, what should you specify and check?

<details class="answer-check" markdown="1"><summary>Show the useful distinction</summary>

Ask **“What improvement do we actually want?”** Then check the barrier, the method and whether the chosen measure reflects that improvement.

</details>

### Try it {#try-it}

**A new fictional situation:** A support team is rewarded for answering more tickets. The ticket count rises, but customers contact the team again because issues remain unresolved.

What distinction matters, and what would you review before changing the incentive? Say or write your answer before opening the example.

<details class="answer-check" markdown="1"><summary>Show an example answer</summary>

Volume and useful resolution differ. Examine case difficulty, support methods and repeat contacts rather than assuming laziness or removing all incentives. Check both quality and workload before agreeing a change.

</details>

</section>

### Use it this week {#agree-experiment}

This week, identify one measure you use for your own improvement or shared work. Ask whether it reflects the outcome you want. Make one appropriate adjustment and review quality, burden and other people’s experience—not only the number.
