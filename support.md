# When this book is the wrong tool

Sometimes “Why are they doing this?” is less useful than “What help is needed?” or “What would make this safer?” Understanding behaviour can support a decision, but it need not come before care or protection.

## When the question is about mental health

You may wonder whether withdrawal, missed commitments or difficult interactions indicate depression or a personality disorder. A short account of someone's behaviour cannot settle those questions. Avoid treating the chapters as symptom checklists or using a diagnostic label as an explanation of a disagreement.

If you are concerned about your own mental health, a primary care clinician or qualified mental health professional can help you assess what is happening and discuss support. The US National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) recommends seeking professional help for severe or distressing symptoms lasting two weeks or more and identifies changes affecting sleep, concentration, interest and usual activities as reasons for concern. You can seek advice earlier; that timeframe is not a requirement to wait when something worries you. [NIMH guidance](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/topics/caring-for-your-mental-health).

For someone else, you can describe what you have noticed without proposing a diagnosis: “You seem to be having a hard time, and you've mentioned struggling with everyday tasks. Would you like help finding someone to talk to?” Respect their choices where there is no immediate safety emergency.

### Put it into practice: prepare for a conversation about help {#prepare-help}

If it is safe to make notes, record **what changed, when it began and how it affects daily life**. Include what you want help with. Bring those observations to a health professional, or use them to open a supportive conversation with the person concerned.

You do not need to arrive with a diagnosis.

## When there is fear, coercion or abuse

If someone threatens you, controls your access to money or support, or makes you afraid of their reaction, confronting them to test an explanation may increase the risk. Consider confidential advice from an appropriate local domestic-abuse service and a safety plan suited to your circumstances. You do not have to wait for an emergency to seek help. The UK National Health Service (NHS) [domestic-abuse page](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/getting-help-for-domestic-violence/) describes support options in the UK; readers elsewhere should use services in their own country.

If your device or browsing may be monitored, consider a safer device or another way to contact support. Use the support service's advice about digital safety rather than assuming private-browsing mode makes activity invisible.

For immediate danger or an urgent risk of serious harm, contact emergency services where you are. More analysis of someone's motives can wait.

## When the decision needs specialist evidence

A legal dispute, a safeguarding concern or a high-stakes medical or financial decision may require an appropriate professional or formal reporting process. A conversation technique cannot establish the facts those decisions require.

For everyday decisions, return to the [worksheet](worksheet.md). What can you reasonably do with the information you have? What do you still need to know? Who could help?
