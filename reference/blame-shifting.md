# Shifting responsibility

Shifting responsibility describes redirecting accountability for an action toward another person or circumstance. The description can be useful when an explanation repeatedly leaves the actor with nothing to own. It is not proof that context is irrelevant or that every shared problem belongs to one person.

> **Remember:** Separate each person’s action and responsibility.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

This material provides general information and has not received specialist review. It is not a diagnostic tool or an individual assessment of safety. For advice about your circumstances, seek appropriately qualified support.

See [When to seek other help](../support.md) for support routes and their limits.

## How it may arise

A person may avoid shame, dispute the facts, misunderstand their role or genuinely identify another contributor. You can do the same when you focus on what provoked you rather than what you chose. Context can explain difficulty without making a harmful response inevitable.

## What the evidence supports

Roy Lewicki and colleagues ran two studies of how people judged apologies after hypothetical trust violations. Apologies differed in perceived effectiveness depending on what they contained and the kind of violation. Judging a scenario differs from rebuilding trust after real harm. No apology formula obliges forgiveness or renewed access. [Lewicki, Polin & Lount (2016). An Exploration of the Structure of Effective Apologies.](https://doi.org/10.1111/ncmr.12073)

June Tangney and colleagues reviewed theory and research on moral emotions, including distinctions between evaluating one’s whole self and evaluating a particular action. Emotions have different effects across contexts. An observer cannot read shame, guilt or remorse directly from a facial expression. [Tangney, Stuewig & Mashek (2007). Moral emotions and moral behavior.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16953797/)

Jason Colquitt and colleagues combined 183 organisational-justice studies. Fair outcomes, procedures, interpersonal treatment and information were related but distinguishable aspects of perceived fairness. Most evidence concerns organisations. Disagreement about fairness is not proof of dishonesty or a single objectively correct allocation. [Colquitt and colleagues (2001). Justice at the millennium: a meta-analytic review of 25 years of organizational justice research.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11419803/)

NHS public guidance describes emotional, physical, sexual and economic abuse and routes to confidential support. Services are UK-specific. It is guidance, not a test that determines a person’s motive or an individual safety assessment. [NHS: Domestic violence and abuse](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/getting-help-for-domestic-violence/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Shared responsibility differs from cancelling one action with another. A system failure can involve several owners; a person can also be blamed unfairly for something beyond their role. Distinguish the event, available choices, agreed duties and consequences. A victim is not responsible for another person choosing abuse merely because a disagreement preceded it.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Ask who did what, what each person controlled and what would need changing to prevent recurrence. Check relevant agreements and information rather than relying on a broad accusation. Notice whether an account can include both circumstances and a concrete acknowledgment. Include exceptions where responsibility was accepted and acted on.

## How to respond

Own your part specifically and make a feasible repair. When another person redirects blame, return to the particular action or duty while allowing a separate discussion of relevant contributions. If the exchange becomes a demand that you accept responsibility for threats or harm done to you, seek support rather than negotiating over how you caused it.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: You snapped at a colleague after receiving an unclear brief. The brief may need improving, and you still own the insulting reply. Correcting the process and apologising for the reply address separate responsibilities.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Context and ownership can coexist.
- Another person’s mistake does not erase your action.
- Nobody causes another person’s chosen abuse by failing to behave perfectly.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Describe one event and each person’s actual role or choice.
2. Acknowledge or request a specific responsibility and a practical correction.
3. Review follow-through and whether the explanation kept relevant context without erasing ownership.
