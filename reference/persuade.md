# Trying to persuade

Trying to persuade means attempting to influence another person’s judgment or choice. It can involve explaining benefits, presenting evidence, appealing to values or asking for action. Persuasion is part of ordinary collaboration and selling. Its quality depends on accuracy, transparency and whether the other person retains a meaningful choice.

> **Remember:** Make reasons visible and refusal possible.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

People may be persuaded by reasons that fit their aims, trust in the source, anticipated consequences or social expectations. These routes can overlap. A well-presented argument can be wrong, while an awkwardly presented argument can be sound. Pressure can also increase resistance when it threatens choice, without making the original proposal either right or wrong.

## What the evidence supports

Christina Steindl and colleagues reviewed psychological-reactance research on responses to perceived threats to freedom. Reactance is one possible process, not an explanation established whenever someone resists a request. Real disagreement and unsuitable demands still matter. [Steindl and colleagues (2015). Understanding Psychological Reactance: New Developments and Findings.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27453805/)

Psychologist Ziva Kunda reviewed research on motivated reasoning. Desired conclusions can shape which reasons people search for and find convincing. Disagreement alone cannot establish a hidden motive, and a desired conclusion can still be correct. [Kunda (1990). The case for motivated reasoning.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2270237/)

Richard Ryan and Edward Deci reviewed self-determination theory and evidence concerning autonomy, competence and relatedness in motivation and well-being. The framework suggests distinctions to investigate. It cannot identify the cause of a particular action or refusal. [Ryan & Deci (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11392867/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Persuasion differs from informing, negotiating and coercing. Providing relevant information leaves room for a different conclusion; bargaining changes the terms; coercion can make refusal unsafe or practically unavailable. A persuasive outcome does not prove the person understood the costs or wanted the arrangement. Avoid calling an ordinary disagreement manipulation merely because someone is trying to influence you.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Check the claim, evidence, omissions, incentives and consequences of refusal. Ask what the person proposing the choice stands to gain, including yourself. Can the decision be delayed, reviewed or reversed? Compare the proposed arrangement with actual alternatives rather than an artificial choice between buying now and losing everything.

## How to respond

Present a clear proposition, relevant limitations and room for questions. Ask what matters to the other person without using a personal vulnerability as a pressure point. If you are deciding, separate the seller’s confidence from the evidence and terms. A useful conversation may end with a better-informed no.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: A client hesitates over a service. Explaining the scope, total cost and cancellation terms helps them decide. Suggesting that hesitation proves they lack ambition replaces a practical discussion with pressure on their self-image.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- An informed refusal can be a good outcome.
- Check evidence and terms independently of presentation.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Identify one choice you are proposing or considering and the relevant benefits and costs.
2. Make one missing limitation, alternative or refusal option explicit.
3. Review the quality of understanding and consent, not just whether persuasion succeeded.
