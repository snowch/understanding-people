# Assumptions in questions & decision rights

A question can ask for information while treating another claim as already settled. That background assumption is a presupposition. You can dispute it without refusing the whole conversation. A related issue is decision authority: a speaker may present a proposal as if everyone has already agreed that they can decide.

> **Remember:** Check the assumption before answering within it.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

Ordinary conversation depends on shared assumptions, so a presupposition is not inherently a trick. Trouble arises when an uncertain, disputed or false claim is smuggled into the starting point. You may answer the apparent question and accidentally leave that starting point unchallenged.

## What the evidence supports

Linguist Christopher Potts explains how language can carry background assumptions and implied meanings alongside its main assertion. Context affects how these are understood. This is a linguistics handbook draft, not a test of manipulative intent. Indirect or presupposing language is also routine communication. [Potts (2014 draft). Presupposition and Implicature.](https://web.stanford.edu/~cgpotts/manuscripts/potts-blackwellsemantics.pdf)

Melisa Stevanovic and Anssi Peräkylä analysed recorded workplace planning discussions. People could accept a plan while resisting another speaker’s assumed right to decide it. Conversation analysis of a specific workplace does not establish every family’s decision rights or validate a tactic detector. [Stevanovic & Peräkylä (2012). Deontic Authority in Interaction.](https://researchportal.helsinki.fi/en/publications/deontic-authority-in-interaction-the-right-to-announce-propose-an/)

## What is suggested without being directly said {#implied-meaning}

An implication is meaning a listener draws from words in context. “Some people make time for family” might be heard as criticism when you decline a visit, but the wording alone does not establish that intention. Ask about the specific implication if it matters, and compare what follows with the explanation.

Do not equate plausible deniability with proven deception. You can describe the practical effect of a repeated insinuation and request direct discussion, even if the speaker disputes your interpretation. Apply the same standard to hints you expect others to understand.

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

A mistaken assumption, an abbreviated question and an intentional attempt to constrain the discussion can sound similar. A person may reasonably know more about a task while lacking authority to decide another person’s commitments. Being confident or senior does not settle either issue.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Write the question as two parts: what is being assumed, and what answer is requested. Check whether the assumption is shared or evidenced. Then identify who can decide, who should be consulted and whose consent is needed. Notice whether a correction is accepted or the same disputed premise is repeatedly imposed.

## How to respond

Use a short correction and a route forward: “I have not agreed to host. Are you asking whether I can?” If the assumption is partly correct, acknowledge that part and clarify the rest. Ask for an explicit decision process where several people are affected. Avoid treating every indirect request as hostile.

**A brief fictional example:** A colleague asks, “Why are you refusing to support the launch?” You supported the launch but questioned its date. “I support the launch; I have not agreed that Friday is workable. Which deadline constraints should we compare?” corrects the premise and returns to the actual decision.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Questions can carry assumptions that need checking.
- Knowledge, expertise and the right to decide are different.
- Correct a disputed premise and identify the decision that actually remains.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Choose a confusing request and write its assumed claim separately from its requested answer. Include a harmless explanation for the wording.
2. Draft one sentence that corrects the uncertain part and one that returns to the real question or decision. Use it where appropriate.
3. Review whether the issue and decision rights became clearer, including any correction to your own reading.
