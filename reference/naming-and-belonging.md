# Names, respect & belonging

Names, titles and kinship terms do more than identify someone: they can communicate familiarity, respect or a place in a group. Naming asymmetry means using different kinds of address for people whose situations seem comparable. The difference is observable; its meaning and whether it is deliberate need separate investigation.

> **Remember:** Compare the naming rule, the preference and the response.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

<span id="what-the-full-entry-will-cover"></span>

## How it may arise

A title may follow an age convention, a person’s preference, an established family habit or a way of distinguishing two people with the same name. It may also elevate one person or mark another as less included. Several influences can operate together. A convention can preserve a hierarchy without anyone consciously planning each use.

## What the evidence supports

Roger Brown and Albert Gilman examined how address pronouns express power and solidarity, drawing on historical material and language informants. The mainly European-language material does not show that a particular family uses names deliberately to establish rank. [Brown & Gilman (1960). The Pronouns of Power and Solidarity.](https://pages.mtu.edu/~rlstrick/rsvtxt/pronoun.htm)

Carol Ember and colleagues summarise cross-cultural research on kinship terms, descent, residence and family organisation. These are related but distinct arrangements. Ethnographic group patterns do not determine one household’s practices or justify assuming values from ethnicity. [Ember, Droe & Russell (2022). Residence and Kinship.](https://hraf.yale.edu/ehc/summaries/residence-and-kinship)

## Preserving dignity without accepting a hierarchy {#face-and-dignity}

Face means the social identity or respect someone wants recognised in an interaction. Face-saving can include allowing a correction without public humiliation. It can support collaboration; it can also become pressure if preserving one person’s dignity always requires another person to remain silent.

A practical option is to correct the introduction briefly and discuss a recurring disagreement privately if safe. Privacy is an option for reducing unnecessary embarrassment, not a condition that must be met before you may object. A factual correction does not become disrespectful merely because someone dislikes it.

[Cardon & Okoro (2010). A Meta-Analysis of the Cultural Propositions about Conflict Management Styles in Face-Negotiation Theory.](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/rpcg/vol1/iss1/2/). The accessible abstract describes the review’s questions without detailed results. No country-specific prediction or claim that a face-saving technique works is drawn from it.

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Different terms need not mean different affection or rights. “Mum” expresses a relationship to the speaker that a first name may not. Equal respect does not require identical words. Conversely, calling something a tradition does not settle whether people may choose how they are addressed.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Compare who uses which exact terms, for whom, in whose presence and under which rule. Are comparable requests respected? Does the difference extend to invitations, speaking time or decisions? Include occasions that contradict the status explanation. A readily corrected habit and deliberate repeated disregard after a clear request give different information.

## How to respond

Start with the name you want used: “Please introduce me as Maya.” If appropriate, ask how the convention works rather than accusing someone of a strategy. Discuss any concrete exclusion separately. You can ask for respectful address without proving why the old wording was used; do not pressure another person to adopt a parental title they do not want.

**A brief fictional example:** At family meals, one grandmother is introduced as “Grandma Rose” and the other as “Helen”. Before inferring a ranking, the parents check preferences. Helen says she chose her name. If instead she requested “Grandma Helen” and was repeatedly ignored, the same surface difference would raise a different concern.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- A naming difference is an observation, not proof of intent.
- Check convention, preference and comparable treatment together.
- A clear request about address can be useful while the explanation remains uncertain.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Choose one naming difference, including an occasion when the apparent rule changed. Record the exact terms and each person’s stated preference, if known.
2. Where appropriate, clarify the convention or express one naming preference. Write what response would weaken your first explanation.
3. Review whether the preference was respected and whether participation or treatment changed; do not treat one correction as proof about the whole relationship.
