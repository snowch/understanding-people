# Borderline personality disorder (BPD)

Borderline personality disorder, or BPD, is a clinical term for a broader pattern of difficulties involving emotion regulation, self-image, relationships and sometimes impulsive behaviour. Experiences and severity vary. Intense feelings, a fear of losing someone or a difficult argument do not by themselves establish BPD.

## How it may arise

NIMH describes several possible risk influences, including family, biological and environmental factors. Risk factors are not individual causes: experiencing adversity does not determine that a person will develop BPD, and a diagnosis does not tell you exactly what happened in childhood. Assessment considers the person’s account, history, functioning and other possible conditions.

## What the evidence supports

NIMH public guidance describes difficulties involving emotions, self-image, impulsivity and relationships; presentations vary and effective treatments are available. Risk factors are not individual causes. A professional assessment considers the wider history and possible co-occurring or alternative conditions. [NIMH: Borderline personality disorder](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/borderline-personality-disorder)

NHS clinical information describes enduring difficulties in relating, thinking, feeling or behaving that cause distress or problems in daily life, with support and treatment options. It is general guidance; diagnosis requires professional assessment of the person, history, context and alternatives. [NHS: Personality disorder](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/personality-disorder/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Rapid emotional changes can have several explanations. A period of stress, trauma-related responses, a mood disorder, substance effects or relationship circumstances may need consideration by a professional. BPD is not a synonym for manipulation, abuse or an inability to care. Harmful behaviour should be addressed on its own terms, whatever diagnosis is or is not present.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

If a professional diagnosis exists, ask what it means for this person and what support plan they want others to know about. If it does not, record the actual difficulty and its effects rather than using relationship events as a checklist. A new, marked change deserves attention to health and circumstances, not only a personality explanation.

## How to respond

Professional treatments are available, and a clinician can discuss suitable options. In ordinary support, be clear about what you can offer, keep agreements realistic and respect the person’s agency. You can acknowledge distress while maintaining a limit on harmful conduct. Any immediate threat to life or safety needs appropriate urgent support rather than a debate about whether the distress is genuine.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: A friend becomes intensely distressed after cancelled plans. You can clarify the cancellation, offer a realistic next contact and ask what support is needed. That response does not require deciding that the distress is either BPD or a deliberate attempt to control you.

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Describe a concrete difficulty, its duration and impact without inferring a diagnosis.
2. Identify a suitable support contact or clarify an agreed support arrangement.
3. Review whether the need was addressed; use urgent local help for immediate danger.
