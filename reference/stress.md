# Stress & overload

Stress describes responses to demands or threats that strain available resources. It can affect attention, memory, decision-making, bodily arousal and interaction. A person may become more reactive, quiet, rushed or rigid, but those actions alone cannot establish stress or its cause. People’s responses and circumstances differ.

## How it may arise

A task may become harder when there are too many decisions, interruptions, uncertainties or competing responsibilities. Sleep, pain, health and support can also matter. Some demands are temporary; others arise from ongoing work or care arrangements. Describing stress should not turn a structural overload into a requirement that one person simply cope better.

## What the evidence supports

Grant Shields and colleagues combined studies of acute stress and executive functions. Stress tended to impair working memory and flexible switching; effects on inhibition were more mixed. Laboratory averages cannot explain a particular person’s sharp response or remove responsibility for harm. [Shields, Sazma & Yonelinas (2016). The effects of acute stress on core executive functions: A meta-analysis and comparison with cortisol.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27371161/)

George Bonanno and Charles Burton reviewed coping and emotion-regulation research. Their framework emphasises noticing context, having several response options and adjusting to feedback. It is a framework with acknowledged measurement limits, not evidence that one response or this worksheet always works. [Bonanno & Burton (2013). Regulatory Flexibility: An Individual Differences Perspective on Coping and Emotion Regulation.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173226/)

NIMH public guidance recommends considering distress, persistence and interference with usual activities when seeking help. US service information may not apply elsewhere; support should be adapted to local options and urgency. [NIMH: Caring for your mental health](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/topics/caring-for-your-mental-health)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

A sharp reply can also involve disagreement, habit, poor communication or deliberate hostility. Calm behaviour does not prove low stress. Stress may help explain a response while leaving responsibility for its effects intact. A persistent or sudden change in functioning may call for support or medical assessment rather than an assumption about temperament.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Ask what has changed in demands, resources and recovery. Compare difficult occasions with more manageable ones. Identify what is urgent, what can wait and which tasks require concentrated attention. If you are affected by another person’s behaviour, describe the particular action and its impact rather than trying to prove how stressed they were.

## How to respond

Reduce one unnecessary demand, clarify priorities or arrange a pause with a return point. Make support concrete and check whether it actually reduces work rather than adding coordination. If conduct is harmful, maintain relevant limits alongside support. Persistent distress, impaired functioning or urgent health changes deserve appropriate professional attention.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: During a caregiving week you repeatedly snap over routine questions. Agreeing one place for updates and asking a relative to own a specific task may reduce interruptions. You can apologise for snapping while explaining the arrangement you need next.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Stress involves demands and resources, not only personality.
- A response does not reveal its cause by itself.
- Support and accountability can proceed together.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Describe one difficult period, its demands and one response you want to handle differently.
2. Change one avoidable demand or make a specific support request.
3. Review load, recovery and effects on others; seek help if functioning remains impaired.
