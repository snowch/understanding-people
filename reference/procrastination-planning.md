# Procrastination & making a start

Procrastination usually means delaying an intended action despite expecting the delay to make things worse. It differs from a deliberate priority decision or waiting for a necessary resource. A person may value an outcome while avoiding the immediate discomfort of starting; wanting success does not settle what makes action difficult.

> **Remember:** Name the obstacle and make the first action small and specific.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

A task may feel unclear, unpleasant, risky to one’s self-image or too large to begin. Relief after postponing can make avoidance attractive again. Lack of time, sleep, materials, accessibility or skill may also be decisive. These possibilities can overlap; calling yourself lazy does not distinguish them.

## What the evidence supports

Thomas Webb and Paschal Sheeran combined 47 experimental tests in which intentions changed. Changes in behaviour were generally smaller than changes in intention. The result supports separating a promise from follow-through, not deciding why an individual failed to act. [Webb & Sheeran (2006). Does changing behavioral intentions engender behavior change? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16536643/)

Martin Hagger and Aleksandra Luszczynska reviewed action-planning interventions in health settings, including differences in results and study quality. A concrete plan may be useful, but health-intervention findings do not validate a family or workplace agreement. [Hagger & Luszczynska (2014). Implementation intention and action planning interventions in health contexts: state of the research and proposals for the way forward.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24591064/)

Sezer and colleagues compared daily with one-off planning in a week-long registered study of 297 participants. The daily group reported less bedtime procrastination. The specific comparison and short duration do not establish a durable treatment for every kind of procrastination. [Sezer and colleagues (2025). An experimental investigation of daily mental contrasting with implementation intentions and goal motives in reducing bedtime procrastination.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40219795/)

## Imagine the obstacle as well as the outcome {#contrast-and-plan}

Mental contrasting pairs a desired outcome with a present obstacle. Implementation intentions connect a cue with a response. You can use the ideas by naming a worthwhile outcome, a real obstacle and a feasible “if this happens, I will…” action. A positive image alone does not define the work.

The daily-planning study above concerned bedtime procrastination and compared daily with one-off practice over a week. It supports a bounded planning lead; it does not show that this exercise treats every cause of delay. Keep the plan changeable when circumstances provide new information.

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

A missed task can reflect disagreement, a changed goal or genuinely insufficient capacity. Persistent concentration or mood difficulties may warrant suitable support, but procrastination alone identifies no condition. A detailed plan cannot supply missing money, safety or knowledge.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Compare what happens just before postponement and what happens instead. Does a clearer task or smaller first step change the response? Identify the obstacle most likely to occur, rather than collecting every possible reason. Include whether your chosen goal is actually wanted and whether the timing is realistic.

## How to respond

Specify a cue and an action: “After breakfast, I will open the draft and write its first heading.” Then plan for one likely obstacle: “If I do not know the next section, I will write the question I need answered.” Review and adjust rather than adding punishment. Planning is an editorial application of research, not a guarantee of completing the larger goal.

**A brief fictional example:** You intend to study for an hour but keep avoiding the chapter. The actual first difficulty is not knowing which problems matter. Asking for the relevant exercise list may help more than another reminder to be motivated.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- An intention and the conditions for starting are different.
- Check a practical barrier before attributing delay to character.
- Use a realistic cue, small action, obstacle plan and review.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Choose one intended action you delayed and one occasion you started successfully. Identify a specific difference rather than writing “more willpower”.
2. Write a cue–action plan and a response to one likely obstacle. Try it at a realistic opportunity.
3. Review whether you started, what remained difficult and whether the goal or resources need changing. Completion of one step is not proof of a new lifelong habit.
