# Repeatedly asking for reassurance

Repeatedly asking for reassurance means seeking confirmation that something is safe, acceptable or not a sign of rejection. It can be a reasonable response to missing information. It becomes worth examining when the same answer brings only brief relief, the question quickly returns and time or relationships are affected.

> **Remember:** Check what a repeated answer is accomplishing.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

A person may be struggling with uncertainty, anxiety, unclear communication or an unreliable arrangement. The answer may also have been vague or inconsistent. These are alternatives to compare, not a diagnosis. Reassurance can communicate care while failing to answer the practical question or changing only discomfort for a short time.

## What the evidence supports

Sarah Shihata and colleagues reviewed what was known and unresolved about intolerance of uncertainty across emotional disorders. Uncertainty can be difficult without implying a disorder. The review does not establish what level of uncertainty another person should tolerate. [Shihata and colleagues (2016). Intolerance of uncertainty in emotional disorders: What uncertainties remain?](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27212227/)

NIMH public guidance distinguishes occasional anxiety from difficulties that persist, spread or interfere with ordinary activities. It does not make repeated reassurance or avoidance a diagnosis. Support depends on the person’s actual difficulties. [NIMH: Anxiety disorders](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/topics/anxiety-disorders)

NIMH public information describes recurring intrusive obsessions, compulsions or both, their potential impact on daily life and available treatment approaches. This is clinical guidance, not a questionnaire or a way to diagnose someone from cleanliness, checking or an unwanted thought. [NIMH: Obsessive-compulsive disorder](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/topics/obsessive-compulsive-disorder-ocd)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

A new question, a memory difficulty and repeated checking of the same settled detail are different situations. Requests related to health, safety or access can require legitimate professional clarification. OCD and anxiety disorders are separate assessment questions, not labels established by how often someone asks you something.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Clarify the exact worry, what is known and what information would change a decision. Notice how long an answer helps and whether anything new prompted the next question. Include the quality and consistency of your own answers. Ask whether the pattern interferes with tasks or contact the person wants.

## How to respond

For an ordinary uncertainty, provide a clear answer within your knowledge, acknowledge what is unknown and agree a useful next step. Be honest about your availability. If the pattern is persistent or impairing, encourage appropriate support. Do not abruptly withhold all reassurance as a home treatment, or offer certainty about matters you cannot know; follow an agreed professional plan where one exists.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: A friend repeatedly asks whether you are angry after a short message. Clarifying that you were rushing and agreeing how to flag a real concern can supply relevant information. If the same fear continues to dominate their day, another identical answer may not be enough.

<section class="key-takeaways" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="key-takeaways">

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Repeated questions can reflect missing information or a recurring relief cycle.
- Check the actual question and the effect of the answer.
- Persistent impairment deserves support rather than improvised treatment.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Record one repeated question, the answer and how long it helped.
2. Clarify a missing fact or agree an appropriate next step or support route.
3. Review useful information and functioning, without requiring complete certainty.
