# Denial, attack & blame reversal

DARVO abbreviates deny, attack, reverse victim and offender. It describes a response in which an allegation is denied, the person raising it is attacked and responsibility is reversed. Naming the sequence can help keep attention on conduct and evidence. It is not a method for determining that the original allegation is true.

> **Remember:** Keep the original conduct and any counter-complaint separate.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

This material provides general information and has not received specialist review. It is not a diagnostic tool or an individual assessment of safety. For advice about your circumstances, seek appropriately qualified support.

See [When to seek other help](../support.md) for support routes and their limits.

## How it may arise

A counter-accusation can redirect attention from an issue to the credibility or character of the person who raised it. An observer may lose track of the original question. However, someone falsely accused may also deny it and raise a legitimate concern about how they were treated. The pattern of words alone cannot settle the competing accounts.

## What the evidence supports

Sarah Harsey and Jennifer Freyd experimentally varied fictional sexual-assault accounts read by 230 undergraduates. Denial, attack and blame reversal affected judgments of the people described. This concerns observers’ judgments in a specific scenario. It is not a test of guilt, family prevalence or personality disorder. [Harsey & Freyd (2023). The Influence of Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender and Insincere Apologies on Perceptions of Sexual Assault.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37154429/)

Jason Colquitt and colleagues combined 183 organisational-justice studies. Fair outcomes, procedures, interpersonal treatment and information were related but distinguishable aspects of perceived fairness. Most evidence concerns organisations. Disagreement about fairness is not proof of dishonesty or a single objectively correct allocation. [Colquitt and colleagues (2001). Justice at the millennium: a meta-analytic review of 25 years of organizational justice research.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11419803/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

A relevant factual correction is not automatically denial in bad faith. A real counter-complaint deserves attention without erasing the first issue. A shared argument need not have one wholly innocent and one wholly responsible participant; serious coercion also should not be flattened into mutual misunderstanding.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Record the original action, evidence and request. Then record the counter-claim and its evidence separately. Does the response address the specific issue or mainly attack the speaker? What would independently support or weaken each account? Include whether you use a criticism of another person to avoid your own responsibility.

## How to respond

Where appropriate and safe, return to the specific issue: “We can discuss that concern separately; what happened to the agreed payment?” Do not demand a confession as the only acceptable outcome. For serious harm, preserve relevant records where safe and seek a suitable support or formal process. A calm tone does not prove truth and an emotional response does not disprove it.

**A brief fictional example:** When asked about missing shared funds, a partner responds that the question proves you are controlling and that they are the real victim. The payment history still needs checking. A separate concern about intrusive monitoring can also be examined; it does not by itself answer where the money went.

<section class="key-takeaways" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="key-takeaways">

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- A reversal sequence can redirect attention without proving either account.
- Separate the original conduct, credibility attacks and any genuine counter-claim.
- Use evidence and suitable support rather than a label as a verdict.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Choose one response sequence and separate the original issue from later personal attacks or counter-claims. Record what supports and challenges each.
2. Choose a safe factual clarification, boundary or appropriate support route. Avoid using the DARVO label as your evidence.
3. Review whether the conduct was addressed and whether the process respected each person’s ability to raise a legitimate concern.
