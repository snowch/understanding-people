# Minimising harm or impact

Minimising describes presenting an event or its effects as less important than another person’s account suggests. It can concern harm, workload, risk or a broken agreement. The label does not settle which account is accurate. The practical task is to clarify what happened and which consequences need addressing.

> **Remember:** Keep the actual effect in view.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

This material provides general information and has not received specialist review. It is not a diagnostic tool or an individual assessment of safety. For advice about your circumstances, seek appropriately qualified support.

See [When to seek other help](../support.md) for support routes and their limits.

## How it may arise

A person may be embarrassed, protecting a self-image, lacking information or genuinely assessing the effect differently. They may also be trying to avoid accountability. Several explanations can coexist. Your own wish to move on can make an effect seem smaller than it was for the person who bore it.

## What the evidence supports

Roy Lewicki and colleagues ran two studies of how people judged apologies after hypothetical trust violations. Apologies differed in perceived effectiveness depending on what they contained and the kind of violation. Judging a scenario differs from rebuilding trust after real harm. No apology formula obliges forgiveness or renewed access. [Lewicki, Polin & Lount (2016). An Exploration of the Structure of Effective Apologies.](https://doi.org/10.1111/ncmr.12073)

June Tangney and colleagues reviewed theory and research on moral emotions, including distinctions between evaluating one’s whole self and evaluating a particular action. Emotions have different effects across contexts. An observer cannot read shame, guilt or remorse directly from a facial expression. [Tangney, Stuewig & Mashek (2007). Moral emotions and moral behavior.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16953797/)

NHS public guidance describes emotional, physical, sexual and economic abuse and routes to confidential support. Services are UK-specific. It is guidance, not a test that determines a person’s motive or an individual safety assessment. [NHS: Domestic violence and abuse](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/getting-help-for-domestic-violence/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Different perspectives and uncertainty about severity are not automatically manipulation. An observer may need evidence to understand a claim. Conversely, saying that no harm was intended does not establish that no harm occurred, and being unable to quantify an effect does not make it irrelevant. Repeated dismissal alongside control or intimidation raises a wider concern.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Separate the event, the reported effect, the disputed interpretation and the requested response. Ask which part is being questioned. Check relevant records where available, but do not require someone to prove every feeling. Notice whether the discussion allows acknowledgment or repeatedly returns to “It was nothing” without examining the consequences.

## How to respond

If you may have minimised, ask about the specific effect and respond to the part you can establish. If affected, state what changed for you and what practical correction or limit matters. You do not need the other person to adopt an identical emotional reaction. If discussion becomes a repeated barrier to support or protection, seek another suitable route.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: A colleague says missing a deadline was “only a day,” while you had to cancel a booked service. Naming the cancellation cost makes the effect discussable without requiring a verdict about whether the colleague is generally uncaring.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- A practical consequence can matter without identical emotional reactions.
- Repeated dismissal may require a different response route.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Describe one event, its effect and the exact point being disputed.
2. Ask for or offer a specific acknowledgment, correction or relevant safeguard.
3. Review whether the consequence was addressed rather than only whether the discussion ended.
