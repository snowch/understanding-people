# Restricting contact with other people

Restricting contact with other people can reduce someone’s access to support, information and independent choices. It may involve interference with friendships, family, work or services. Choosing privacy or distance for oneself is different from another person enforcing isolation through threats, punishment or control of resources.

> **Remember:** Distinguish chosen distance from restricted contact.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

This material provides general information and has not received specialist review. It is not a diagnostic tool or an individual assessment of safety. For advice about your circumstances, seek appropriately qualified support.

## How it may arise

Restrictions may be presented as loyalty, protection, a practical inconvenience or concern about another relationship. Those explanations can be genuine, incomplete or part of a controlling pattern. The affected person may stop seeking contact to avoid consequences, so outsiders may see fewer explicit prohibitions over time.

## What the evidence supports

Home Office statutory guidance describes controlling or coercive behaviour as an unfolding pattern and discusses restrictions, threats, economic abuse and effects on everyday freedom. It addresses law and practice in England and Wales. This book does not establish whether a legal offence occurred or replace local advice. [Home Office: Controlling or coercive behaviour — statutory guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/controlling-or-coercive-behaviour-statutory-guidance-framework/controlling-or-coercive-behaviour-statutory-guidance-framework-accessible)

NHS public guidance describes emotional, physical, sexual and economic abuse and routes to confidential support. Services are UK-specific. It is guidance, not a test that determines a person’s motive or an individual safety assessment. [NHS: Domestic violence and abuse](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/getting-help-for-domestic-violence/)

Roy Baumeister and Mark Leary reviewed evidence for a widespread motivation to form and maintain stable interpersonal attachments. The review does not imply that everyone wants the same amount or form of contact, or must maintain an unsafe relationship. [Baumeister & Leary (1995). The need to belong: desire for interpersonal attachments as a fundamental human motivation.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7777651/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

A mutually chosen plan, a specific safety concern and an individual boundary need distinguishing from an imposed restriction. A person may reasonably choose not to see someone harmful. No relationship automatically entitles another adult to determine all their contacts. One missed visit does not establish isolation; a serious restriction still deserves attention without waiting for a long record.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Using existing information where safe, notice which contacts have become difficult, who controls practical access and what happens after contact or disagreement. Include transport, money, communication tools and care needs. Ask the affected person privately only when the circumstances allow it; do not assume a message or device is confidential.

## How to respond

Seek suitable support if contact restrictions may be part of coercion or danger. If supporting another person, offer a dependable, nonjudgmental route without demanding that they confront someone or leave on your timetable. For an ordinary disagreement about plans, clarify the specific concern and preserve meaningful choice rather than imposing a loyalty test.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: A partner repeatedly creates a crisis before visits to friends and later threatens loss of access to money if visits continue. The pattern concerns practical restriction and consequences. It is not resolved by asking whether the partner says they feel lonely.

<section class="key-takeaways" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="key-takeaways">

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Chosen solitude and imposed isolation are different.
- Look at practical access and consequences, not only explicit bans.
- Support should avoid adding pressure or exposing private contact.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. If safe, describe an existing restriction and its effect on access to people or services.
2. Identify a confidential support option or a safe way to maintain contact.
3. Review whether support and choice increased without testing the restriction through confrontation.
