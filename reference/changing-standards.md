# Changing criteria & moving goalposts

Moving the goalposts is an everyday phrase for changing the standard after someone has met, or started meeting, the earlier one. A change can make progress or agreement difficult to evaluate. It is a description to investigate, not an established diagnosis of the person making the change.

> **Remember:** Name the agreed standard and the reason for changing it.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

<span id="what-the-full-entry-will-cover"></span>

## How it may arise

Requirements may change because new information appears, a risk becomes clear or the first agreement was incomplete. They may also shift because someone avoids admitting agreement or seeks more work without renegotiation. You may contribute by assuming a shared definition of “finished” that was never checked.

## What the evidence supports

Martin Hagger and Aleksandra Luszczynska reviewed action-planning interventions in health settings, including differences in results and study quality. A concrete plan may be useful, but health-intervention findings do not validate a family or workplace agreement. [Hagger & Luszczynska (2014). Implementation intention and action planning interventions in health contexts: state of the research and proposals for the way forward.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24591064/)

Jason Colquitt and colleagues combined 183 organisational-justice studies. Fair outcomes, procedures, interpersonal treatment and information were related but distinguishable aspects of perceived fairness. Most evidence concerns organisations. Disagreement about fairness is not proof of dishonesty or a single objectively correct allocation. [Colquitt and colleagues (2001). Justice at the millennium: a meta-analytic review of 25 years of organizational justice research.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11419803/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Learning something new can justify a revision. Correcting an unsafe plan differs from adding an unrelated condition whenever the earlier one is satisfied. Scope creep may be unplanned rather than strategic. An unfair change can still require action while intent remains uncertain.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Write the original criterion, what was done, the changed criterion and the reason given. Was the change relevant, proportionate and communicated when the information became available? Compare whether revisions apply consistently and whether either person can renegotiate cost or timing. Include evidence that the original understanding was ambiguous.

## How to respond

Confirm an operational standard and how changes will be handled: “This review covers these three requirements; a new request needs a revised date.” Allow necessary corrections without making every preference an urgent requirement. For recurring unfair changes, document decisions and consider an appropriate escalation or boundary rather than chasing an indefinitely moving target.

**A brief fictional example:** A client accepts a page layout, then asks for a new section. The designer asks whether this replaces earlier work or changes the scope and deadline. A genuine new need can be accommodated through a new agreement; it need not be labelled manipulation.

<section class="key-takeaways" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="key-takeaways">

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- A changed standard can reflect new information, ambiguity or unfair pressure.
- Compare the original terms, timing and stated reason for revision.
- Use a clear change process and a feasible definition of completion.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Choose one changed requirement and record the original terms, later change and relevant new information. Identify where the first agreement was unclear.
2. Propose one concrete completion standard and a way to renegotiate future changes.
3. Review whether revisions became clearer and costs were shared fairly. Note any evidence that changes your view of why the pattern occurred.
