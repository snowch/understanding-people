# Who else is present

Who else is present can change what people say, notice or feel able to do. Public and private exchanges may differ because of role expectations, privacy, recognition, fear of consequences or the need to coordinate with others. A difference is useful evidence about context, but does not by itself prove insincerity.

> **Remember:** Compare the setting without assuming a performance.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

An audience may make a comment feel like public criticism or a request feel harder to refuse. A person may also learn new information during the group discussion. You can contribute to the problem by presenting private encouragement as a public commitment that was never agreed.

## What the evidence supports

Cameron Anderson and colleagues reviewed evidence on the desire for respect, admiration and voluntary deference, distinguishing status from power, money and belonging. A broad motive does not explain every interruption, disagreement or wish for recognition. [Anderson, Hildreth & Howland (2015). Is the desire for status a fundamental human motive? A review of the empirical literature.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25774679/)

Dacher Keltner, Deborah Gruenfeld and Cameron Anderson reviewed theory and evidence connecting unequal power with opportunities, threats and behavioural tendencies. This is a broad framework, not a universal rule about every powerful or less powerful person. [Keltner D, Gruenfeld DH, Anderson C. (2003). Power, approach, and inhibition.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12747524/)

Tal Eyal and colleagues tested interpersonal predictions across a series of experiments. Imagining another person’s perspective did not consistently improve accuracy; a final experiment found that obtaining information through conversation helped. Conversation does not guarantee honesty, complete understanding or safety. These were specific prediction tasks, not tests of every relationship. [Eyal, Steffel & Epley (2018). Perspective mistaking: Accurately understanding the mind of another requires getting perspective, not taking perspective.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29620401/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

A public–private difference can reflect deception or a strategic choice, but it can also reflect uncertainty, changed circumstances or different meanings of support. Consistency is not automatically truth, and inconsistency is not automatically bad faith. Compare the actual questions, information and commitments in each setting.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Write what was said in each context, who was present and what changed between them. Ask about the specific difference rather than beginning with a character accusation. Notice decision authority and the consequences of disagreement. Include whether your own wording or expectations changed when others were watching.

## How to respond

Before a consequential meeting, clarify what each person is prepared to support publicly and what concerns remain. Use a private or written route for sensitive information where appropriate. If repeated public undermining affects you, address the conduct and agree how decisions will be recorded. Do not seek a confession of an inner strategy as a prerequisite for protecting your work.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: A colleague praises an idea privately but raises concerns at the meeting. Asking which part they supported and what changed separates encouragement, readiness and public recommendation. The answer can then be checked against the discussion and later actions.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Audience can alter both freedom and meaning.
- A contextual difference does not settle motive.
- Clarify public commitments before relying on private encouragement.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Compare one public and private exchange, including information and wording.
2. Ask a specific clarification or agree how a future commitment will be recorded.
3. Review whether the distinction explained the difference and whether the arrangement became more dependable.
