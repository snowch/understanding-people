# Access, participation & gatekeeping

Gatekeeping describes influencing another person’s access to information, participation, people or resources. It can facilitate involvement or restrict it. A person coordinating care or protecting privacy may have a legitimate role; the same broad word can also describe exclusion or control. State the actual access decision before interpreting it.

> **Remember:** Identify the gate, the authority and the effect.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

Someone may control access because they have an agreed responsibility, hold key information or have become the default organiser. They may be trying to manage risk, avoid conflict, preserve a role or exclude another participant. The literature on parental gatekeeping has a narrower scope than every everyday use of the word.

## What the evidence supports

Reis and colleagues reviewed 16 studies of parental gatekeeping and parenting. They describe complex, bidirectional relationships and disagreement about concepts and measurement. The literature largely concerns coparenting and father involvement. It does not directly establish a grandmother’s or in-law’s strategy. [Reis and colleagues (2025). Relations Between Parental Gatekeeping and Parenting in Families With Children: A Scoping Review.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40439085/)

Jason Carroll and colleagues reviewed 37 studies across settings including divorce, stepfamilies, illness and caregiving. Boundary ambiguity concerns uncertainty about family membership or roles. This differs from deciding a personal limit. The review does not supply a checklist for assigning family roles or blame. [Carroll, Olson & Buckmiller (2007). Family Boundary Ambiguity: A 30-Year Review.](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/4337/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Privacy and safeguarding can justify limits; nobody is owed another adult’s medical information or unrestricted contact. A child’s needs and legal arrangements can constrain access. Conversely, being the organiser does not automatically confer authority to decide everything. Coparenting studies cannot establish a grandmother’s or in-law’s strategy.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Specify what access was requested, whose consent matters and who can grant it. Is the rule explained and consistent? Are there practical alternatives? Is information withheld selectively, or does the organiser also lack it? Include whether your own request assumes a right that has not been agreed.

## How to respond

Clarify the role and seek the least intrusive route that meets the actual need. An agreed update process can reduce reliance on one messenger where appropriate. Respect privacy and legitimate refusals. Where access is restricted through threats, financial control or coercion, consider qualified support rather than trying to bypass the person covertly.

**A brief fictional example:** One sibling receives all care updates and others feel excluded. The parent wants some details private but welcomes a shared schedule. Agreeing to share the schedule with consent solves a coordination problem without treating all medical information as a family entitlement.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Gatekeeping can facilitate involvement, manage legitimate limits or exclude.
- Consent and actual decision authority matter.
- Describe the access and its effect before assigning a strategy.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Choose one access decision and record what was requested, whose consent was needed and the reason given. Identify a legitimate limit as well as a concerning possibility.
2. Ask for one clear role or agreed information route that respects the relevant person’s wishes.
3. Review whether participation improved without loss of privacy or choice. Record unresolved concerns separately.
