# Open-mindedness / openness

Open-mindedness, often called openness to experience, concerns tendencies toward intellectual curiosity, aesthetic engagement and imagination. It does not mean agreeing with every unfamiliar idea. Someone may be adventurous in art and cautious in finances; another may enjoy abstract questions while preferring a familiar daily routine.

> **Remember:** Curiosity is compatible with checking evidence.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

Interest in novelty interacts with expertise, opportunity, perceived risk and the setting. Familiarity can make an option easier to evaluate; genuine constraints can make experimentation costly. Trait descriptions summarise these patterns across occasions, while leaving room for someone to behave differently when a decision matters or circumstances change.

## What the evidence supports

Christopher Soto and Oliver John developed and tested the BFI-2 across three studies, organising personality descriptions into five broad dimensions and narrower facets. These are dimensions, not fixed types or clinical diagnoses. This entry does not reproduce, administer or score the questionnaire. [Soto & John (2017). The next Big Five Inventory (BFI-2): Developing and assessing a hierarchical model with 15 facets to enhance bandwidth, fidelity, and predictive power.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27055049/)

William Fleeson used repeated reports of everyday behaviour in three studies. Individuals varied considerably across occasions while showing stable differences in their average patterns. A few memorable events cannot establish a lifelong trait; self-reports and the sampled settings have limits. [Fleeson (2001). Toward a structure- and process-integrated view of personality: traits as density distribution of states.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11414368/)

Psychologist Ziva Kunda reviewed research on motivated reasoning. Desired conclusions can shape which reasons people search for and find convincing. Disagreement alone cannot establish a hidden motive, and a desired conclusion can still be correct. [Kunda (1990). The case for motivated reasoning.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2270237/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Rejecting a proposal can reflect evidence, timing, expense or a conflicting aim rather than a closed mind. Accepting an unusual claim can reflect careful inquiry or uncritical enthusiasm. Neither tradition nor novelty guarantees quality. Openness is also distinct from political identity, intelligence and moral superiority.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Ask whether the person can identify what they would need to learn or see before changing a view. Apply that question to yourself. Compare responses to proposals of similar risk rather than equating enthusiasm for a low-cost hobby with willingness to make a major life change. Notice whether you dismiss an idea because of who proposed it.

## How to respond

Separate exploration from commitment. A small, reversible trial can answer a practical question while preserving limits on cost and responsibility. Invite reasons for caution as well as arguments for change. If the answer is no, clarify the decision criteria rather than accusing the person of a defective personality. You can develop a habit of inquiry without making novelty compulsory.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: A parent declines a new budgeting app because it requires sharing banking data, but agrees to compare spending categories on paper. The refusal concerns the arrangement, not necessarily a general unwillingness to learn. The revised comparison can still serve the shared goal.

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Choose one idea you dismissed and state the actual reason.
2. Identify a fair question or bounded trial that could change your assessment.
3. Review what you learned without treating acceptance of the idea as the required result.
