# Changing roles & life transitions

Life transitions change roles, routines, resources or relationships. Examples include moving home, starting work, retirement, separation, parenthood and changes in care needs. A person’s behaviour during a transition may reflect a changing situation rather than a newly revealed fixed character.

> **Remember:** Update the arrangement as well as the expectations.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

<span id="what-the-full-entry-will-cover"></span>

## How it may arise

A transition can remove familiar cues and support while adding new decisions and work. It can also affect identity, belonging and a sense of competence. People may adapt at different rates or want different forms of involvement. Those possibilities should be explored rather than used as a complete explanation for every difficult response.

## What the evidence supports

Richard Ryan and Edward Deci reviewed self-determination theory and evidence concerning autonomy, competence and relatedness in motivation and well-being. The framework suggests distinctions to investigate. It cannot identify the cause of a particular action or refusal. [Ryan & Deci (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11392867/)

Wendy Wood and Dennis Rünger reviewed habit research. Repetition in recurring contexts can make responses more automatic, alongside deliberate goals. Not every repeated action is a habit. A pattern may also reflect ongoing constraints, decisions or coercion. [Wood & Rünger (2016). Psychology of Habit.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26361052/)

George Bonanno and Charles Burton reviewed coping and emotion-regulation research. Their framework emphasises noticing context, having several response options and adjusting to feedback. It is a framework with acknowledged measurement limits, not evidence that one response or this worksheet always works. [Bonanno & Burton (2013). Regulatory Flexibility: An Individual Differences Perspective on Coping and Emotion Regulation.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173226/)

William Fleeson used repeated reports of everyday behaviour in three studies. Individuals varied considerably across occasions while showing stable differences in their average patterns. A few memorable events cannot establish a lifelong trait; self-reports and the sampled settings have limits. [Fleeson (2001). Toward a structure- and process-integrated view of personality: traits as density distribution of states.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11414368/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Adjustment, grief, practical overload, a health change and an ongoing relationship problem can coexist. A transition does not make all harmful conduct temporary or acceptable. It can also be positive without being easy. Avoid imposing a standard timetable for feeling settled or assuming that the same event means the same thing to everyone.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Ask what changed in actual tasks, decision rights, money, support and daily rhythm. Compare expectations before and after the transition. Identify responsibilities that became unowned or continued by default. Notice whether your interpretation overlooks the person’s earlier baseline or the work they are now doing.

## How to respond

Renegotiate one concrete arrangement rather than asking everyone to return to the old normal. Preserve useful support, clarify new responsibilities and plan a review after some experience. Make room for mixed feelings without requiring them to resolve before practical help begins. Seek suitable support when distress or functional difficulties persist or a health change is marked.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: After retirement, one partner starts reorganising the household. They may want a meaningful contribution, while the other experiences unwanted control. Discussing which tasks and decisions will be shared addresses the new arrangement without assuming either motive is settled.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Role changes alter conditions as well as feelings.
- A transition can be welcome and demanding at once.
- Explicitly renegotiate work, choice and support.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. List one transition and the responsibilities or resources that changed.
2. Agree one updated arrangement and a realistic review point.
3. Review workload, choice and adjustment, including whether further support is needed.
