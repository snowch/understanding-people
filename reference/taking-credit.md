# Taking or withholding credit

Taking or withholding credit concerns how contributions are represented. It can involve presenting shared work as one person’s achievement, omitting a contributor or assigning responsibility inaccurately. It affects recognition and sometimes opportunities or pay. An error in attribution and a deliberate appropriation require different evidence.

> **Remember:** Trace the contribution before judging character.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

Roles may be unclear, a summary may be careless or someone may be seeking recognition or advantage. People can also overestimate their own visible contribution while overlooking coordination and support. These are possible explanations, not an excuse to leave attribution inaccurate.

## What the evidence supports

Cameron Anderson and colleagues reviewed evidence on the desire for respect, admiration and voluntary deference, distinguishing status from power, money and belonging. A broad motive does not explain every interruption, disagreement or wish for recognition. [Anderson, Hildreth & Howland (2015). Is the desire for status a fundamental human motive? A review of the empirical literature.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25774679/)

Jason Colquitt and colleagues combined 183 organisational-justice studies. Fair outcomes, procedures, interpersonal treatment and information were related but distinguishable aspects of perceived fairness. Most evidence concerns organisations. Disagreement about fairness is not proof of dishonesty or a single objectively correct allocation. [Colquitt and colleagues (2001). Justice at the millennium: a meta-analytic review of 25 years of organizational justice research.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11419803/)

Russell Cropanzano and Marie Mitchell reviewed social-exchange theory in organisational research, including different exchange rules, resources and relationships. The review also identifies conceptual ambiguity. Relationships cannot be reduced to a universal ledger of favours. [Cropanzano & Mitchell (2005). Social Exchange Theory: An Interdisciplinary Review.](https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206305279602)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Leadership, sponsorship, authorship and doing a task are different contributions. Credit need not be distributed identically to be accurate. A disagreement about contribution is not automatically narcissism or jealousy. At the same time, a polished public account does not override records of who did the work.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Identify the specific representation, the actual contributions and the standard relevant to the setting. Use records you can legitimately access, including prior agreements about authorship or acknowledgment. Notice whose less visible work is omitted repeatedly and what happens when a correction is requested. Examine your own summaries of shared success.

## How to respond

Request a precise correction and clarify future attribution before the next presentation or handover. Give credit where the relevant audience can see it, without exposing unnecessary private details. If the pattern affects employment or formal rights, use suitable procedures or advice. You can protect a contribution without proving the other person’s personality or hidden intent.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: A manager introduces a team analysis as their own work. A follow-up note identifying the team’s specific contributions and requesting correction addresses the attribution. Whether the omission was intentional needs more than the omission alone.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Credit should reflect actual contributions and applicable standards.
- An attribution error does not reveal its motive by itself.
- Correct the record and clarify future expectations.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Write one inaccurate attribution and the evidence for each contribution.
2. Request or make a specific correction through an appropriate route.
3. Review the corrected record and whether future agreements reduce repeated omissions.
