# Histrionic personality disorder

Histrionic personality disorder is a clinical term involving a persistent pattern of attention-seeking and emotionally expressive behaviour that is considered in relation to functioning and the person’s wider life. It should not be inferred from clothing, flirtation, an expressive communication style or someone describing a person as dramatic.

## How it may arise

The causes of an individual’s difficulties cannot be reconstructed from appearance or a social encounter. People may seek attention for many reasons, including connection, distress, practical help or enjoyment of performance. A clinician examines the broader pattern and alternatives, rather than turning an observer’s dislike into a diagnosis.

## What the evidence supports

The MSD Manual’s public clinical overview explains this condition in the context of an enduring pattern, effects on functioning and professional assessment. This is explanatory clinical guidance, not a checklist to apply to relatives. The short examples and response suggestions here are editorial, not diagnostic criteria. [MSD Manual: Histrionic personality disorder](https://www.msdmanuals.com/home/mental-health-disorders/personality-disorders/histrionic-personality-disorder)

NHS clinical information describes enduring difficulties in relating, thinking, feeling or behaving that cause distress or problems in daily life, with support and treatment options. It is general guidance; diagnosis requires professional assessment of the person, history, context and alternatives. [NHS: Personality disorder](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/personality-disorder/)

Psychologists Daniel Gilbert and Patrick Malone reviewed research on correspondence bias: people can underweight circumstances when explaining another person’s behaviour. This does not establish that a particular judgment about someone’s character is mistaken. [Gilbert & Malone (1995). The correspondence bias.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7870861/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Expressiveness can be a cultural norm, a professional skill or an ordinary preference. A person who communicates distress intensely may still have a legitimate need. Gendered judgments about emotion and appearance can bias interpretation. Clinical vocabulary is not a reliable way to decide that someone’s feelings are shallow, false or intended to manipulate.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

If conduct affects you, identify what actually happened: an agreement interrupted, an unwanted interaction, a request for attention or a missed responsibility. Ask about the practical issue without inferring an inner life from presentation. If a diagnosis has been reported, keep that information separate from what you conclude about this occasion.

## How to respond

For persistent personal difficulties, professional assessment can clarify needs and support options. In ordinary interaction, make respectful requests about conduct and your availability without policing appearance or emotional style. A boundary can be relevant even when the other person’s feelings are genuine. Neither attention nor withholding attention should be used as an improvised treatment.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: A colleague speaks animatedly and enjoys presenting. That does not establish a disorder. If they repeatedly interrupt a shared meeting, address turn-taking and the meeting’s purpose rather than using a clinical label to criticise their style.
