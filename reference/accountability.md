# Taking responsibility & making corrections

Accountability means recognising responsibility for an action or commitment, addressing its consequences and following through on a reasonable correction. It concerns conduct, not a declaration that someone is wholly good or bad. An explanation can clarify circumstances while leaving responsibility intact.

> **Remember:** Name the action, repair the effect, change the arrangement.

## How it may arise

People may defend against a global accusation because it feels humiliating or inaccurate. That can make a specific discussion harder. The person raising the issue can also demand remorse in a particular emotional form rather than identifying the needed correction. Neither difficulty makes an apology unnecessary or excuses repeated harm.

## What the evidence supports

Roy Lewicki and colleagues ran two studies of how people judged apologies after hypothetical trust violations. Apologies differed in perceived effectiveness depending on what they contained and the kind of violation. Judging a scenario differs from rebuilding trust after real harm. No apology formula obliges forgiveness or renewed access. [Lewicki, Polin & Lount (2016). An Exploration of the Structure of Effective Apologies.](https://doi.org/10.1111/ncmr.12073)

June Tangney and colleagues reviewed theory and research on moral emotions, including distinctions between evaluating one’s whole self and evaluating a particular action. Emotions have different effects across contexts. An observer cannot read shame, guilt or remorse directly from a facial expression. [Tangney, Stuewig & Mashek (2007). Moral emotions and moral behavior.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16953797/)

Thomas Webb and Paschal Sheeran combined 47 experimental tests in which intentions changed. Changes in behaviour were generally smaller than changes in intention. The result supports separating a promise from follow-through, not deciding why an individual failed to act. [Webb & Sheeran (2006). Does changing behavioral intentions engender behavior change? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16536643/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

An apology, an explanation, restitution and sustained change are different parts of responding. A convincing apology does not prove future reliability, and a quiet or awkward apology does not prove lack of concern. Shared responsibility is possible, but each person’s action should remain visible rather than being cancelled by the other’s mistake.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Identify what was done or promised, the effects, which facts are agreed and who can change what. Notice whether the response names the action specifically or shifts to unrelated grievances. Check subsequent conduct and practical repair rather than trying to read sincerity from tears, confidence or the exact words used.

## How to respond

If responsible, acknowledge the action accurately, hear its effect and make a feasible repair or prevention plan. Avoid promising unlimited change to end an uncomfortable conversation. If affected, state what correction matters and what limits you need while trust is uncertain. You can recognise an apology without granting forgiveness, renewed access or the same arrangement.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: You missed an agreed school pickup. “I was busy” explains part of the context but leaves the effect untouched. Contacting the affected people, arranging the immediate solution and changing the calendar handover addresses the actual responsibility.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Explanation and responsibility can coexist.
- An apology and sustained repair are different evidence.
- Accepting acknowledgment does not require restoring trust immediately.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Name one action you own and its concrete effect on another person.
2. Offer a specific correction or prevention step you can carry out.
3. Review follow-through and the affected person’s feedback without demanding forgiveness.
