# Capability, opportunity & motivation

Capability, opportunity and motivation are three useful questions about an action: can the person do it, do the circumstances allow it, and what directs them toward or away from it? Together they form COM-B, a behaviour-change framework. The questions interact rather than divide people into fixed categories.

> **Remember:** Find the missing condition before adding pressure.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

A person may know how to do a task but lack the time or equipment. They may have access and still find the activity threatening, unrewarding or inconsistent with another goal. Changing one condition can alter the others: a clearer instruction can make a task feel more achievable, while a crowded setting can make a familiar skill difficult to use.

## What the evidence supports

Susan Michie and colleagues reviewed behaviour-change frameworks and proposed COM-B: capability, opportunity and motivation as interacting conditions for behaviour. They examined its use in tobacco-control and obesity policy. It is a framework for analysing and designing change, not a diagnostic test or proof that one identified barrier caused an action. [Michie, van Stralen & West (2011). The behaviour change wheel: a new method for characterising and designing behaviour change interventions.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21513547/)

Thomas Webb and Paschal Sheeran combined 47 experimental tests in which intentions changed. Changes in behaviour were generally smaller than changes in intention. The result supports separating a promise from follow-through, not deciding why an individual failed to act. [Webb & Sheeran (2006). Does changing behavioral intentions engender behavior change? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16536643/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

COM-B is not a test that proves which cause explains one action. “They need motivation” can become a circular explanation if it simply means “They did not act.” Real disagreement, refusal of an unwanted goal and harmful external constraints must remain visible. Do not use a behaviour-change framework to make another person comply with a goal they have not chosen.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Define the action narrowly: who would do what, where and when? Ask about physical and psychological skills, practical and social conditions, and competing reasons or automatic responses. Seek a concrete example of the barrier. Compare an occasion when the same action was easier, including what changed in the environment.

## How to respond

Choose a small change matched to the evidence: demonstrate a skill, remove an access barrier, simplify a step, agree a cue or reconsider the goal. Change one condition where feasible so you can learn from the result. Include the person in the design. If a responsibility is shared, negotiate a workable contribution rather than silently redesigning their behaviour.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: A volunteer misses an online form. The problem is that the form cannot be completed on their phone. Repeating reminders addresses neither access nor usability. Providing an accessible route tests a specific explanation without declaring the volunteer uncommitted.

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Name one action precisely and one suspected missing condition.
2. Agree a small change to that condition or ask for a concrete explanation.
3. Review whether the action became feasible and whether the goal still makes sense.
