# Social comparison & envy

Social comparison means evaluating something about yourself by reference to someone else. Envy concerns an advantage another person has that you would like. Jealousy usually concerns a threatened loss of a valued relationship or position. These experiences can overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

> **Remember:** Ask what the comparison means and what action it invites.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

A comparison may provide useful information, an attainable model or a painful judgment about your worth. Its meaning depends on what matters to you, whether the comparison is fair and whether the advantage seems reachable. Online information may show selected outcomes while hiding resources, effort and setbacks.

## What the evidence supports

A 2022 critical review examined social comparison, envy and well-being on social media. The evidence included mixed, conditional and person-dependent findings. Comparison is not uniformly harmful, and findings about social media cannot explain every offline comparison. [Social comparison and envy on social media: A critical review (2022).](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35184028/)

Cameron Anderson and colleagues reviewed evidence on the desire for respect, admiration and voluntary deference, distinguishing status from power, money and belonging. A broad motive does not explain every interruption, disagreement or wish for recognition. [Anderson, Hildreth & Howland (2015). Is the desire for status a fundamental human motive? A review of the empirical literature.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25774679/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Criticism of an unfair allocation can be justified; it is not dismissed by calling it envy. Admiration need not conceal resentment. Someone else’s success may highlight a real unmet need, a chosen goal or a comparison that does not fit your circumstances.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Identify the exact comparison and what you inferred: “They earned more” differs from “I am a failure.” What conditions are known and which are missing? Compare your response across occasions: motivation, avoidance, hostility or useful learning. Include positive or neutral comparisons instead of collecting only painful ones.

## How to respond

Name a desired outcome you can actually pursue, or decide the comparison is not useful. Seek information about a process rather than comparing a whole life with a selected result. Appreciate another person without pretending you feel no disappointment. Address an unfair arrangement directly rather than attacking the person who benefited.

**A brief fictional example:** A friend publishes a book and you feel behind. You do not know their time, support or goals. Asking about a writing routine may supply a useful model; concluding you lack talent from their publication date adds a claim the comparison cannot establish.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Comparison can inform, inspire or distress; its effects vary.
- Envy, jealousy and a legitimate fairness concern are different.
- Translate a useful comparison into a chosen action, or stop using an unsuitable standard.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Record one comparison, the known facts and the judgment you added about yourself or another person. Identify one missing difference in circumstances.
2. Choose a useful process to learn from or change a comparison habit that repeatedly disrupts a goal. State what you want the change to improve.
3. Review mood, action and fairness concerns separately. Do not assume that feeling better means the original concern was false.
