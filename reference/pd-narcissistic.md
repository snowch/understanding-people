# Narcissistic personality disorder (NPD)

Narcissistic personality disorder is a clinical term involving persistent difficulties around an inflated self-view, reliance on admiration and how other people’s needs are recognised, considered within the person’s functioning and history. It is more specific than everyday uses of narcissist. A confident, ambitious, selfish or inconsiderate act does not establish the disorder.

> **Remember:** Distinguish entitlement, a trait and a diagnosis.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

This material provides general information and has not received specialist review. It is not a diagnostic tool or an individual assessment of safety. For advice about your circumstances, seek appropriately qualified support.

## How it may arise

Public clinical accounts describe possible contributions from inherited tendencies and developmental experience, while the explanation of an individual remains uncertain. Do not infer a particular childhood or parenting history from current behaviour. A clinician explores the wider pattern and alternative explanations rather than deciding from a person’s charm, social success or a conflict with you.

## What the evidence supports

The MSD Manual’s public clinical overview explains this condition in the context of an enduring pattern, effects on functioning and professional assessment. This is explanatory clinical guidance, not a checklist to apply to relatives. The short examples and response suggestions here are editorial, not diagnostic criteria. [MSD Manual: Narcissistic personality disorder](https://www.msdmanuals.com/home/mental-health-disorders/personality-disorders/narcissistic-personality-disorder)

NHS clinical information describes enduring difficulties in relating, thinking, feeling or behaving that cause distress or problems in daily life, with support and treatment options. It is general guidance; diagnosis requires professional assessment of the person, history, context and alternatives. [NHS: Personality disorder](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/personality-disorder/)

Cameron Anderson and colleagues reviewed evidence on the desire for respect, admiration and voluntary deference, distinguishing status from power, money and belonging. A broad motive does not explain every interruption, disagreement or wish for recognition. [Anderson, Hildreth & Howland (2015). Is the desire for status a fundamental human motive? A review of the empirical literature.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25774679/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Narcissistic traits, entitlement, desire for status and NPD are related but different ideas. Abusive conduct can occur without NPD, and a diagnosis is not proof that someone is abusive. Popular categories such as covert narcissist may combine descriptions, theories and informal labels; they are not conclusions this reference can assign from your account.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

For a practical concern, identify the conduct: whose needs were dismissed, what agreement was broken, what happened after feedback and whether exceptions occur. Keep information about the person’s diagnosis separate from your interpretation. Avoid turning every ambiguous action into another point for a preselected type.

## How to respond

If the question concerns your own persistent difficulties with self-worth or relationships, a qualified clinician can help assess them and discuss support. If someone else’s conduct affects you, use specific requests, realistic agreements and limits suited to the circumstances. You do not need diagnostic certainty to respond to exploitation or seek support, and no communication technique guarantees that another person will change.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: A colleague takes credit for your work. Correcting attribution and documenting contributions addresses the event. Calling them a narcissist adds a clinical-sounding explanation that the event cannot establish and may distract from the remedy you need.
