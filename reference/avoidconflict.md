# Trying to avoid conflict

Trying to avoid conflict is a possible intention to prevent disagreement, escalation, rejection or an unpleasant exchange. It may lead to silence, quick agreement, delay or changing the subject. None of those actions proves the intention. Avoiding a dangerous confrontation can be protective; avoiding every ordinary difference can leave problems unresolved.

> **Remember:** Ask what postponement is protecting—and costing.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

An immediate reduction in discomfort can make postponement attractive even when a later cost grows. A person may also be tired, lack words, need privacy or reasonably expect hostility. The important distinction is whether the action protects something necessary or prevents a manageable issue from being addressed. You need context and the person’s own account to investigate that.

## What the evidence supports

Stefan Hofmann and Angela Hay reviewed avoidance in anxiety treatment, including circumstances in which avoidance can be adaptive. Function and actual danger matter. This is not a reason to remove someone’s safety arrangements or conduct exposure treatment yourself. [Hofmann & Hay (2018). Rethinking avoidance: Toward a balanced approach to avoidance in treating anxiety disorders.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29550689/)

George Bonanno and Charles Burton reviewed coping and emotion-regulation research. Their framework emphasises noticing context, having several response options and adjusting to feedback. It is a framework with acknowledged measurement limits, not evidence that one response or this worksheet always works. [Bonanno & Burton (2013). Regulatory Flexibility: An Individual Differences Perspective on Coping and Emotion Regulation.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173226/)

NHS public guidance describes emotional, physical, sexual and economic abuse and routes to confidential support. Services are UK-specific. It is guidance, not a test that determines a person’s motive or an individual safety assessment. [NHS: Domestic violence and abuse](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/getting-help-for-domestic-violence/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

A pause is different from refusing all future discussion. Disagreement is different from intimidation. Some apparent avoidance reflects a decision that an argument is unproductive, not fear. Do not make “willingness to confront” the standard for honesty or courage when power, safety or access to support differs.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Ask whether there is a specific return point, a preferred way to discuss the issue and an explanation of what makes the conversation difficult. Notice what happens when a lower-pressure option is offered. Include your own part: do you ask for discussion while interrupting, belittling or making refusal costly?

## How to respond

For an ordinary shared problem, propose one concrete issue, a suitable time and a clear purpose. Allow preparation or a written route. Agree what happens if more time is needed. Where threats or retaliation are plausible, prioritise support over proving that direct discussion is possible. You can also set a practical limit when a shared responsibility remains unaddressed.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: A partner says, “Not now,” during a rushed morning. Offering a time that evening distinguishes a need for a pause from an indefinite refusal to discuss the bill. Neither response alone establishes a complete motive.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Avoidance may protect safety or postpone a manageable problem.
- A return arrangement distinguishes a pause from indefinite delay.
- Check how your own conduct affects the conversation.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Describe one postponed issue and what might make discussing it difficult.
2. If appropriate, propose a bounded conversation or another route with a return point.
3. Review whether the issue became more workable; use support rather than escalation if the response is threatening.
