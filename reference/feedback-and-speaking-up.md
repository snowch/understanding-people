# Feedback & speaking up

Feedback gives information about an action or result that can guide a next attempt. Psychological safety concerns whether people expect interpersonal risks—such as admitting uncertainty, asking a question or reporting a mistake—to be met without punishment or humiliation. It is different from comfort, universal agreement or freedom from accountability.

> **Remember:** Make the issue specific and the response to concerns reliable.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

A person may hear a task correction as a judgment of their worth, especially when it is vague, public or tied to status. Teams also learn from what happens after someone raises a concern. A warm invitation to speak is weak evidence if earlier reports led to ridicule, exclusion or retaliation.

## What the evidence supports

Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi combined feedback experiments. Performance improved on average, but more than a third of the interventions reduced it. Their account emphasises where feedback directs attention. No fixed wording or praise formula follows from the average result; task and context matter. [Kluger & DeNisi (1996). The effects of feedback interventions on performance.](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-effects-of-feedback-interventions-on-performance-a-historical/)

Lance Frazier and colleagues combined 136 workplace samples to examine psychological safety and its connections with leadership, work behaviour and performance. The evidence does not guarantee that speaking up is safe in a particular organisation or that one invitation will change its consequences. [Frazier and colleagues (2017). Psychological Safety: A Meta-Analytic Review and Extension.](https://digitalcommons.odu.edu/management_fac_pubs/13/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Defending against an inaccurate criticism is not automatically defensiveness. A quiet employee may have no concern, need time to think or reasonably fear consequences. Frequent praise is not evidence of safety, and a disagreement does not by itself establish an unsafe climate.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Separate the specific work, the standard and the next opportunity to act. Ask whether the feedback is usable and whether the standard was known. For speaking up, compare actual responses to questions or mistakes, including whose input is acknowledged. If you hold authority, notice whether you invite disagreement and then debate every concern until it disappears.

## How to respond

Describe the relevant action and impact, ask for missing information, then agree a feasible next step. Keep person-level insults out of the task. When someone raises a risk, acknowledge it, clarify what action follows and return with an update. A person facing retaliation may need a confidential or formal route; no conversation technique guarantees protection.

**A brief fictional example:** A manager says, “Be more professional.” The employee cannot tell what to change. “The client did not receive the revised delivery date; can we agree who sends that update?” identifies an action and a future process. The employee may still supply information that changes the manager’s account.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Feedback can help or hinder; usable information and context matter.
- Safety is learned partly from the consequences of speaking up.
- Specific standards and reliable follow-up support accountability without humiliation.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Choose one feedback exchange. Write the action, standard and opportunity to change; note anything the other person could legitimately dispute.
2. Give or request one specific clarification and agree a next step. If you receive a concern, state what you will do with it.
3. Review whether the information improved the task and whether questions or corrections remained welcome. Include unintended effects.
