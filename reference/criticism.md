# Repeated criticism

Repeated criticism involves recurring negative evaluation of someone’s work, choices or conduct. It can include necessary feedback, an unresolved standards dispute or a demeaning pattern. Frequency alone does not distinguish them. The content, context, proportionality and opportunity to respond all matter.

> **Remember:** Separate the useful issue from the way it is delivered.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

A person may be trying to correct a real problem, expressing frustration, applying an unclear standard or seeking influence. Public criticism can also affect status and willingness to speak. These are possible explanations, not an exhaustive list or a way to establish intent. A legitimate concern can be delivered harmfully, and an unpleasant delivery does not automatically make every factual point wrong.

## What the evidence supports

Jason Colquitt and colleagues combined 183 organisational-justice studies. Fair outcomes, procedures, interpersonal treatment and information were related but distinguishable aspects of perceived fairness. Most evidence concerns organisations. Disagreement about fairness is not proof of dishonesty or a single objectively correct allocation. [Colquitt and colleagues (2001). Justice at the millennium: a meta-analytic review of 25 years of organizational justice research.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11419803/)

Guy Itzchakov and colleagues manipulated listening quality in four laboratory experiments. Attentive, nonjudgmental listening reduced speakers’ social anxiety and helped them recognise mixed attitudes in the studied tasks. The experiments do not show that listening guarantees agreement or repairs a harmful relationship. [Itzchakov, Kluger & Castro (2017). I Am Aware of My Inconsistencies but Can Tolerate Them: The Effect of High Quality Listening on Speakers' Attitude Ambivalence.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27856728/)

The US Equal Employment Opportunity Commission describes harassment linked to protected characteristics and retaliation within the laws it administers. Legal definitions, coverage and procedures vary by jurisdiction. Unfair or harmful conduct can still matter when it does not meet this legal definition. [US EEOC: Harassment](https://www.eeoc.gov/harassment)

## A general tendency or a pattern that gets results? {#tendency-or-learned-pattern}

Calling someone a critical person summarises what you have noticed; it does not explain why the criticism continues. Compare a tendency across settings with expectations learned in particular relationships and with what the criticism accomplishes. Criticism might lead others to change plans or stop disagreeing. That outcome is a possible influence, not proof of motive or reinforcement.

These explanations can coexist. The person may also be unaware of the impact or believe their comments are welcome. No objection does not establish agreement: people may be tired, dependent or worried about the response. Compare occasions, exceptions and what happened when concerns were safely raised. Selective restraint is useful information about context, not proof of a deliberate campaign.

The [guide to tendencies, expectations and consequences](reinforcement.md#tendency-expectation-consequence) explains the comparison. Whatever the explanation, a recipient does not cause another person’s harmful conduct by failing to object.

[Fleeson (2001). Toward a structure- and process-integrated view of personality: traits as density distribution of states.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11414368/). A few memorable events cannot establish a lifelong trait; self-reports and the sampled settings have limits.

[Frazier and colleagues (2017). Psychological Safety: A Meta-Analytic Review and Extension.](https://digitalcommons.odu.edu/management_fac_pubs/13/). The evidence does not guarantee that speaking up is safe in a particular organisation or that one invitation will change its consequences.

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Specific feedback differs from a global judgment such as “You are useless.” A demanding role can justify careful review without justifying humiliation. A person may feel criticised by an ordinary disagreement, while another may minimise a genuine pattern. Compare the conduct and effects rather than deciding from either label alone.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Collect a few representative examples, including what was criticised, the stated standard, the audience and what followed. Include positive or ordinary feedback and whether the same standard applies to others. Ask whether you can understand what would count as adequate work. If criticism is yours, notice whether it identifies a change or merely communicates dissatisfaction. Compare how feedback is delivered to different people and what changes after a concern is raised, where it is safe to do so. A single improvement or refusal to change does not settle the explanation.

## How to respond

For an ordinary issue, ask for a specific example and a usable standard. Address any accurate part while requesting a different method of feedback where needed. If the pattern is belittling, discriminatory or retaliatory, consider relevant support and procedures. Do not make your safety or self-respect depend on proving the critic’s psychological motive.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: A manager says a report is poor but gives no criteria. Asking which decision it fails to support and what needs changing makes the issue more concrete. Repeated public insults would remain a separate concern even if the report needs revision.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Feedback content and delivery can require separate responses.
- A usable standard matters more than a global label.
- Patterns of humiliation deserve attention without a motive verdict.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Write one representative criticism, its context and the standard you understood.
2. Ask for a concrete correction or choose a suitable support route for harmful conduct.
3. Review whether the feedback became usable and respectful, including any repeated pattern.
