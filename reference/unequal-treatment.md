# Different standards & unequal treatment

Unequal treatment means people receive different resources, opportunities, attention or rules. A double standard applies a different judgment to comparable conduct without a relevant justification. Differences can be unfair, but equal treatment and fair treatment are not always identical: people may have different needs or responsibilities.

> **Remember:** Compare like situations and explain relevant differences.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

Unequal treatment can follow preference, habit, accessibility needs, role duties, resource constraints or a judgment about past reliability. It can also privilege an insider or repeatedly burden the same person. These are alternatives to check, not a complete list or a way to infer the reason from the unequal outcome alone.

## What the evidence supports

Jensen and Thomsen combined research involving 26,451 participants. Differences in parental treatment were associated with children’s internalising and externalising difficulties. The largely White child/adolescent samples do not establish an adult in-law’s motive, and association does not identify a single cause. [Jensen & Thomsen (2024). Parental differential treatment of siblings linked with internalizing and externalizing behavior: A meta-analysis.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38439142/)

Jason Colquitt and colleagues combined 183 organisational-justice studies. Fair outcomes, procedures, interpersonal treatment and information were related but distinguishable aspects of perceived fairness. Most evidence concerns organisations. Disagreement about fairness is not proof of dishonesty or a single objectively correct allocation. [Colquitt and colleagues (2001). Justice at the millennium: a meta-analytic review of 25 years of organizational justice research.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11419803/)

## Golden child and scapegoat are descriptions to unpack {#labels}

These popular labels can name a painful experience, but they can also make a family seem to have a permanent script. Replace the label with what someone receives or is blamed for, how often comparable events happen, and who has a chance to correct the account. Do not assume that a favoured child welcomes the role or is responsible for another adult’s treatment.

A single serious discriminatory or threatening incident can warrant action; you do not need a long pattern to take it seriously. Repetition is useful for understanding a recurring standard, not a requirement for asking for help.

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

A younger child needing more help is different from one child being praised for behaviour another is punished for. A temporary illness can justify changed duties without making every later difference necessary. Differences between adult in-laws are not directly explained by research on parents and young siblings.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Choose a specific comparison: same conduct, relevant circumstances, rule and consequence. Record any stated reason and an exception that challenges your account. Ask who can question the rule and whether the explanation is consistently applied. Avoid counting every welcome difference as favouritism or dismissing a concern because the people are not identical.

## How to respond

Raise the actual allocation or standard: “We were both late; why was only one person excluded?” Ask for a transparent future rule or a needs-based adjustment. You can seek fair treatment without demanding identical affection. If you set the rules, explain relevant differences before resentment grows and be willing to correct an inconsistency.

**A brief fictional example:** Two siblings are expected to contribute to a family event. One is excused because of a medical appointment, while the other was refused an exception for a similar appointment. Comparing the circumstances and rule is more informative than immediately naming one the “golden child”.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Unequal treatment can be justified or unfair; compare the relevant circumstances.
- Child-sibling research cannot establish an adult family strategy.
- Ask for a clear rule or adjustment rather than a permanent family label.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Record one pair of comparable occasions, the different response and the reason given. Note any important difference that weakens the comparison.
2. Request one fair future rule or explain a relevant adjustment if you are making the decision.
3. Review how the rule was applied next time, including exceptions, effects and any change in your original explanation.
