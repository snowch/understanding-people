# Repair after a misunderstanding

Repair after a misunderstanding means identifying where an exchange went wrong and making a useful correction. It may involve a word, an assumption, an action or a relationship expectation. It is more than restarting as though nothing happened, and it need not require deciding that one person is entirely at fault.

> **Remember:** Return to the missed meaning and the needed change.

## How it may arise

Misunderstandings can grow when people respond to an inferred intention, use the same phrase differently or assume that a concern was already understood. Defending the original intention may then obscure the effect. Both parties can contribute to confusion, while responsibility for a harmful action remains specific rather than cancelled out.

## What the evidence supports

Tal Eyal and colleagues tested interpersonal predictions across a series of experiments. Imagining another person’s perspective did not consistently improve accuracy; a final experiment found that obtaining information through conversation helped. Conversation does not guarantee honesty, complete understanding or safety. These were specific prediction tasks, not tests of every relationship. [Eyal, Steffel & Epley (2018). Perspective mistaking: Accurately understanding the mind of another requires getting perspective, not taking perspective.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29620401/)

Roy Lewicki and colleagues ran two studies of how people judged apologies after hypothetical trust violations. Apologies differed in perceived effectiveness depending on what they contained and the kind of violation. Judging a scenario differs from rebuilding trust after real harm. No apology formula obliges forgiveness or renewed access. [Lewicki, Polin & Lount (2016). An Exploration of the Structure of Effective Apologies.](https://doi.org/10.1111/ncmr.12073)

Thomas Webb and Paschal Sheeran combined 47 experimental tests in which intentions changed. Changes in behaviour were generally smaller than changes in intention. The result supports separating a promise from follow-through, not deciding why an individual failed to act. [Webb & Sheeran (2006). Does changing behavioral intentions engender behavior change? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16536643/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

A misunderstanding differs from a disagreement about values or an established breach of an agreement. Calling repeated conduct a misunderstanding does not make it one. A useful repair can clarify meaning without restoring trust immediately or requiring forgiveness. The person affected can still need a practical limit.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Identify the exact wording, expectation or action that diverged. Ask what each person understood at the time, and compare with any relevant record. Notice whether new information changes the account or whether the same concern is being dismissed again. Include what you can own without accepting an inaccurate global accusation.

## How to respond

State the correction, acknowledge the relevant effect and agree the next action. If you made an unsupported assumption, say what you now understand differently. If a commitment was missed, address practical repair and prevention as well as wording. Review later follow-through rather than treating a smooth conversation as proof the issue is resolved.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: You thought “I support the idea” meant a colleague would recommend it in a meeting. They meant it was promising but unfinished. Clarifying the remaining work and how future support will be stated repairs a specific expectation without settling every question about the relationship.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Locate the actual divergence in meaning or action.
- Clarification and accountability can both be needed.
- Trust depends on what follows as well as what is said.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Write one point where your understanding and the other person’s may have differed.
2. Ask a specific clarification or make a correction you own.
3. Review whether the next agreement and later conduct became clearer.
