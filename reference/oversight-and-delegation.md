# Oversight, delegation & micromanagement

Oversight checks whether responsibilities, risks and standards are being managed. Delegation gives someone responsibility with enough authority and resources to act. Micromanagement is a common description of unnecessarily detailed control over how another person works; the label alone does not establish where the appropriate level of supervision lies.

> **Remember:** Agree the outcome, decision space and necessary checks.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

Frequent checks may follow genuine risk, a new skill, previous failures, unclear expectations or a manager’s difficulty relinquishing control. The same check can be proportionate in a safety-critical task and disruptive in routine work. A person may experience repeated direction as evidence that their judgment is not trusted.

## What the evidence supports

Gavin Slemp and colleagues combined 83 unique workplace samples. Perceived autonomy-supportive supervision was associated with autonomous motivation, well-being and positive work behaviour. The largely correlational evidence cannot determine whether one manager’s oversight is excessive or show that removing all checks improves work. [Slemp and colleagues (2018). Leader autonomy support in the workplace: A meta-analytic review.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30237648/)

Jason Colquitt and colleagues combined 183 organisational-justice studies. Fair outcomes, procedures, interpersonal treatment and information were related but distinguishable aspects of perceived fairness. Most evidence concerns organisations. Disagreement about fairness is not proof of dishonesty or a single objectively correct allocation. [Colquitt and colleagues (2001). Justice at the millennium: a meta-analytic review of 25 years of organizational justice research.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11419803/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Autonomy is not absence of accountability. Clear standards differ from prescribing every step, and requested coaching differs from unwanted takeover. Conversely, “trust me” does not remove agreed reporting or safeguarding duties. Someone’s competence in one area cannot establish readiness for a different task.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

List the decisions the person can actually make, the reason for each check and the resources available. Does the frequency reduce as demonstrated competence increases? Are corrections about an agreed outcome or the supervisor’s preferred style? Include whether delays or missing updates force others to chase information.

## How to respond

Agree an outcome, acceptable constraints, decision rights and a review rhythm. Use an early warning threshold for risks rather than checking every move. Ask what support is needed, and give feedback against an agreed standard. If you are the supervisor, make a real decision you will leave with the other person; if supervised, propose a reliable update that could replace repeated chasing.

**A brief fictional example:** A manager asks for hourly progress messages on a routine report. The writer proposes a draft by Thursday and an early warning if a dependency slips. Agreeing that rhythm tests whether clearer visibility meets the manager’s need without constant interruption.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Good delegation joins responsibility with authority and resources.
- The need for checks depends on task, skill and consequences.
- Review outcomes and coordination costs rather than guessing a manager’s personality.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Choose one task and list its outcome, risks, decision rights and current checks. Identify one check whose purpose is unclear.
2. Propose one proportionate review rhythm or a specific decision to delegate. State when more support or escalation would be needed.
3. Review quality, missed risks, interruptions and ownership. Adjust the arrangement using results, not a verdict about who is controlling.
