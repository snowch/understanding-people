# Escalating requests & everyday influence

A request sequence uses more than one request in a way that may affect the later decision. Foot-in-the-door describes a small request followed by a larger one. Door-in-the-face describes a large request that is refused, followed by a smaller one. These name studied procedures, not guaranteed effects or proof of a requester’s motive.

> **Remember:** Evaluate the current request on its own terms.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

Earlier agreement can make consistency feel important. A retreat from an excessive request can look like a concession, and a favour can create a sense of obligation. Genuine collaboration also uses gradual commitments and compromise. Examine the actual terms, transparency and freedom to decline, rather than treating any sequence as manipulation.

## What the evidence supports

Jerry Burger reviewed research on small requests followed by larger requests. Several processes may contribute, including consistency, commitment and reactance; conditions can also eliminate or reverse the effect. This is not a guaranteed way to obtain agreement or a way to infer a requester’s intent. [Burger (1999). The foot-in-the-door compliance procedure: a multiple-process analysis and review.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15661679/)

Alexandre Pascual and Nicolas Guéguen compared studies of small-then-large and large-then-smaller requests. They found no significant difference between the procedures in those direct comparisons. Comparing the two procedures does not alone establish that either beats an ordinary request in every setting. [Pascual & Guéguen (2005). Foot-in-the-door and door-in-the-face: a comparative meta-analytic study.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15825914/)

Papakonstantinou and colleagues reviewed 89 trials of social-norm health messages. The apparent average benefit disappeared after adjustment for publication bias. This finding concerns specific health interventions in developed countries, not whether social norms ever influence people. [Papakonstantinou and colleagues (2025). A systematic review and meta-analysis of social norms messaging approaches for improving health behaviours.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40983670/)

## “Everyone does it” is a claim to check {#norms-and-claims}

Descriptive norms concern what people do; prescriptive norms concern what they approve of. A statement about either may be inaccurate, selective or irrelevant to your circumstances. Popularity does not establish quality, and disapproval does not settle whether a request is fair.

The social-norm messaging review above found that benefits disappeared after publication-bias adjustment in its health-intervention setting. This is a reason to check claims of effectiveness, not to conclude that norms never matter. For your decision, ask who the comparison group is and whether their circumstances fit.

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

A project may genuinely change after new information. A smaller request may be a reasonable adjustment to your capacity. Urgency may reflect a real deadline or an artificial sales device. Ask what makes the deadline necessary and what happens if you wait; a claim of scarcity is not independent evidence of value.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Compare the first and current requests: time, cost, privacy, authority and consequences. Did the requester disclose what they expected next? Can you reconsider without being threatened or shamed? Include whether you used a small favour as an unstated purchase of later agreement.

## How to respond

Pause and assess the present request: “I agreed to review one page, not rewrite the report.” Ask for the complete commitment and relevant evidence. A concession does not oblige you to accept unsuitable terms. If another person declines, do not escalate pressure by invoking gratitude or their earlier yes.

**A brief fictional example:** A service offers a free assessment, then asks you to buy an expensive package before leaving. Attending the assessment does not establish the package’s suitability. Comparing price, evidence and alternatives is useful whether the sequence was deliberate sales design or ordinary follow-up.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- A request sequence can influence a decision without determining it.
- An earlier yes does not create unlimited consent or obligation.
- Check reasons, terms and the real cost of saying no.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Choose one request that grew or changed. Record the original terms, later terms and any information that explains the change.
2. Assess the current request independently; ask for missing terms or state a clear limit without using a counter-tactic.
3. Review whether the decision remained informed and voluntary. If you made the request, review whether the other person could genuinely decline.
