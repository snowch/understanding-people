# Favours that create unwanted obligation

Favours can create unwanted obligation when help is treated afterward as permission to demand unrelated access, loyalty or repayment that was never agreed. A freely accepted exchange is different from a hidden condition. Gratitude for help does not establish consent to every later request.

> **Remember:** Clarify the terms before a favour becomes a debt.

## How it may arise

People may bring different assumptions about gifts, loans, family duties and reciprocity. A giver may expect a return without having made it explicit; a recipient may hear a gift where repayment was intended. The pattern can also become coercive when dependence or essential resources are used to enforce compliance.

## What the evidence supports

Russell Cropanzano and Marie Mitchell reviewed social-exchange theory in organisational research, including different exchange rules, resources and relationships. The review also identifies conceptual ambiguity. Relationships cannot be reduced to a universal ledger of favours. [Cropanzano & Mitchell (2005). Social Exchange Theory: An Interdisciplinary Review.](https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206305279602)

Richard Ryan and Edward Deci reviewed self-determination theory and evidence concerning autonomy, competence and relatedness in motivation and well-being. The framework suggests distinctions to investigate. It cannot identify the cause of a particular action or refusal. [Ryan & Deci (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11392867/)

Home Office statutory guidance describes controlling or coercive behaviour as an unfolding pattern and discusses restrictions, threats, economic abuse and effects on everyday freedom. It addresses law and practice in England and Wales. This book does not establish whether a legal offence occurred or replace local advice. [Home Office: Controlling or coercive behaviour — statutory guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/controlling-or-coercive-behaviour-statutory-guidance-framework/controlling-or-coercive-behaviour-statutory-guidance-framework-accessible)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

An explicit loan, professional service or shared responsibility has terms that should be considered fairly. Not every request for reciprocity is manipulation. Equally, generosity does not buy decision rights over another adult. A person can acknowledge a contribution while disputing a new condition or declining further help.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Identify what was offered, which terms were known, whether acceptance was optional and what is now being demanded. Compare whether refusal of the later request is respected. Include your own assumptions when you give help: are you freely offering something, making a proposal or expecting an unspoken return?

## How to respond

For an ordinary exchange, clarify costs, repayment and decision rights before proceeding. If terms are disputed afterward, separate the original agreement from the new request and seek appropriate advice where money or rights are involved. If essential support is being used to restrict choices, consider confidential support rather than trying to repay enough to earn freedom.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: A parent offers moving help, then says this means they must have a key to the new home. The practical help and ongoing access are separate questions. Appreciation does not settle whether a key will be given.

<section class="key-takeaways" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="key-takeaways">

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- A favour and unrelated access are different agreements.
- Make exchange terms explicit rather than relying on a hidden ledger.
- Dependence can make apparent agreement less voluntary.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Describe one offer and the terms actually understood at acceptance.
2. Clarify a condition or seek suitable advice about a disputed or coercive obligation.
3. Review whether the arrangement remains voluntary and understandable, without staging a refusal as a test.
