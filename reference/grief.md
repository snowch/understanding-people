# Grief, loss & changing needs

Grief is a response to loss. It can follow a death, the end of a relationship, a change in health, a lost role or another meaningful loss. People may experience sadness, anger, numbness, relief or mixed feelings, sometimes at unexpected times. There is no compulsory emotional sequence that everyone must display.

> **Remember:** Make room for variation without ignoring a need.

## How it may arise

A loss can change practical routines, responsibilities, social contact and the future someone expected. A person may want company on one day and privacy on another. Anniversaries or ordinary reminders can carry meaning. These possibilities help frame a question; they should not become a timetable imposed on someone’s recovery.

## What the evidence supports

NHS public guidance describes varied responses to loss and ways to seek support when grief is difficult to manage. There is no compulsory sequence of feelings or timetable that every grieving person must follow. [NHS: Grief after bereavement or loss](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/feelings-symptoms-behaviours/feelings-and-symptoms/grief-bereavement-loss/)

NIMH public guidance distinguishes passing low feelings from depression that can significantly affect thinking, sleep, eating, work and other daily activities. Only assessment can establish the explanation of a particular change; quietness or withdrawal alone is insufficient. [NIMH: Depression](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/topics/depression)

George Bonanno and Charles Burton reviewed coping and emotion-regulation research. Their framework emphasises noticing context, having several response options and adjusting to feedback. It is a framework with acknowledged measurement limits, not evidence that one response or this worksheet always works. [Bonanno & Burton (2013). Regulatory Flexibility: An Individual Differences Perspective on Coping and Emotion Regulation.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173226/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Grief and depression can overlap, and persistent or severe difficulties may need assessment. A moment of enjoyment does not prove a person is no longer grieving, and a quiet response does not reveal a lack of love. Harmful conduct still has effects even when it occurs during loss. The other people affected may need support too.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Ask what is hardest now and whether the person wants listening, practical help, contact or space. Notice daily functioning and changes over time without evaluating them against a required stage. Compare the help you are offering with what is actually needed, including work that has become unowned after the loss.

## How to respond

Offer a specific manageable contribution and allow an honest answer. Remember that needs may change after initial attention fades. Avoid requiring positive meaning, closure or a particular form of expression. If distress or functioning is persistently difficult, encourage suitable support; immediate safety concerns require appropriate urgent help. You can support grief without being the person’s only resource.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: A bereaved friend laughs during lunch and cries later while sorting paperwork. The two responses are compatible. Helping with one administrative task may be more useful than interpreting the laughter as evidence that they have moved on.

<section class="key-takeaways" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="key-takeaways">

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Grief can include changing and apparently conflicting feelings.
- No required stage or display establishes how much a loss matters.
- Practical support and professional help can be appropriate.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Identify a specific current need related to a loss, using the person’s account where possible.
2. Offer one wanted contribution or find a suitable support route.
3. Review whether it helped and what needs changed, without scoring progress through stages.
