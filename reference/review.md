# Review what happened

Review means returning to a chosen action and asking what happened, what changed in your understanding and what to do next. It differs from replaying a conversation without new information or collecting evidence for a verdict about character. A useful review preserves the original account and records later learning separately.

> **Remember:** Compare the result with what you expected.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

Expectations can shift after an outcome, making a result seem more predictable than it was. You may also remember the most emotional part and miss a practical improvement. Writing a modest prediction and review point beforehand can make the comparison clearer. Monitoring can help chosen goals, but excessive tracking can become a burden or surveillance.

## What the evidence supports

Benjamin Harkin and colleagues combined 138 experiments. Interventions that increased monitoring of goal progress improved goal attainment on average. Monitoring a chosen goal is different from surveillance of another person; the review did not test this book’s complete method. [Harkin and colleagues (2016). Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/)

George Bonanno and Charles Burton reviewed coping and emotion-regulation research. Their framework emphasises noticing context, having several response options and adjusting to feedback. It is a framework with acknowledged measurement limits, not evidence that one response or this worksheet always works. [Bonanno & Burton (2013). Regulatory Flexibility: An Individual Differences Perspective on Coping and Emotion Regulation.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173226/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

A single outcome does not prove an explanation or show that your intervention caused it. Other conditions may have changed, and a response can have mixed effects. A respected refusal, a clearer disagreement or deciding to seek support can be useful outcomes. Agreement or immediate warmth is not the only measure of collaboration.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Record what you actually tried, what each person said or did afterward, and what information remains missing. Compare the result with your stated aim. Include costs, unintended effects and the other person’s feedback where available. Distinguish a direct report from what you infer about how they felt.

## How to respond

Choose whether to keep, change or stop the approach. Make the next adjustment small enough to learn from where possible. If repeated attempts produce no benefit, do not assume that more analysis is always better. Look at the arrangement, available support and whether a practical decision is now needed. Keep the record proportionate and protect privacy.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: You asked before offering advice. Your friend still declined, but the conversation was calmer and they explained what help would suit them. The approach may have improved clarity even though the answer was not the one you hoped for.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Preserve the original observation and add later learning.
- One result rarely establishes a cause.
- Measure clarity, choice and practical effects as well as agreement.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Write what you expect one chosen response to improve and when you will review it.
2. Try the response and note the observable result without rewriting the original prediction.
3. Decide what to retain, adjust or stop, including whether further help is needed.
