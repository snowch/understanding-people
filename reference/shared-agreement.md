# Make a workable agreement

A workable agreement makes responsibilities sufficiently clear for people to act and notice when something needs changing. It includes what will be done, who owns it, by when, with which resources and how difficulties will be raised. It can be informal, but it should not depend on each person silently supplying a different meaning.

> **Remember:** Agree the outcome, ownership and review.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

People may agree enthusiastically while picturing different amounts of work or different decision rights. A plan may also fail when capacity changes or a task depends on someone who was never consulted. Wanting an outcome and carrying out its steps are related but distinct. Clarity and feasibility need attention alongside goodwill.

## What the evidence supports

Thomas Webb and Paschal Sheeran combined 47 experimental tests in which intentions changed. Changes in behaviour were generally smaller than changes in intention. The result supports separating a promise from follow-through, not deciding why an individual failed to act. [Webb & Sheeran (2006). Does changing behavioral intentions engender behavior change? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16536643/)

Martin Hagger and Aleksandra Luszczynska reviewed action-planning interventions in health settings, including differences in results and study quality. A concrete plan may be useful, but health-intervention findings do not validate a family or workplace agreement. [Hagger & Luszczynska (2014). Implementation intention and action planning interventions in health contexts: state of the research and proposals for the way forward.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24591064/)

Jason Colquitt and colleagues combined 183 organisational-justice studies. Fair outcomes, procedures, interpersonal treatment and information were related but distinguishable aspects of perceived fairness. Most evidence concerns organisations. Disagreement about fairness is not proof of dishonesty or a single objectively correct allocation. [Colquitt and colleagues (2001). Justice at the millennium: a meta-analytic review of 25 years of organizational justice research.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11419803/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

A written agreement is not proof that consent was free, the terms fair or the work achievable. A shared document can clarify decisions or become a tool for blaming and monitoring. Silence, politeness and private encouragement are not automatically commitments. Legal, employment and care arrangements may require advice beyond an ordinary conversation.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Ask each person to state what they will do and what they need from others. Identify dependencies, standards of completion and the point at which someone should warn that the plan is at risk. Include invisible coordination work. Check whether anyone can honestly decline or renegotiate without threats.

## How to respond

Write a brief account of the actual agreement, not a transcript of every feeling. Build in a review date and a fair route for correcting misunderstanding. If one person cannot contribute as hoped, reconsider scope or another arrangement rather than relying on repeated rescue. A good agreement makes an early warning useful instead of treating it as a moral failure.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: “We will each cook twice this week” leaves shopping and planning unowned. Clarifying who buys ingredients and which nights are covered makes the arrangement more testable without requiring a judgment about who cares most.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- A shared goal does not define the work by itself.
- Consent, feasibility and fairness matter alongside clarity.
- An early warning should lead to a practical adjustment.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Choose one recurring shared task and list ownership, timing and dependencies.
2. Agree one small plan and how capacity changes will be raised.
3. Review completion and coordination burden, then amend the agreement rather than rewriting the past.
