# Adult attachment: anxiety, avoidance & security

Adult attachment research examines expectations and responses around closeness, support and separation. Two commonly studied dimensions are attachment anxiety—concern about availability or rejection—and attachment avoidance—discomfort with dependence or closeness. These are dimensions, not diagnoses or four permanent kinds of person.

## How it may arise

Earlier experiences can contribute to expectations, while current relationships and circumstances also matter. A response may differ with a dependable friend and an unreliable partner. A developmental story is a possibility to explore through the person’s account, not something established by one refusal, unanswered message or argument.

## What the evidence supports

Zhang and colleagues combined studies of attachment anxiety and avoidance. Both dimensions were associated with mental-health measures. The associations do not diagnose anyone or establish a fixed attachment type or childhood cause. [Zhang and colleagues (2022). The relationship between adult attachment and mental health: A meta-analysis.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36201836/)

Benjamin Hadden and colleagues reviewed associations between attachment dimensions, relationship satisfaction and commitment. Relationship duration moderated associations in cross-sectional samples. Differences between longer and shorter relationships do not establish a person’s future or show that duration caused deterioration. [Hadden, Smith & Webster (2014). Relationship duration moderates associations between attachment and relationship quality.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24026179/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Independence is not automatically avoidance, and a request for reassurance can fit a genuinely inconsistent situation. Avoidant attachment is different from avoidant personality disorder. Cultural practices, communication preferences, trauma, workload and safety can affect contact or help-seeking without establishing an attachment explanation.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Ask which situations evoke concern about rejection or loss of choice and what reassurance or distance actually changes. Compare relationships and exceptions. Does the concern fit current conduct? Include whether your own inconsistency or repeated pursuit changes the interaction. Do not require someone to accept a style label to have their concern heard.

## How to respond

Make availability and boundaries clearer where you can: say when you will reply, ask directly about a need and respect the answer. Support can include reliable contact and chosen independence. If recurring distress or relationship difficulties are impairing life, appropriate professional support may help; this reference cannot determine a cause or treatment.

**A brief fictional example:** A person worries after a partner repeatedly cancels without notice but feels secure with friends who keep plans. The contrast suggests checking current reliability before deciding they are an “anxious person”. It does not rule out broader expectations; both may matter.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Attachment anxiety and avoidance describe dimensions, not fixed identities.
- Current reliability, context and relationship differences matter.
- Use clearer agreements and appropriate support without diagnosing a relative.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Describe one closeness or support difficulty and a contrasting occasion or relationship. Separate the current facts from a proposed attachment explanation.
2. Choose one clear request or self-directed response that respects both people’s boundaries; write what it could clarify.
3. Review the practical result and remaining uncertainty. Do not use reassurance-seeking or withdrawal alone to assign a type.
