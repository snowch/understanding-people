# Feeling safe

Security concerns protection from harm and a sense of enough stability to act. It can involve physical safety, housing, money, employment or dependable relationships. Feeling safe and being safe are related but different: reassurance can reduce fear without changing a risk, while a precaution can be sensible even when someone appears calm.

> **Remember:** Separate a feeling of danger from the danger itself.

## How it may arise

An uncertain arrangement may threaten several forms of security at once. A person facing unstable work may hesitate to disagree with a manager; a family member may resist a change that affects housing. Past experience can shape expectations, but an observer should not invent a history to explain caution. Actual dependence and available alternatives matter.

## What the evidence supports

Susan Michie and colleagues reviewed behaviour-change frameworks and proposed COM-B: capability, opportunity and motivation as interacting conditions for behaviour. They examined its use in tobacco-control and obesity policy. It is a framework for analysing and designing change, not a diagnostic test or proof that one identified barrier caused an action. [Michie, van Stralen & West (2011). The behaviour change wheel: a new method for characterising and designing behaviour change interventions.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21513547/)

NIMH public guidance recommends considering distress, persistence and interference with usual activities when seeking help. US service information may not apply elsewhere; support should be adapted to local options and urgency. [NIMH: Caring for your mental health](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/topics/caring-for-your-mental-health)

NHS public guidance describes emotional, physical, sexual and economic abuse and routes to confidential support. Services are UK-specific. It is guidance, not a test that determines a person’s motive or an individual safety assessment. [NHS: Domestic violence and abuse](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/getting-help-for-domestic-violence/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Caution may be a reasonable response to evidence or a response that has spread beyond the current danger. You cannot settle that distinction from visible anxiety alone. Privacy, predictability and control can also be mistaken for one another. Someone’s wish to feel secure does not entitle them to monitor or restrict another person.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Clarify the concrete threat, available information and consequences of being wrong. Ask what would make an arrangement more dependable: notice, written terms, independent advice or another practical safeguard. If there are threats or restrictions, examine the pattern and support options rather than asking the person to prove they are frightened enough.

## How to respond

Address a real vulnerability before relying on reassurance. Make promises only about things you can control, and explain uncertainty honestly. If the main problem is persistent distress despite reasonable safeguards, professional support may help. Where coercion or immediate harm is a concern, use the support page and an appropriate local service rather than conducting a relationship experiment.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: A client hesitates to sign because cancellation would leave them without a service. Clear delivery terms and a credible fallback address the risk better than repeating “Trust me.” Their hesitation does not establish a suspicious personality.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Security includes material conditions, not only feelings.
- Reassurance and risk reduction are different actions.
- Fear does not justify controlling someone else.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Name one uncertainty and the specific harm you are trying to prevent.
2. Choose a proportionate safeguard or seek advice about a risk beyond your knowledge.
3. Review what became safer and what remains uncertain; do not provoke danger to test an explanation.
