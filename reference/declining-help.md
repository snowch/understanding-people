# Declining help

Declining help means saying no to an offer of assistance. To understand the refusal, look at what was offered, which decisions it would affect, and what accepting it would involve.

> **Remember:** Compare the offers, not just the refusals.

## What a refusal tells you {#what-a-refusal-tells-you}

A refusal tells you that this particular offer was not accepted. It does not, by itself, explain why. Wanting a problem solved, wanting assistance and wanting the assistance you offered are different things.

Keep the action separate from your interpretation. “They declined my offer” describes what happened. “They do not trust me” is a possible explanation to check. A clear refusal also gives you enough information to stop pressing, even when the reason is unknown.

**Example:** Someone turns down your offer to organise their move but accepts help carrying boxes. They have declined one kind of help, not all help.

## Accepting help can preserve independence {#independence}

Autonomy means having a sense of choice over what you do. It is different from doing everything without assistance. You can choose to receive help while keeping the decisions that matter to you.

Competence concerns feeling effective and capable. Relatedness concerns connection with others. These are distinct questions: does the offer leave me a choice, let me use or develop my abilities, and fit the relationship?

Psychologists Richard Ryan and Edward Deci reviewed self-determination theory and its evidence in 2000. Their framework distinguishes autonomy, competence and relatedness. It helps us ask useful questions; it cannot identify the cause of a particular refusal.

**Example:** “Could you drive me to the appointment? I want to speak to the doctor myself.” The person chooses practical support while keeping responsibility for the conversation.

[Ryan & Deci (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.68)

## Why an offer might be declined {#compare-possibilities}

The following are possible explanations to compare, not an exhaustive list or a test of personality. More than one may apply. The questions and examples are practical suggestions, not established findings about the person you have in mind.

### Keeping a decision or a chance to practise

Help may include taking over a decision the person wants to make, or a task they want to learn. Separate assistance from control of the task. Ask which part, if any, they want help with.

**Example:** A child wants to assemble a model themselves but asks you to hold two pieces together. Assistance is welcome; taking over is not.

### The offer does not fit

Consider the task, timing, amount of help and whether assistance is needed at all. A refusal can be a reasonable response to an unsuitable offer. Ask what would be useful before assuming reluctance to accept support.

**Example:** Your friend declines help tonight because they are working, but welcomes the same offer on Saturday.

### Obligation or previous experience

Accepting help may involve a real cost: repayment, favours, criticism or unwanted involvement later. Ask what the person expects would follow. Distinguish something they report from a history you are inventing.

**Example:** Someone declines a loan because the lender previously expected a say in their spending. The terms of the help matter.

### Privacy, trust or embarrassment

Assistance can require sharing information or letting someone see a difficulty. Notice whether the response changes with the helper or the audience, but do not treat that difference as proof of distrust or shame.

**Example:** A colleague accepts a private offer to check a presentation but declines the offer when it is made in front of the team.

### Effort, stress or difficulty explaining the task

Accepting assistance can itself require effort: explaining what is needed, coordinating people or changing a routine. Check whether the proposed help would reduce that burden or add to it. A refusal alone cannot establish a health condition.

**Example:** An exhausted parent declines help planning meals because explaining the routine feels like another task. They may welcome an agreed shopping trip instead.

### Personal preference, learned expectations or another reason

Someone may enjoy doing the task, value managing it alone, or have learned that asking for help is discouraged. Ask what accepting help means to them. If none of these explanations fits a detail you observed, keep that detail visible and leave the reason open.

**Example:** “I like fixing the bicycle myself” expresses a preference. It need not conceal a problem with the person offering help.

## One refusal or a recurring pattern? {#patterns-and-exceptions}

Compare occasions before drawing a broader conclusion. Record who offered, what the help involved, when it was offered, which decisions would change hands, and the answer. Include accepted offers and ordinary occasions as well as refusals.

A recurring pattern becomes more useful when you can describe its conditions: “They decline unsolicited advice but ask for practical help.” That is more informative than “They never accept help.” A difference suggests something to ask about; it does not establish the reason.

**Example:** Your father accepts transport from you but prefers an accountant to help with tax paperwork. The task, expertise, privacy or relationship could matter. Compare those differences rather than deciding he rejects your help generally.

## Could it run in a family? {#family}

Relatives can share inherited tendencies, learned expectations and environments. They can also perform the same action for different reasons. Seeing a parent, yourself and a child decline help does not tell you which of those influences explains the resemblance.

Tinca Polderman and colleagues’ 2015 meta-analysis combined twin studies across many human traits. It found genetic contributions to variation in many studied traits. It did not establish a “declining help” gene or explain the refusals in an individual family. Heritability describes variation in a studied population, not the genetic percentage of one person’s action.

Keep each person’s examples separate. Ask about their preferences and experiences, and compare when they accept help. You may discover a shared expectation or different concerns. These conversations cannot separate genetic influences from upbringing.

**Example:** A grandparent says help creates a debt, a parent prefers a particular method, and a child wants to practise a new skill. Similar refusals can have different reported meanings.

[Polderman et al. (2015). Meta-analysis of the heritability of human traits based on fifty years of twin studies.](https://doi.org/10.1038/ng.3285)

## When you are the one declining {#my-part}

Compare an offer you accepted with one you refused. What differed: the helper, the task, the timing, the decisions you would lose, or the obligations you expected? Notice which concerns come from this offer and which come from an earlier experience.

You can state a preference, name a concern or suggest a narrower form of help. You can also decline without negotiating an alternative. Another person’s wish to help does not oblige you to accept.

**Example:** “I want to make the decision, but I would welcome help comparing the options.” Or simply: “Thank you. I prefer to do this myself today.”

## How to respond when someone declines {#responding}

First, respect the answer. If clarification would be welcome, ask once whether a different task or arrangement would help. Avoid repeatedly changing the offer until the person gives in.

Notice your part in the exchange. Did you ask before acting, or announce that you would take over? Can you offer assistance without requiring access, gratitude or control in return?

When the task affects you too, discuss the shared responsibility separately. Respecting someone’s refusal does not mean silently taking on their work. Agree what each person will do and what support, if any, they want.

**Example:** “You prefer to handle your part yourself. What can we agree will be ready by Friday?” This addresses a shared deadline without requiring them to accept your method.

<details class="evidence-detail reference-case" markdown="1" id="a-case-that-changes">

<summary>Optional worked example: A fictional example that changes as you learn</summary>

Alex offers to organise Sam’s move. Sam says, “No, I’ll manage.” Alex guesses that Sam does not trust them, then remembers that Sam accepted help carrying boxes the previous week. The offers differed in how much Alex would take over.

Alex asks whether help with one part would be welcome. Sam requests help carrying heavy boxes on Saturday and says, “I want to choose what gets packed first.” They agree on that division of tasks.

Afterwards, Sam says the arrangement worked because the packing decisions stayed with Sam. Alex now has a better-supported explanation for this occasion. It does not explain every earlier refusal. If Sam had declined again, Alex could still respect the answer without settling the motive.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- A refusal concerns a particular offer; it does not identify a motive or a personality trait.
- Accepting support can be compatible with keeping choice and responsibility.
- Compare occasions and exceptions, including the way you offered help.
- A useful outcome may be a clearer agreement or a respected no.

## Put it into practice {#try-and-review}

1. Compare: choose one refusal and, if you know one, an accepted offer. Write what differed and one explanation you could check.

2. Respond: choose one action within your control—clarify a welcome offer, state the help you want, agree responsibilities, or accept the refusal. Name what you hope will improve.

3. Review: after the next relevant occasion, record what you did, what the other person said or did, and what changed in your explanation. Was there more choice, clarity or respect? Decide what to keep or change. Acceptance of help is not the measure of success.
