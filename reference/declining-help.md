# Declining help

Saying no to an offer of help. Start with the particular offer and the person's response: the same action can serve different purposes.

> **Remember:** Compare the offers, not just the refusals.

This reference entry connects the book with the app. Read it here, then use **Declining help → Work through my example** in your downloaded app. You can also use the notes at the end of this page.

## A fictional example that changes as you learn {#a-case-that-changes}

Alex offers to organise Sam's move. Sam replies, ‘No, I'll manage.’ Alex thinks, ‘Sam doesn't trust me.’ So far, Alex knows that Sam declined this offer. The reason remains a guess.

A week earlier, Sam accepted Alex's offer to carry boxes. That exception matters. Organising the whole move and carrying boxes are different offers. Perhaps Sam wants to keep the decisions, dislikes Alex's proposed timing, or wants to avoid imposing. These possibilities could overlap.

Alex asks, ‘Would help with one part be useful, or would you prefer to handle it?’ Sam replies, ‘Could you carry the heavy boxes on Saturday? I want to choose what gets packed first.’ Alex agrees to that specific task.

Afterwards, Sam says the arrangement helped because the packing decisions stayed with Sam. Alex revises the original explanation: ‘This refusal did not mean Sam rejected all my help. Keeping the decisions mattered in this instance.’ They still do not know why every earlier offer was declined.

If Sam had declined the smaller offer too, that would not prove mistrust. Timing, privacy, fatigue, the task being under control, or simply not wanting assistance could still matter. Alex can respect a no without solving the motive.

## What could explain it? {#compare-possibilities}

These are starting points, not an exhaustive list or a way to identify someone's personality. Look for differences that could distinguish explanations. Include ordinary occasions, accepted help and your own part in the exchange.

| Possibility | What could help you distinguish it? |
| --- | --- |
| Keeping a choice or practising a skill | Was help accepted when the person chose the task, timing or method? Ask which decisions they want to keep. |
| The offer does not fit | Was it the wrong time, too much help, the wrong task, or something they already had under control? Ask what part, if any, would be useful. |
| Cost, obligation or past experience | Has this person said that help comes with repayment, criticism or conditions? Distinguish their account from a history you are guessing. |
| Privacy, trust or embarrassment | Does the response differ depending on who offers or who is present? Ask privately if appropriate; a difference alone does not establish the reason. |
| Current capacity and circumstances | Could tiredness, stress, access needs or trouble explaining the request matter? Check what is known instead of assigning a condition. |
| Habit, family expectations or wider norms | Is the same expectation expressed across occasions: for example, ‘I should manage on my own’? Ask what accepting help means to this person. |
| Something not covered here | What detail does none of these explanations account for? Record what you do not know, and invite the person's own explanation if useful and safe. |

## Does independence mean doing everything alone? {#independence}

In self-determination theory, autonomy concerns acting with a sense of choice and endorsement. It is not the same as doing everything without assistance. Someone can freely choose to accept help. Competence concerns feeling effective; relatedness concerns connection with others.

Ryan and Deci's 2000 review explains this framework. It gives us useful questions about choice, capability and connection. It does not tell us which need explains a particular refusal. The offer-and-review exercise here is an editorial practice suggestion, not a validated assessment.

## Could it run in a family? {#family}

Possibly, but noticing the same behaviour in a parent, yourself and a child cannot tell you how it arose. Relatives can share inherited tendencies, learned expectations and environments. They may also decline help for different reasons.

Polderman and colleagues' 2015 review combined twin studies across many human traits. It supports genetic contributions to variation in many studied traits. It did not establish a ‘declining help’ gene or study the cause of your family's refusals. Heritability describes variation in a studied population; it is not the genetic percentage of one person's action.

Keep each person's examples separate. Compare who offered, what was offered, who kept the decisions, what was accepted, and what the person said mattered. These comparisons can improve a conversation. They cannot separate genes from upbringing. A family resemblance is a question to investigate, not an inherited label.

## If you are the one declining {#my-part}

Think of one offer you accepted and one you refused. What differed? You might want to keep a decision, avoid a real obligation, practise the task, protect privacy, or simply do it yourself. Leave room for another reason.

Try saying what would fit: ‘I'd like to make the decision, but could you help me compare the options?’ Or, ‘Thank you. I prefer to do this myself today.’ You do not owe someone acceptance of an unwanted offer.

If you are offering help, notice whether you asked first or announced that you would take over. A practical offer can unintentionally remove a choice. If repeated offers are unwelcome, stop repeating them.

## One small experiment, then a review {#try-and-review}

Choose an ordinary occasion involving yourself, someone else or your interaction. Record the actual offer and answer. Keep ‘they don't trust me’ in the explanation field, not in the description of what happened.

Compare an exception if you know one. Choose a response within your control: clarify the offer, ask what would be welcome, accept the refusal, or state the help you would want. An example is: ‘Would help with one part be useful, or would you rather handle it?’

Before trying it, name what would make the exchange more workable. Perhaps you will understand the preferred task, stop pressing after a no, or agree who does what. Success does not require the person to accept help.

After the next relevant occasion, record your action, their actual words and what changed. Did your explanation become more accurate? Was there more choice, clarity or respect? What feedback did they give? What will you keep or change? Also ask whether this exercise helped or left you overanalysing.

One outcome cannot establish a stable motive. Use it to improve the next exchange, and revisit the explanation when new information appears.

## Research and limits

[Ryan & Deci (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.68) A review of the theory and evidence on autonomy, competence and relatedness; not a test for the cause of refusing help.

[Polderman et al. (2015). Meta-analysis of the heritability of human traits based on fifty years of twin studies.](https://doi.org/10.1038/ng.3285) A synthesis across many traits and populations; not evidence for a specific help-refusal gene or an explanation of an individual family.
