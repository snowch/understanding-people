# Observation & interpretation

An observation is an account of something said, done or recorded. An interpretation is your explanation of its meaning. Both matter: you need an account of the event and a working explanation. Keeping them distinct makes it possible to change the explanation without pretending the event never happened. “They left before I finished” is different from “They wanted to humiliate me.”

> **Remember:** Describe it before explaining it.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

People rarely receive a complete record of an interaction. We fill gaps using earlier experience, expectations and what seems important. That is useful for acting quickly, but the completed story can feel as certain as the part we actually witnessed. Even an observation has a source and limits: hearing an account from a friend differs from being present, and remembering exact words differs from having a message.

## What the evidence supports

Psychologists Daniel Gilbert and Patrick Malone reviewed research on correspondence bias: people can underweight circumstances when explaining another person’s behaviour. This does not establish that a particular judgment about someone’s character is mistaken. [Gilbert & Malone (1995). The correspondence bias.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7870861/)

Tal Eyal and colleagues tested interpersonal predictions across a series of experiments. Imagining another person’s perspective did not consistently improve accuracy; a final experiment found that obtaining information through conversation helped. Conversation does not guarantee honesty, complete understanding or safety. These were specific prediction tasks, not tests of every relationship. [Eyal, Steffel & Epley (2018). Perspective mistaking: Accurately understanding the mind of another requires getting perspective, not taking perspective.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29620401/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

A record can establish wording or timing while leaving intent uncertain. Conversely, missing documentation does not make an experience unreal. Do not turn “I cannot prove why” into “nothing happened.” Multiple witnesses may share a viewpoint rather than provide independent confirmation. A recording can also omit what came before or after.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Write the smallest useful account: who said or did what, on which occasion, and how you know. Mark remembered wording as approximate. Separate the effect on you from the motive you suspect. Then identify one missing detail that would distinguish explanations, such as whether an interruption followed a scheduled deadline or happened repeatedly only when you spoke.

## How to respond

Use a description another person could understand without accepting your character judgment. Ask a specific clarification if that is safe and useful. You can request a change or set a limit based on what happened and its effects while leaving intent unresolved. If you were the actor, explain the relevant circumstances and acknowledge the effect rather than demanding that your good intention settle the matter.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: You say, “You ignored me.” Your notes show a message sent on Monday and no reply by Wednesday. A useful check is whether the person saw the request and knew when you needed an answer. The unanswered message remains an observation whatever the answer.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Keep the event, its effect and your explanation distinct.
- Mark the source and uncertainty of an account.
- A practical limit can be justified before intent is known.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Choose one ordinary interaction and write the action separately from the meaning you gave it.
2. Identify one check that could change that meaning; ask or consult a relevant record if appropriate.
3. Return after checking. Keep the original account and add what changed in your understanding.
