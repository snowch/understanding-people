# Truth, lies & uncertainty

A lie involves an attempt to mislead about something the speaker believes to be false. An inaccurate statement may instead reflect memory error, misunderstanding, incomplete information or uncertainty. Concealing information and giving a technically true but misleading answer raise related questions about honesty. The first practical task is to establish what the account claims and what can be checked.

> **Remember:** Check the account, not the performance.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

People may mislead to avoid consequences, gain an advantage, protect privacy, preserve a relationship or serve another aim. These are possibilities, not a complete list. Several aims can coexist. A confident explanation of motive is weaker evidence than a relevant discrepancy, and a discrepancy still requires interpretation. Honest people can be nervous; practiced deception can look calm.

## What the evidence supports

Charles Bond and Bella DePaulo combined studies of unaided truth–lie judgments. Average accuracy was about 54%, with truths recognised more accurately than lies. This is an average across tasks, not the probability that a particular person is lying. [Bond & DePaulo (2006). Accuracy of deception judgments.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16859438/)

Aldert Vrij, Maria Hartwig and Pär Granhag reviewed nonverbal deception research. Available behavioural cues were faint and unreliable for practical lie detection. Nervousness, eye contact or confidence cannot settle whether an account is accurate. [Vrij, Hartwig & Granhag (2019). Reading Lies: Nonverbal Communication and Deception.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30609913/)

Elizabeth Loftus reviewed experimental research on misinformation after an event. Later suggestions can change what people report remembering. This does not show that a particular memory is false, or justify dismissing an account of harm. [Loftus (2005). Planting misinformation in the human mind: a 30-year investigation of the malleability of memory.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16027179/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Changing an account can be a correction rather than a sign of lying. Consistency can reflect truth, rehearsal or a shared story. Eye contact, fidgeting, pauses, accent and visible anxiety are not dependable lie detectors. Distinguish whether the information is wrong, whether the person knew it was wrong, and whether they intended to mislead you.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Clarify dates, actions and the meaning of ambiguous wording. Compare an account with independent, relevant information you have legitimate access to. Ask open questions before suggesting an answer; document revisions as revisions. Check whether your source is actually independent. Do not manufacture tests, secretly provoke a reaction or treat an AI’s confidence as evidence.

## How to respond

Where possible, make the next decision depend on verifiable steps rather than a verdict about character. You might require written approval before spending money or confirm a delivery before paying the balance. If questioning could expose you to retaliation, use a suitable support route. Suspicion does not authorise surveillance, and uncertainty does not oblige you to accept an arrangement you cannot reasonably trust.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: A seller says an item was posted on Tuesday. Tracking shows the carrier received it on Thursday. Ask what “posted” meant and check the promised delivery terms. The discrepancy matters, but alone it does not establish whether the seller was mistaken or deliberately misleading.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Inaccuracy and intentional deception are different questions.
- Demeanour does not provide a reliable lie test.
- Base important arrangements on checks and safeguards.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Write one disputed claim precisely, including what is uncertain about it.
2. Choose an independent check you can legitimately make, or a safeguard for your next decision.
3. Record what the check established and what remains unknown about knowledge or intent.
