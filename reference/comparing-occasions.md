# Compare occasions & test an explanation

A pattern is a recurring relationship among actions and circumstances. Several similar memories can suggest a pattern, but they do not by themselves establish its cause. Comparing occasions means checking what repeats, what changes and what information distinguishes explanations. It is a way to reason under uncertainty, not a test that reads someone’s intentions.

> **Remember:** Compare an occasion, an exception and what would change your mind.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

<span id="what-the-full-entry-will-cover"></span>

## How it may arise

Memorable or painful events can dominate an account. After adopting a label, you may notice matching conduct and overlook exceptions. A short record made near an event can help preserve the original observation. The record remains your perspective and may omit information that matters to another person.

## What the evidence supports

Saul Shiffman and colleagues reviewed repeated sampling of experiences close to the time they occur and in everyday settings. This can reduce reliance on global retrospective reports. A private notebook is not a validated assessment or proof of causality. Recording must remain proportionate and should not become surveillance. [Shiffman, Stone & Hufford (2008). Ecological momentary assessment.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18509902/)

Aaron Fisher and colleagues examined six repeated-measures datasets and highlighted difficulties transferring aggregate patterns to individual processes. This is a reason to check individual context and uncertainty, not a claim that population research can never inform individuals; methodological debate continues. [Fisher, Medaglia & Jeronimus (2018). Lack of group-to-individual generalizability is a threat to human subjects research.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29915059/)

Benjamin Harkin and colleagues combined 138 experiments. Interventions that increased monitoring of goal progress improved goal attainment on average. Monitoring a chosen goal is different from surveillance of another person; the review did not test this book’s complete method. [Harkin and colleagues (2016). Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/)

## Optional comparison template {#comparison-template}

| Record | What to include |
| --- | --- |
| Occasion | Exact action, context and what you observed directly. |
| Comparison | A similar opportunity, an exception, or an explicit gap in information. |
| Explanations | At least two credible possibilities, including constraints or misunderstanding where relevant. |
| Distinguishing information | A question or observation that could weaken the first account. |
| Action | A safe step within your control and what you expect it to clarify. |
| Review | Later result, unintended effects, other feedback and what remains unknown. |

Use the existing notes below or an equivalent private notebook. Do not record more identifying information than needed. Several reports of the same event remain one occasion; AI restating a report does not create another source.

</details>

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Repetition may reflect a continuing constraint, a habit, a relationship expectation or deliberate influence. A one-off act can still be serious. Shared behaviour across relatives does not separate genetics from learning, and a repeated effect does not prove a long-term strategy.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Choose a narrow question: when does this kind of help get declined, rather than “what kind of person are they?” Compare opportunities, including times the behaviour could have occurred but did not. Distinguish no observation from a genuine absence. Ask what evidence would make a rival explanation more plausible.

## How to respond

Choose a respectful clarification or a small reversible change within your control, then compare the result with a modest prediction. Keep the original account and add later learning rather than rewriting it. Stop if tracking becomes rumination, surveillance or an obstacle to a practical decision. Seek support for serious harm without waiting to complete a log.

**A brief fictional example:** Your father declines help with finances but accepts lifts and help carrying bags. The contrast weakens “he refuses all help”. It makes decision ownership, privacy and the nature of the task worth checking; it does not prove any of those explanations.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Compare opportunities and exceptions, not only memorable repetitions.
- A possible explanation needs information that could challenge it.
- A useful action and review can improve choices while the motive remains uncertain.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. In the notes below, record one occasion and a genuine comparison or missing comparison. Put two plausible explanations and evidence against each in your interpretation field.
2. Choose one safe action that could clarify a difference. State what you expect and what result would make you reconsider.
3. On the review date, record the result, costs and remaining uncertainty. Decide what to retain, change or stop rather than scoring someone’s character.
