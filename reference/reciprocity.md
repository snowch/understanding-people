# Reciprocity & mutual consideration

Reciprocity concerns how people respond to one another’s contributions within a relationship. It can involve practical help, attention, information or care. A reciprocal relationship need not have equal contributions on every occasion or exchange the same kind of resource. Capacity, need and the kind of relationship matter.

> **Remember:** Look for mutual care over time, not identical exchanges.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

People may have different expectations about whether help is a gift, a trade, a role responsibility or something to return later. Trouble arises when one person assumes an obligation the other never accepted, or when a caring relationship becomes a hidden ledger. The meaning of an exchange should not be inferred from its size alone.

## What the evidence supports

Russell Cropanzano and Marie Mitchell reviewed social-exchange theory in organisational research, including different exchange rules, resources and relationships. The review also identifies conceptual ambiguity. Relationships cannot be reduced to a universal ledger of favours. [Cropanzano & Mitchell (2005). Social Exchange Theory: An Interdisciplinary Review.](https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206305279602)

Jason Colquitt and colleagues combined 183 organisational-justice studies. Fair outcomes, procedures, interpersonal treatment and information were related but distinguishable aspects of perceived fairness. Most evidence concerns organisations. Disagreement about fairness is not proof of dishonesty or a single objectively correct allocation. [Colquitt and colleagues (2001). Justice at the millennium: a meta-analytic review of 25 years of organizational justice research.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11419803/)

NHS public guidance describes emotional, physical, sexual and economic abuse and routes to confidential support. Services are UK-specific. It is guidance, not a test that determines a person’s motive or an individual safety assessment. [NHS: Domestic violence and abuse](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/getting-help-for-domestic-violence/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

A temporary imbalance can reflect illness, caregiving or changing resources. A persistent one-sided arrangement can also be a real problem. Gratitude is different from consent to another request. Receiving help does not give the helper a general right to money, private information, access or compliance.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Compare contributions and opportunities over a reasonable period, including less visible work and genuine constraints. Ask what each person expects rather than counting only the resource easiest for you to measure. Notice whether refusal is respected and whether commitments are explicit. Include your own tendency either to overgive without agreement or to assume others will always compensate.

## How to respond

Clarify the terms of substantial help before accepting or offering it. In an ordinary relationship, discuss what is sustainable and what kind of mutuality matters. You can reduce a contribution you cannot maintain without presenting the other person with a moral invoice. If obligation is enforced through threats or dependence, consider the wider controlling pattern and support options.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: You drive a friend to appointments while they regularly help with your paperwork. The exchange need not involve equal hours to feel mutually valued. If either contribution becomes unmanageable, the arrangement needs discussion rather than a silent accusation of selfishness.

<section class="key-takeaways" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="key-takeaways">

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Mutuality does not require identical contributions each time.
- Clarify whether help is a gift, agreement or role duty.
- A favour does not buy unrelated access or compliance.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Describe one ongoing exchange, including less visible contributions and constraints.
2. Clarify one expectation or adjust an unsustainable contribution.
3. Review whether the arrangement remains voluntary, understood and workable for both people.
