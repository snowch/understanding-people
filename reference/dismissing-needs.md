# Dismissing another person’s needs

Dismissing another person’s needs means treating a reported concern or requirement as unworthy of consideration. It differs from saying that you cannot meet a request or disagree about the proposed solution. A useful exchange can recognise the need while leaving the arrangement open to negotiation.

> **Remember:** Acknowledge a need without promising to meet it.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

This material provides general information and has not received specialist review. It is not a diagnostic tool or an individual assessment of safety. For advice about your circumstances, seek appropriately qualified support.

## How it may arise

A person may lack information, feel overloaded, see a conflict with their own needs or believe a request is unfair. They may also be avoiding responsibility or exercising control. Several can coexist. Calling something a need does not settle how it must be met, while calling it inconvenient does not make it disappear.

## What the evidence supports

Richard Ryan and Edward Deci reviewed self-determination theory and evidence concerning autonomy, competence and relatedness in motivation and well-being. The framework suggests distinctions to investigate. It cannot identify the cause of a particular action or refusal. [Ryan & Deci (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11392867/)

Jason Colquitt and colleagues combined 183 organisational-justice studies. Fair outcomes, procedures, interpersonal treatment and information were related but distinguishable aspects of perceived fairness. Most evidence concerns organisations. Disagreement about fairness is not proof of dishonesty or a single objectively correct allocation. [Colquitt and colleagues (2001). Justice at the millennium: a meta-analytic review of 25 years of organizational justice research.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11419803/)

Tal Eyal and colleagues tested interpersonal predictions across a series of experiments. Imagining another person’s perspective did not consistently improve accuracy; a final experiment found that obtaining information through conversation helped. Conversation does not guarantee honesty, complete understanding or safety. These were specific prediction tasks, not tests of every relationship. [Eyal, Steffel & Epley (2018). Perspective mistaking: Accurately understanding the mind of another requires getting perspective, not taking perspective.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29620401/)

NHS public guidance describes emotional, physical, sexual and economic abuse and routes to confidential support. Services are UK-specific. It is guidance, not a test that determines a person’s motive or an individual safety assessment. [NHS: Domestic violence and abuse](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/getting-help-for-domestic-violence/)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

A request, preference, access requirement, shared duty and immediate safety concern call for different responses. A clear no can be respectful. An apparent accommodation can be coercive if it buys unrelated compliance. Avoid treating all disagreement as invalidation or all expressions of need as manipulation.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Clarify the actual difficulty, its impact and what outcome would help. Ask which part is being disputed: existence, importance, responsibility or the proposed method. Notice whether each person can state the other’s concern accurately. Include whose needs are routinely treated as the default and whose must be repeatedly justified.

## How to respond

Acknowledge the concern you understand, state real constraints and discuss available options. Make shared duties explicit and use appropriate advice for access or rights issues. If needs are dismissed alongside threats, deprivation or control, seek support for the practical pattern. Understanding does not require endless debate before you protect a legitimate limit.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: A partner needs quiet to work, while another needs to use the room for a call. Naming both requirements allows discussion of timing or another space. Saying “Your work is not important” changes the issue from a scheduling conflict to dismissal.

<section class="key-takeaways" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="key-takeaways">

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Recognising a need and agreeing to a solution are different.
- Clarify what is disputed and who owns the responsibility.
- Both people’s constraints deserve consideration.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Describe one need and separate it from your preferred solution.
2. Ask what the other person understood and discuss one feasible option or relevant support route.
3. Review whether the need and constraints were acknowledged and a practical decision made.
