# Gaslighting & undermining your reality

Gaslighting refers to a pattern of undermining someone’s confidence in their perception, memory or account of events, often through distortion, denial or attacks on credibility. It is more than an ordinary disagreement or one mistaken recollection. The term can help identify a concern, but it does not give a book or AI a validated way to classify every disputed conversation.

> **Remember:** Check the event and the effort to discredit your account.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

This material provides general information and has not received specialist review. It is not a diagnostic tool or an individual assessment of safety. For advice about your circumstances, seek appropriately qualified support.

## How it may arise

Power can matter because one person may be better placed to have their version accepted by family, workplaces or services. Social stereotypes and dependence can also affect whose account is believed. A repeated challenge to credibility may become consequential beyond the original event. These processes should be examined from actual conduct, not inferred as a complete plan from one contradiction.

## What the evidence supports

Sociologist Paige Sweet used domestic-violence accounts to develop an explanation of gaslighting involving power and structural inequalities. This is a qualitative sociological analysis, not a validated checklist for classifying any disagreement. [Sweet (2019). The Sociology of Gaslighting.](https://doi.org/10.1177/0003122419874843)

Elizabeth Loftus reviewed experimental research on misinformation after an event. Later suggestions can change what people report remembering. This does not show that a particular memory is false, or justify dismissing an account of harm. [Loftus (2005). Planting misinformation in the human mind: a 30-year investigation of the malleability of memory.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16027179/)

Home Office statutory guidance describes controlling or coercive behaviour as an unfolding pattern and discusses restrictions, threats, economic abuse and effects on everyday freedom. It addresses law and practice in England and Wales. This book does not establish whether a legal offence occurred or replace local advice. [Home Office: Controlling or coercive behaviour — statutory guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/controlling-or-coercive-behaviour-statutory-guidance-framework/controlling-or-coercive-behaviour-statutory-guidance-framework-accessible)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Memory error, ambiguous wording, embarrassment, selective reporting and deception about one fact can overlap with parts of the pattern. Those alternatives do not erase an established event or make repeated discrediting harmless. You can establish that an account is inaccurate without establishing intent, and you can seek help before settling whether the label fits.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Separate the event, the disputed account and what happens to your credibility or choices afterward. Note relevant messages or records you already legitimately have, and mark uncertainty. Ask whether corrections are accepted, whether your account is repeatedly dismissed globally, and whether other restrictions or threats occur. Multiple people repeating one account are not independent confirmation.

## How to respond

Focus on practical support, reliable information and your options rather than winning an endless argument about reality. Where safe, clarify a particular fact or make future agreements explicit. If the pattern includes intimidation or control, seek appropriate confidential support and consider the privacy of notes and devices. Do not stage memory tests or provoke denial to obtain proof.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: After a clear agreement is documented, a relative repeatedly denies it and tells others you cannot distinguish events from imagination. The record and subsequent discrediting are separate relevant observations. Neither a single denial nor an outsider’s label establishes the entire pattern by itself.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Power and wider restrictions can shape the effect.
- You can seek support without obtaining an admission of intent.

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. If safe, separate one event, the conflicting accounts and any effect on your credibility or choices.
2. Choose a legitimate factual clarification or confidential support route without provoking a response.
3. Review what became clearer and what practical protection or support is still needed.
