# Correspondence bias

Treating someone’s behaviour as evidence of their character while giving too little weight to the circumstances. A character explanation may be possible without being established.

> **Remember:** Before deciding “that is what they are like”, compare what happens under different conditions.

Examples are fictional. The comparisons and practice steps are editorial suggestions, not a test that identifies bias in a person.

## What it looks like {#meaning}

A colleague gives a short reply and you decide they are rude. They may be; they might also be dealing with an urgent deadline. In this fictional example, the bias would be giving the character judgment more weight than the available information supports.

It can concern positive judgments too. Someone answers every question correctly, but they wrote the questions and chose a subject they know. Inferring broad brilliance without considering that advantage repeats the same kind of mistake.

## Why does it happen? {#why}

Gilbert and Malone’s review describes several routes. You may not know about a constraint. You may know about it but underestimate how strongly it shapes behaviour. Your expectations may colour what you think you saw. Or an early character judgment may remain even after circumstances become known.

For example, knowing that someone is under pressure does not mean you have allowed enough for its effect. Revising an initial judgment takes attention; a hurried observer may stop at the first explanation. These are possible mechanisms, not one universal account of every judgment.

**Research behind this explanation:** [Gilbert & Malone (1995)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7870861/)

## How can I recognise it? {#recognise}

Look for the jump from a specific action to a broad description of the person. Then compare what the description predicts with what you actually know.

| A thought to examine | A more useful check |
| --- | --- |
| “They are lazy” after a missed task. | Was the task clear, feasible and agreed? Compare similar tasks with enough time and resources. |
| “They are unfriendly” after silence. | Does this happen in small, familiar groups as well as public meetings? Check what differs; a difference alone does not tell you why. |
| “They are naturally helpful” after a polite service encounter. | How much does the role require this response? A good interaction need not reveal behaviour outside that role. |
| “Anyone else would have managed.” | What evidence supports that comparison? Do others actually have the same information, choices, skills and constraints? |

## How much can a comparison tell me? {#limits}

The same person behaving differently in different conditions weakens an “always like this” claim. It does not prove which condition caused the difference. Repetition also deserves attention, but repetition in one setting may reflect a repeated constraint as well as a tendency.

Do not replace “bad person” with an equally confident invented excuse. A deadline matters if it is known or worth checking, not just because it is a comforting possibility. You can request a change or set a boundary while the explanation remains uncertain.

Do not assume you always excuse yourself and blame others. Malle’s analysis found that this proposed actor–observer pattern was not a general rule across studies. Correspondence bias is a more specific question about underweighting circumstances.

**Research behind this explanation:** [Malle (2006)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17073526/)

## Can it change? {#change}

Aim to make your explanation more specific and better checked. Replace “They are unreliable” with “They missed two agreed deadlines on this project.” Name what you know about the conditions. Ask what a character explanation and a practical constraint would each predict next.

Morewedge and colleagues’ training study included fundamental attribution error, a related label for underweighting situational influences. Training improved tested judgments, with effects still found at least two months later. It does not show that reading a page permanently changes how someone treats family or colleagues.

Our practical suggestion is to build a pause into important judgments: describe the action, check constraints, compare an occasion and review. If the behaviour continues despite a workable agreement, address that pattern. Understanding a constraint does not make the impact disappear.

**Research behind this explanation:** [Morewedge et al](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/12324/)

## How is this different from confirmation bias? {#distinguish}

Correspondence bias is about the explanation: “This behaviour shows what sort of person they are.” Confirmation bias is about how you handle evidence for that explanation. You could do either without the other, or make a character judgment and then overlook everything that challenges it.

## Put it into practice: check one character judgment {#practice}

1. Choose an ordinary event where you thought “They are…” or “I am…”. Write the actual action and keep the character label separate.
2. Identify one known circumstance and one useful comparison. Ask about a constraint or clarify an agreement where appropriate. You do not need to settle the person’s character.
3. Review what happened next. Was your description more accurate? Did your request become more specific or workable? Record what remains uncertain and what response is still needed.

## Sources and limits

[Gilbert & Malone (1995). The correspondence bias.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7870861/) A review describing several routes to underweighting circumstances when judging a person. It does not establish that every character judgment is mistaken.

[Malle (2006). The actor–observer asymmetry in attribution: A (surprising) meta-analysis.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17073526/) A synthesis of 173 studies that challenges a blanket rule about explaining our own behaviour through situations and others’ through personality.

[Morewedge et al. (2015). Debiasing decisions: Improved decision making with a single training intervention.](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/12324/) Two longitudinal experiments with games and instructional videos; gains on tested judgment problems persisted at least two months. This does not establish lasting improvement in family or workplace relationships from reading this entry.
