# Trying to protect

Trying to protect is a possible intention to reduce a perceived risk to someone or something valued. Protective action can be thoughtful and necessary. The claim of protection does not establish that the risk assessment is sound or that the method is acceptable. The affected person’s wishes, rights and ability to choose still matter.

> **Remember:** Protection should be proportionate and reviewable.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

A person may act on a real hazard, an earlier experience, limited information or an exaggerated expectation of harm. A parent, manager or caregiver may have genuine responsibilities while also finding uncertainty difficult. Several motives can coexist, including a wish to retain influence. Do not assume either care or control solely from the explanation offered.

## What the evidence supports

Richard Ryan and Edward Deci reviewed self-determination theory and evidence concerning autonomy, competence and relatedness in motivation and well-being. The framework suggests distinctions to investigate. It cannot identify the cause of a particular action or refusal. [Ryan & Deci (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11392867/)

NHS public guidance describes emotional, physical, sexual and economic abuse and routes to confidential support. Services are UK-specific. It is guidance, not a test that determines a person’s motive or an individual safety assessment. [NHS: Domestic violence and abuse](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/getting-help-for-domestic-violence/)

Home Office statutory guidance describes controlling or coercive behaviour as an unfolding pattern and discusses restrictions, threats, economic abuse and effects on everyday freedom. It addresses law and practice in England and Wales. This book does not establish whether a legal offence occurred or replace local advice. [Home Office: Controlling or coercive behaviour — statutory guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/controlling-or-coercive-behaviour-statutory-guidance-framework/controlling-or-coercive-behaviour-statutory-guidance-framework-accessible)

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

A necessary safety restriction differs from an unlimited right to decide for another adult. A warning differs from preventing contact or demanding account passwords. Legitimate care can also be poorly communicated. Examine what the action does, how it is justified and whether less restrictive alternatives are available.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Clarify the specific hazard, the evidence, who bears the cost and what would allow the restriction to end. Is the precaution connected to the hazard? Can the affected person ask questions or obtain independent advice? Compare occasions when choice is respected. If there are threats or repeated restrictions, consider the wider pattern rather than evaluating the stated intention in isolation.

## How to respond

Explain a concrete concern and invite a proportionate safeguard that preserves as much choice as possible. Where responsibilities for a child or a person needing care apply, use appropriate professional and local guidance rather than pretending the responsibilities disappear. For adult relationships, protection is not a standing entitlement to monitor, isolate or punish.

**A brief fictional example:** Fictional example: A father worries about his adult daughter travelling. Offering route information and asking what support she wants preserves her decision. Requiring constant location sharing under threat of withdrawing unrelated support raises a different issue from giving advice.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- A protective intention does not settle whether a method is justified.
- Connect restrictions to a specific risk and a review point.
- Safety and the affected person’s agency both matter.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Describe one claimed protective action, the hazard and whose choices it changes.
2. Discuss or seek advice on a less restrictive safeguard where appropriate.
3. Review risk, consent and freedom of action; seek support if questioning leads to threats.
