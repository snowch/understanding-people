# Exclusion, gossip & reputation

Ostracism means being ignored or excluded. It differs from someone restricting your access to other people, although both can affect belonging. Reputation is what others believe about a person; gossip passes information about someone who is absent. Gossip can inform, protect or distort, depending on its content and transmission.

> **Remember:** Check who did what and where each story came from.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

Exclusion may follow conflict, practical limits, mistaken assumptions or a deliberate attempt to marginalise. Stories can circulate because people seek support, warn others, entertain a group or recruit agreement. Information that is memorable or negative may receive disproportionate attention even without a coordinated campaign.

## What the evidence supports

Kipling Williams reviewed research on being ignored, rejected or excluded. Such experiences can threaten belonging and other needs, with different behavioural responses. A painful exclusion does not establish its cause, and one missed invitation does not prove deliberate ostracism. [Williams (2007). Ostracism.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16968209/)

Samantha Grayson and colleagues reanalysed economic-game gossip and ran an experiment with new readers. Negative stories were disproportionately shared, leading readers to underestimate how cooperatively the original groups behaved. Economic-game findings do not establish that every warning is biased or that a specific family’s reputation is false. [Grayson, Feinberg, Willer & Zaki (2025). Ironic effects of prosocial gossip in driving inaccurate social perceptions.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2024.104682)

## Presenting ourselves to different audiences {#audience-and-presentation}

Impression management means shaping how others see you. It can include accurate explanations of your contribution, selective emphasis or deception; changing presentation alone does not determine which occurred. Compare the factual claims and commitments, not simply whether someone sounds different in public.

Peck and Levashina’s 2017 meta-analysis examined interviews and job-performance ratings. The relationship between self-presentation and ratings varied with tactics and research design. Applying that literature to an individual family is an editorial comparison, not evidence of a hidden strategy. Ask what information, role or audience expectation actually changed.

[Peck & Levashina (2017). Impression Management and Interview and Job Performance Ratings.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28261135/). These workplace findings do not establish dishonesty whenever someone changes their presentation across audiences.

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

An invitation is not always owed, and choosing distance from harmful conduct differs from punishing someone for dissent. A warning is not false because it is negative or second-hand. Conversely, several people repeating one account do not create several independent observations.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Separate direct observation, the original report and later retellings. What changed in the telling? Who has first-hand knowledge, and who could correct an error? Compare ordinary logistical explanations for an exclusion with repeated selective treatment. Notice whether you have shared a vivid claim more confidently than its source supports.

## How to respond

Check a consequential claim before passing it on. Correct your own inaccurate account clearly and avoid unnecessary identifying detail. For an ordinary exclusion, clarify the arrangement or express a preference without demanding inclusion. For threats, discrimination or restricted contact, a suitable support or formal route may matter more than winning a reputation dispute.

**A brief fictional example:** Three relatives say a cousin refused to help. All heard it from one person; no one asked what was requested. Tracing the account reveals one source rather than three confirmations. The original report may still be true, but confidence should not grow merely with repetition.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Exclusion, contact restriction and reputation are related but different issues.
- Repeated retellings are not independent corroboration.
- Check consequential claims while respecting legitimate support-seeking and safety.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Choose one exclusion or story and identify what you observed directly, what was reported and where it originated. Note an alternative explanation.
2. Make one proportionate clarification or correct something you passed on. Avoid confronting someone if doing so could expose them or you to harm.
3. Review whether the information became more accurate or participation clearer. Do not count agreement from more listeners as new evidence.
