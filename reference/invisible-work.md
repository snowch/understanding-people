# Invisible work & shared responsibility

Invisible work is effort that others may not see when counting completed tasks. Cognitive work includes noticing needs, finding options, remembering deadlines and checking progress. Doing a chore and owning the work needed to make it happen are different contributions. Emotional effort, physical work and planning can overlap but are not interchangeable.

> **Remember:** Share ownership of the task, including noticing and coordinating.

The brief example is fictional. Possible explanations and practical suggestions are editorial applications; they are not a test that identifies a motive, trait or diagnosis.

## How it may arise

A division such as “I help when asked” can leave one person managing the whole system. Different schedules, knowledge, access and expectations can shape the arrangement. A capable person may gradually become the default coordinator because they notice problems first; this does not by itself establish anyone else’s deliberate strategy.

## What the evidence supports

Sociologist Allison Daminger used interviews with members of 35 couples to examine cognitive household work, including anticipating needs, identifying options and monitoring progress. This qualitative study offers distinctions for examining work. It is not a population estimate or a rule about what women or men always do. [Daminger (2019). The Cognitive Dimension of Household Labor.](https://doi.org/10.1177/0003122419859007)

Martin Hagger and Aleksandra Luszczynska reviewed action-planning interventions in health settings, including differences in results and study quality. A concrete plan may be useful, but health-intervention findings do not validate a family or workplace agreement. [Hagger & Luszczynska (2014). Implementation intention and action planning interventions in health contexts: state of the research and proposals for the way forward.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24591064/)

## Remembering relationships is work too {#care-and-connection}

Arranging visits, remembering birthdays, checking on relatives and passing on updates can become a substantial coordination role. Sometimes called kin-keeping, it should be discussed as actual work rather than assumed to belong to the person who has always done it. Ask which activities matter, who wants them and what can be shared or stopped.

Recognising the work does not mean every tradition must continue or that people owe unlimited emotional availability. Decide what is worthwhile and sustainable. The household cognitive-labour study supports examining unseen work, not a universal theory of all family relationship maintenance.

## What else could explain it? {#a-distinction-to-keep-in-mind}

Missing a task can reflect unclear ownership, lack of skill, illness, time pressure, disagreement about standards or avoidance. “Weaponised incompetence” implies someone deliberately performs inability to shift work; the burden can be real without that intent being established. One poorly done task is not a motive detector.

These alternatives are starting points, not an exhaustive list.

## What to notice and check

Trace one recurring task from noticing the need through completion. Who decides what is adequate, obtains resources, reminds others and fixes omissions? Compare what happens after ownership, standards and training are clear. Include whether you reclaim tasks before the other person has a realistic opportunity to complete them.

## How to respond

Allocate an outcome and its coordination, not just instructions to be followed. Agree sufficient standards, budget, timing and when to raise a problem. Allow a different workable method. Where a person cannot take the whole task, divide it explicitly and count coordination as a contribution. Do not put safety-critical care at risk to prove a point.

**A brief fictional example:** One partner cooks dinner while the other chooses meals, checks supplies, writes the list and reminds them to start. “We share cooking” misses the planning burden. Giving one person ownership of a whole meal night, with agreed resources, changes the arrangement being tested.

## Key takeaways {#key-takeaways}

- Completed chores do not capture all the work.
- A repeated burden deserves attention even when intent is uncertain.
- Agree ownership, sufficient standards and review without imposing one preferred method.

<section class="reader-action compact-practice" markdown="1" aria-labelledby="practice">

## Put it into practice {#practice}

1. Map one recurring task: noticing, planning, deciding, doing and following up. Record who currently owns each part.
2. Agree one feasible transfer or division of ownership, including resources, support and what counts as complete.
3. Review total effort and coordination burden as well as completion. Note whether your own reminders or standards helped or recreated the old arrangement.
