# Glossary

Plain-English meanings for terms used in the book. Each example is fictional. Use the chapter link to return to the explanation and its research sources.

These definitions explain how the book uses each term. They do not replace the qualifications in the chapter.

## Autonomy {#autonomy}

Feeling that an action is your own choice and that you agree with doing it. This can include choosing to accept help.

**Example:** Leila chooses an occasional gardening session because it fits what she wants to contribute.

[Read in Chapter 3](chapters/03-needs-motives-goals.md#definition-autonomy).

## Base rate {#base-rate}

How common something is in the relevant group before you consider a particular clue or test result.

**Example:** In Chapter 2’s invented group, 10 of 1,000 people deliberately broke the rule. The base rate is 1%.

[Read in Chapter 2](chapters/02-what-counts-as-knowing.md#definition-base-rate).

## Big Five {#big-five}

A way of describing personality using five broad dimensions, with people varying along each one. These are dimensions, not five types of person.

**Example:** Alex can be less outgoing than a friend while being similarly organised. One dimension does not decide the others.

[Read in Chapter 6](chapters/06-personality-habits-situations.md#definition-big-five).

## Boundary {#boundary}

A limit on what you will accept or take part in, backed by an action you can take.

**Example:** “I will bring food, but I will not organise a gathering I did not agree to organise.”

[Read in Chapter 11](chapters/11-conflict-manipulation-boundaries.md#definition-boundary).

## Cognitive flexibility {#cognitive-flexibility}

Being able to switch between ways of thinking or approaches when the task calls for it.

**Example:** When neither proposed visit date works, Noor considers a shorter visit on a different weekend.

[Read in Chapter 5](chapters/05-emotion-stress-coping.md#definition-cognitive-flexibility).

## Competence {#competence}

In self-determination theory, feeling able to handle a task and make progress.

**Example:** After someone shows Leila how to plant a seedling, she feels able to do the next one herself.

[Read in Chapter 3](chapters/03-needs-motives-goals.md#definition-competence).

## Confirmation bias {#confirmation-bias}

Looking for information—or understanding it in a way—that supports what you already believe.

**Example:** You think Rowan dislikes your idea. You notice every criticism but overlook the positive comments.

[Read in Chapter 1](chapters/01-before-you-explain.md#definition-confirmation-bias).

## Correspondence bias {#correspondence-bias}

Deciding that someone’s behaviour shows what they are like as a person, while giving too little weight to the situation they were in.

**Example:** You decide Rowan is an unhelpful person after one critical comment, without checking whether a new budget problem affected the proposal.

[Read in Chapter 1](chapters/01-before-you-explain.md#definition-correspondence-bias).

## Corroboration {#corroboration}

Additional evidence that supports a claim, especially evidence from an independent source.

**Example:** A supplier’s order confirmation supports the contractor’s statement that a part was ordered.

[Read in Chapter 9](chapters/09-truth-lies-uncertainty.md#definition-corroboration).

## Descriptive norm {#descriptive-norm}

What people in a group commonly do, or believe others commonly do.

**Example:** At the residents’ meeting, newcomers usually stay quiet.

[Read in Chapter 7](chapters/07-culture-groups-power.md#definition-descriptive-norm).

## Extrinsic motivation {#extrinsic-motivation}

Doing an activity for a result beyond the activity itself. The result may matter to you personally, or you may feel pressure to obtain it.

**Example:** Leila gardens because she values improving the neighbourhood, even on days when she does not enjoy the work.

[Read in Chapter 3](chapters/03-needs-motives-goals.md#definition-extrinsic-motivation).

## Gaslighting {#gaslighting}

Psychological abuse that undermines someone’s trust in their own memory, perceptions or judgment. One disagreement about a memory is not enough to establish it.

**Example:** Someone repeatedly denies events, dismisses evidence and tells others that the person challenging them cannot trust their own memory. This pattern warrants concern and support; the example alone is not a test for abuse.

[Read in Chapter 11](chapters/11-conflict-manipulation-boundaries.md#definition-gaslighting).

## Habit {#habit}

A learned tendency to respond fairly automatically when a familiar situation occurs.

**Example:** Alex sits on the sofa and reaches for the phone before deciding how to spend the evening.

[Read in Chapter 6](chapters/06-personality-habits-situations.md#definition-habit).

## If–then plan {#if-then-plan}

A plan that connects a particular situation to an action: if that situation occurs, you will do the planned action.

**Example:** Leila plans: “If the care visit finishes by ten, I will take the ten-twenty bus.”

[Read in Chapter 3](chapters/03-needs-motives-goals.md#definition-if-then-plan).

## Intrinsic motivation {#intrinsic-motivation}

Doing an activity because you enjoy it or find the activity itself interesting.

**Example:** Leila gardens because she enjoys growing plants.

[Read in Chapter 3](chapters/03-needs-motives-goals.md#definition-intrinsic-motivation).

## Leading question {#leading-question}

A question worded in a way that suggests the answer the questioner expects.

**Example:** “You promised on Tuesday, didn’t you?” suggests both that there was a promise and when it was made.

[Read in Chapter 8](chapters/08-questions-listening-disagreement.md#definition-leading-question).

## Manipulation {#manipulation}

In this book, influencing someone in a way that makes it harder for them to choose freely with the information they need.

**Example:** Imagine Martin knowingly tells the family you agreed to organise the gathering, although you agreed only to bring food, to pressure you into taking on the extra work.

[Read in Chapter 11](chapters/11-conflict-manipulation-boundaries.md#definition-manipulation).

## Motivated reasoning {#motivated-reasoning}

Reasoning in which what you want to be true influences how you judge the information.

**Example:** Maya wants to see herself as a considerate friend, so she quickly dismisses Dev’s complaint that her public offer was embarrassing.

[Read in Chapter 4](chapters/04-beliefs-identity-history.md#definition-motivated-reasoning).

## Narrative identity {#narrative-identity}

The changing story you tell about your life: how your past, present and hopes for the future fit together.

**Example:** Dev sees himself as someone who has learned to manage alone and wants to remain independent.

[Read in Chapter 4](chapters/04-beliefs-identity-history.md#definition-narrative-identity).

## Open question {#open-question}

A question that invites someone to explain in their own words, rather than giving a yes-or-no answer.

**Example:** Imani asks Rosa, “What do you still need to decide about the holiday?”

[Read in Chapter 8](chapters/08-questions-listening-disagreement.md#definition-open-question).

## Personality trait {#personality-trait}

A tendency in how someone thinks, feels or behaves across occasions. It does not describe everything they do.

**Example:** Alex often prefers quieter gatherings but sometimes enjoys a large party.

[Read in Chapter 6](chapters/06-personality-habits-situations.md#definition-personality-trait).

## Prescriptive norm {#prescriptive-norm}

What people in a group believe others expect or approve of.

**Example:** A newcomer believes that members will disapprove if they interrupt the chair.

[Read in Chapter 7](chapters/07-culture-groups-power.md#definition-prescriptive-norm).

## Relatedness {#relatedness}

Feeling connected to other people and cared about by them.

**Example:** Leila feels welcome when another gardener remembers her name and invites her to work alongside them.

[Read in Chapter 3](chapters/03-needs-motives-goals.md#definition-relatedness).

## Working memory {#working-memory}

Holding a small amount of information in mind while using it.

**Example:** Noor keeps two possible visit dates in mind while checking which one fits her other plans.

[Read in Chapter 5](chapters/05-emotion-stress-coping.md#definition-working-memory).
