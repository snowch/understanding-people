# Research audit of reference coverage

8 November 2026. Baseline: main at 0b1f8f0, with twelve foundation chapters and 111 developed reference entries.

Implementation: the [audit delivery record](audit-implementation.md) maps all eighteen areas and the additional research leads to the expanded written explanations, app connections, edition handling and checks. The findings below retain the audit's original baseline.

The largest opportunity is to explain **how interactions work over time**: how people establish roles, communicate rank, distribute responsibility, influence decisions and respond to one another. The library already describes many individual behaviours. It needs more connections between those behaviours, their context and useful ways to respond.

Naming asymmetry is a good example. Recognition, power and family expectations are covered, but the reader cannot learn how names and titles can signal different relationships. Adding another paragraph about status would not fully close that gap.

## Scope and evidence

This is a targeted, multidisciplinary research audit, not an exhaustive systematic review. It inventories all 111 reference topics, searches the chapter and reference text, and closely compares relevant entries with selected research. Searches covered sociolinguistics, anthropology, family and relationship science, social and personality psychology, behaviour change and organisational research. Reviews and meta-analyses were preferred where available; conceptual work and individual studies are identified separately below.

The [source notes](#research-source-notes) distinguish abstracts and institutional records from accessible source sections. Search snippets were used to find material, not as the sole basis for the research conclusions below. Restricted full texts were not treated as read. These findings prioritise editorial development; they do not establish the scientific completeness of the library or validate the app's analysis of a person.

Four classifications prevent inflated gap claims:

- **Missing explanation:** no developed treatment of the mechanism was found, although adjacent ideas may exist.
- **Partial coverage:** the idea appears, but its distinctions, evidence or application need depth.
- **Findability:** suitable material exists, but everyday vocabulary does not reliably lead to it.
- **Research lead:** worth investigating before making a teaching claim or creating a new entry.

Priority reflects relevance to the reader's goals, likely usefulness across settings, evidence and overlap. It is an editorial judgment, not a numerical research score. A cluster below may require one new entry, several sections in existing entries, or better links; it does not automatically mean another page or graph node.

### Naming and conversational authority

Brown and Gilman's foundational account connects address pronouns with power and solidarity. It supports examining how language expresses relationships; its historical and mainly European-language evidence does not establish that a particular modern family uses names to manipulate people. HRAF's comparative kinship account also shows why kin terms require cultural context. Naming asymmetry is therefore a useful descriptive pattern, not a validated test of a hidden tactic. [S01](#s01), [S02](#s02)

The new explanation should distinguish **what was said**, **the relationship it may communicate**, **its effect**, and **whether there is evidence of deliberate intent**. A person can reproduce a hierarchy through habit without consciously planning it. Conversely, an upsetting difference need not express hierarchy at all.

For the reader's naming example, the useful comparisons are the exact names or titles used for comparable relatives, who chooses them, each person's stated preference, when the pattern changes and what happens after a naming preference is expressed. These are editorial investigation prompts, not a diagnostic checklist. A respectful request about a name can be reasonable before intent is settled; repeated deliberate disregard supplies different information from a readily corrected habit.

The same broader language gap includes assumptions built into questions. Potts's linguistics chapter explains presupposition and implicature, including their dependence on context. A question can carry an assumption without that assumption being true. The practical extension is to clarify or challenge the assumption separately from answering. Do not present every indirect expression as deception. [S03](#s03)

Conversational authority—who claims the right to define another person's experience or make a decision—is a further research lead. It needs conversation-analysis sources before development; it should not be inferred solely from a title or confident tone.

### Family membership and unequal treatment

Family expectations already discusses contested rules and changing care needs. The missing depth concerns *which relationship and responsibility are being assumed*. Kinship terminology, residence, descent and decision authority are different matters. A leading woman in a family, a matrilineal descent system and living near the wife's family are not interchangeable concepts. Cross-cultural descriptions should help readers ask better questions, rather than assign a family's rules from its ethnicity. [S02](#s02)

Carroll and colleagues' review concerns uncertainty about family membership and roles across settings including divorce, stepfamilies, illness and caregiving. It supports a distinct explanation of boundary ambiguity, rather than using “boundaries” to mean every disagreement. [S04](#s04)

Jensen and Thomsen's meta-analysis links parental differential treatment with children's internalising and externalising difficulties. Its child-sibling samples do not demonstrate an adult relative's strategy or establish that equal treatment is appropriate regardless of age, disability or care needs. A developed entry should distinguish unequal resources, unequal standards, explanations for those differences and how fairly they are experienced. [S05](#s05)

The [third-person entry](../reference/thirdparty.md) already makes an important distinction between mediation, advice and recruiting pressure. Deepen this with family-role research before adopting labels such as coalition, scapegoat or parentification as explanations. They should not become permanent family cast lists.

### Invisible work and interaction cycles

Daminger's qualitative study identifies cognitive work that can disappear from a simple chore count. It offers a useful framework for asking who notices a need, develops options and monitors completion. The study's 35 couples do not establish that every household follows the same gender pattern. The practical addition is to allocate responsibility for coordinating work, not only its visible execution. [S06](#s06)

The demand–withdraw literature supplies a way to examine sequences rather than isolated actions. However, Ross and colleagues found that associations varied with couples' socioeconomic resources. A request may be impossible to meet; disengagement may sometimes reduce escalation. These findings argue against a universal “withdrawal is bad” rule. A developed explanation must also distinguish a temporary pause, avoidance and coercive silence. Neither interaction cycles nor context excuses threats or assigns responsibility for another person's abuse. [S07](#s07), [S08](#s08)

### Positive collaboration needs more depth too

The self and positive conduct are already present: listening, acknowledgment, appreciation, encouragement, reciprocity, repair and review all have entries. The opportunity is to make the skills more specific and connect them, rather than declare them absent.

Perceived responsiveness concerns whether a response is experienced as understanding, validating and caring. The family study by Cook and colleagues separates perceiver, partner and relationship contributions; this is useful protection against treating every interaction as an expression of one person's personality. Slemp and colleagues' large meta-analysis also supports considering competence and relatedness alongside autonomy. These findings can enrich how readers offer help, acknowledge a concern and support learning. They do not establish that a scripted phrase will work in every relationship. [S09](#s09)–[S12](#s12)

Feedback deserves particular care. Kluger and DeNisi found beneficial average effects but also many interventions that reduced performance. Frazier and colleagues' workplace meta-analysis supports developing psychological safety as a distinct area of learning. These are reasons to teach the design and consequences of feedback, not to promise that a “sandwich” or other fixed wording makes it effective. [S21](#s21), [S22](#s22)

The existing [repair entry](../reference/repairing.md) already cites apology research and states that forgiveness is not owed. Further work should focus on sustained changes, responsibility and trust after repair, rather than adding another apology formula.

### Influence needs evidence and ethical context

Reactance is already explicitly researched in autonomy and persuasion. Likewise, Chapter 7 distinguishes descriptive from prescriptive norms. These need connections and examples, not duplicate introductions under new labels.

Sequential-request research is a more specific gap. Burger's review describes multiple processes and conditions, including failures and reduced compliance. Pascual and Guéguen compare two procedures; finding no significant difference between them does not establish a universal advantage over ordinary requests. [S14](#s14), [S15](#s15)

Avoid teaching “social proof works” as an unrestricted law. A 2025 review of health-behaviour norm messages found that the apparent effect disappeared after publication-bias adjustment. That result concerns a defined intervention literature; it does not show that social norms never influence people. The library should preserve this distinction between a plausible mechanism and the effectiveness of a particular attempt to use it. [S16](#s16)

## Popular tactic labels

Readers will search for these terms. Findability should not imply that every label describes a distinct, well-established mechanism.

| Term or pattern | Recommended treatment |
| --- | --- |
| DARVO: denial, attack, reversal of victim and offender | A sourced section connecting denial, credibility attacks and blame reversal. Harsey and Freyd's experiments concern observers judging fictional sexual-assault scenarios. They do not establish a detector of guilt or a personality disorder. S31. |
| Triangulation, scapegoat, golden child | Begin with observed message-passing, alliance recruitment or differential treatment. Separate support-seeking from coercive recruitment; verify family-system claims before teaching role explanations. |
| Love bombing, hoovering, breadcrumbing | Research leads and potential search vocabulary. Compare rapid affection, unwanted repeated contact, changing promises and intermittent attention with existing behaviour entries. Do not assert a single motive or diagnosis. |
| Weaponised incompetence | Potential bridge to task competence, resources, incentives, repeated transfer of work and over-responsibility. Apparent incompetence does not establish deliberate performance. Dedicated research is needed before developing intent claims. |
| Moving goalposts, double standards, loaded questions | Describe changing criteria, unequal rules or assumptions in wording. Link respectively to agreements, fairness and G02. A criticism changing after genuinely new information is not automatically a tactic. |
| Silent treatment | Improve the distinction between withdrawal, a communicated pause, stonewalling and coercive conduct. Existing relevant pages mean this is primarily depth and findability. |

These candidate terms are not an exhaustive list or a validated taxonomy. They should not all become new pages. New person-type labels would add less value than explaining observable patterns and constructive options.

## Coverage to retain and deepen

The following are already present and should not be advertised as discoveries of missing content:

- Confirmation and correspondence biases, motivated reasoning and memory distortion.
- Autonomy and psychological reactance; motivation and COM-B.
- Genetic and learned influences, with an explicit warning that family resemblance does not separate them.
- Listening, asking before advice, access needs and communication differences.
- Fairness, power, culture, family expectations and public–private differences.
- Action planning, goal monitoring, accountability and reviewing outcomes.
- Apologies, repair, boundaries, coercive conduct and routes to support.

There is also an **evidence-specificity gap** in some entries. For example, the third-person entry cites interpersonal prediction, organisational justice and abuse guidance. Those sources support parts of its advice, but do not amount to a review of family triangulation. Similarly, the audience entry uses broad status and power frameworks rather than a focused impression-management evidence base. Peck and Levashina's meta-analysis is a useful next source, with its interview and performance-rating limits retained. [S32](#s32)

Source reuse is not itself an error. The editorial task is to check that the research is close enough to the actual question, and distinguish an evidence-supported mechanism from an application proposed by the author.

## A method for investigating a real situation

The existing observation–action–review approach should remain central. Add a compact comparison table to the relevant reference practice, with optional notes:

| Record | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Exact action and context | Separate what happened from a label or theory. |
| Comparable occasions and exceptions | Check recurrence, selectivity and differences in circumstances. Record when a comparison is unavailable. |
| Two or more plausible explanations | Include preference, constraints, misunderstanding, learning and deliberate influence when relevant. Avoid filling every category by default. |
| What would distinguish them | Choose a useful observation or question that could weaken the first explanation. |
| One response within my control | Clarify, change a practical arrangement, request a preference or set a limit, as appropriate. |
| Later result and remaining uncertainty | Review understanding, freedom to choose, practical cooperation and harm, rather than scoring whether the other person agreed. |

Repeated near-event records can reduce dependence on global recollection, as described in ecological momentary assessment research. An optional personal notebook is not a validated assessment instrument, and logging must remain proportionate and private. Do not encourage covert tests, provocation or surveillance. [S27](#s27)

Population findings can inform possibilities without proving the explanation of one person's action. Fisher and colleagues highlight problems with group-to-individual inference; Adolf and Fried's methodological response cautions against claiming such generalisation is always impossible. This argues for explicit uncertainty and comparison, not abandoning research. [S28](#s28), [S29](#s29)

For AI-supported reflection, research on sycophancy shows why an agreeable answer cannot independently verify the user's account. Ask the model to distinguish reported events, interpretations, unknowns and source-supported general information. Such prompts may help organise thinking; the cited study does not validate them as a reliable prevention of error in this app. [S30](#s30)

## Development order and acceptance criteria

Start with a small release that demonstrates the improvement in depth:

1. **Naming, respect and belonging** (G01), linked to a concise explanation of assumptions in language (G02).
2. **Family membership and different standards** (G03–G04), preserving distinctions between adult-family applications and child-sibling findings.
3. **Invisible work and shared ownership** (G05), extending agreements and capacity.
4. **Interaction cycles** (G06), connecting withdrawal, criticism, resources and boundaries.
5. **Helpful support and responsiveness** (G07), deepening declining help and listening so constructive collaboration receives equal attention.

Then develop workplace oversight and feedback, specific influence processes, attachment dimensions and self-regulation. Narrower personality distinctions, comparison and exclusion can follow. Add G17's comparison method throughout; connect G18 to the app after checking its implementation.

For every developed topic:

- Explain the concept before examples. Removing the example should leave an understandable explanation, practical use and limits.
- Identify the research, its setting, what was read and the claim it supports. Keep explanatory hypotheses separate from established findings.
- Compare an ordinary or legitimate version with a concerning version. Address the reader's own behaviour where relevant.
- Give a useful next action and a review criterion. Questions asking for reflection need somewhere to answer.
- Include at least one question that could change the reader's interpretation. Do not make every path confirm suspicion.
- Link to the relevant foundations and existing references. Create a new entry only for a distinct reusable concept; Read, Listen and Practise remain formats within it.
- Preserve URLs, anchors and note IDs. Edit canonical JSON, then explicitly review any implications for the app and approved narration. This audit does not authorise regenerating recordings.

Judge the result by whether the reader notices something useful, considers a credible alternative and collaborates or protects their choices more effectively. More nodes, longer sessions and greater confidence are insufficient measures by themselves.

## Research source notes

These are original short annotations, not copied abstracts or questionnaire material. Access was checked on 8 November 2026. Unless an accessible text section is specified, the scope is the abstract or institutional research record; detailed methods and effect estimates should be checked in the full article before expanding a research claim. Sources listed as research leads above are not represented as completed evidence reviews.

<a id="s01"></a>
**S01 — Brown and Gilman (1960), [The Pronouns of Power and Solidarity](https://pages.mtu.edu/~rlstrick/rsvtxt/pronoun.htm).** Foundational sociolinguistic account; accessible institutional text sections read. Supports examining address and social relationships. Historical and mainly European-language material; not a family-intent detector.

<a id="s02"></a>
**S02 — Ember, Droe and Russell (2022), [Residence and Kinship](https://hraf.yale.edu/ehc/summaries/residence-and-kinship).** HRAF comparative research synthesis; accessible overview and residence/descent/terminology sections read. Supports distinctions among kinship arrangements. Ethnographic group patterns do not determine one family's practices.

<a id="s03"></a>
**S03 — Potts (2014 draft), [Presupposition and Implicature](https://web.stanford.edu/~cgpotts/manuscripts/potts-blackwellsemantics.pdf).** Handbook chapter draft; introduction and presupposition sections read. Linguistic theory and examples explain assumptions carried by language. Not experimental evidence that a particular question was malicious.

<a id="s04"></a>
**S04 — Carroll, Olson and Buckmiller (2007), [Family Boundary Ambiguity: A 30-Year Review](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/4337/).** Institutional abstract; review of 37 studies in 11 domains. Supports membership/role ambiguity as distinct from a personal boundary. It does not validate a family-role checklist.

<a id="s05"></a>
**S05 — Jensen and Thomsen (2024), [Parental differential treatment of siblings](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38439142/).** Meta-analysis abstract; 26,451 participants, predominantly White samples, with children/adolescents. Supports examining unequal treatment and adjustment associations. Does not establish intent or equivalent effects among adult in-laws.

<a id="s06"></a>
**S06 — Daminger (2019), [The Cognitive Dimension of Household Labor](https://doi.org/10.1177/0003122419859007).** Publisher abstract; qualitative interviews with members of 35 couples. Supplies a framework for invisible cognitive work. It is not a population estimate or universal gender rule.

<a id="s07"></a>
**S07 — Schrodt, Witt and Shimkowski (2014), [Demand/withdraw interaction meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1080/03637751.2013.813632).** Abstract read through the EBSCO bibliographic record; publisher full text restricted. Synthesises 74 studies of associations. Supports investigating interaction sequences, not a causal verdict about a particular couple.

<a id="s08"></a>
**S08 — Ross and colleagues (2019), [Communication and couples' socioeconomic circumstances](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30321045/).** Abstract; two longitudinal observational studies. Socioeconomic context moderated associations involving demand/withdraw and satisfaction. These findings do not prescribe withdrawal or excuse coercion.

<a id="s09"></a>
**S09 — Arican-Dinc and Gable (2023), [Responsiveness in romantic partners' interactions](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37515977/).** Review abstract. Supports explaining perceived understanding, validation and care. Romantic-relationship research does not establish a universal conversation formula.

<a id="s10"></a>
**S10 — Cook, Dezangré and De Mol (2018), [Sources of perceived responsiveness in family relationships](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29745679/).** Study abstract; 207 two-parent, two-child families analysed through a social-relations model. Supports separating person and relationship contributions. Findings do not establish a causal intervention or represent every family structure.

<a id="s11"></a>
**S11 — Slemp and colleagues (2024), [Interpersonal supports for basic psychological needs](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38635183/).** Meta-analysis abstract; 881 independent samples. Supports considering competence, relatedness and autonomy together. Associations do not validate the book's proposed exercises.

<a id="s12"></a>
**S12 — Kluger and colleagues (2024; online 2023), [Perceived listening and work outcomes](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-023-09897-5).** Open article abstract and conceptual-definition sections. Registered systematic review and meta-analyses support listening as a substantive skill. Much evidence uses self-report; correlations alone do not establish effects of a listening script.

<a id="s13"></a>
**S13 — Slemp and colleagues (2018), [Leader autonomy support in the workplace](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30237648/).** Meta-analysis abstract; 83 unique samples. Supports developing autonomy-supportive supervision. Primarily correlational evidence does not determine whether particular oversight is excessive.

<a id="s14"></a>
**S14 — Burger (1999), [The foot-in-the-door compliance procedure](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15661679/).** Review abstract. Describes multiple candidate processes, failures and conditions. No guaranteed compliance mechanism.

<a id="s15"></a>
**S15 — Pascual and Guéguen (2005), [Foot-in-the-door and door-in-the-face comparison](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15825914/).** Meta-analysis abstract of direct comparisons. No significant difference between procedures in the analysed comparisons; this does not alone establish either procedure's absolute effectiveness.

<a id="s16"></a>
**S16 — Papakonstantinou and colleagues (2025), [Social norms messages and health behaviour](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40983670/).** Preregistered systematic review/meta-analysis abstract; 89 trials. Apparent benefits disappeared after publication-bias adjustment. Scope is health messaging among people aged 16+ in developed countries, not all social influence.

<a id="s17"></a>
**S17 — Zhang and colleagues (2022), [Adult attachment and mental health](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36201836/).** Meta-analysis abstract. Supports discussing attachment anxiety and avoidance as dimensions associated with mental-health measures. Does not justify diagnosing a relative or treating attachment as immutable.

<a id="s18"></a>
**S18 — Hadden, Smith and Webster (2014), [Attachment, relationship duration and relationship quality](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24026179/).** Meta-analysis abstract. Duration moderated associations in cross-sectional samples. This is not proof that relationship duration causes attachment-related deterioration.

<a id="s19"></a>
**S19 — Sezer and colleagues (2025), [Daily mental contrasting with implementation intentions](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40219795/).** Registered-report abstract; 297 participants, week-long study comparing daily with one-off practice for bedtime procrastination. Supports a bounded practice lead; not a general treatment claim.

<a id="s20"></a>
**S20 — Zhou and colleagues (2026), [Academic procrastination trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41601124/).** Abstract; 81 students at one Chinese university, short follow-up. Promising results concern aversiveness and willingness to act; they do not establish durable changes in completed academic work across settings.

<a id="s21"></a>
**S21 — Kluger and DeNisi (1996), [Feedback interventions and performance](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-effects-of-feedback-interventions-on-performance-a-historical/).** Institutional abstract; meta-analysis found an average benefit alongside interventions that reduced performance. Supports conditional advice, not a fixed successful feedback formula.

<a id="s22"></a>
**S22 — Frazier and colleagues (2017), [Psychological Safety: A Meta-Analytic Review and Extension](https://digitalcommons.odu.edu/management_fac_pubs/13/).** Institutional abstract; 136 independent workplace samples. Supports distinct coverage of psychological safety and its context. Associations do not guarantee a technique will make speaking up safe.

<a id="s23"></a>
**S23 — Stewart and colleagues, [The finer details of personality prediction](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34236710/).** Abstract retrieved through NCBI; large-sample study of domains, facets and narrower items, with separate training/validation partitions. Supports explaining finer distinctions. Self-reported outcomes and predictive associations are not causal explanations of individual conduct.

<a id="s24"></a>
**S24 — Williams (2007), [Ostracism](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16968209/).** Review abstract. Supports distinguishing being ignored or excluded from restricting another person's contacts. Does not determine why someone was not invited to one event.

<a id="s25"></a>
**S25 — [Social comparison and envy on social media: A critical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35184028/) (2022).** Abstract retrieved through NCBI. Reports mixed, conditional and person-dependent findings. Supports teaching comparison without a universal harmful-social-media claim.

<a id="s26"></a>
**S26 — Reis and colleagues (2025), [Parental gatekeeping and parenting](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40439085/).** Scoping-review abstract; 16 studies with conceptual and methodological disagreement. Largely concerns mothers, fathers and parenting involvement. It cannot simply be generalised to every grandmother or in-law.

<a id="s27"></a>
**S27 — Shiffman, Stone and Hufford (2008), [Ecological momentary assessment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18509902/).** Review abstract. Supports the rationale for near-event, repeated observations and attention to context. Does not validate this app's notes as an assessment or establish causality.

<a id="s28"></a>
**S28 — Fisher, Medaglia and Jeronimus (2018), [Group-to-individual generalisability](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29915059/).** Abstract; analyses of six repeated-measures datasets. Supports caution about transferring aggregate estimates to individuals, not rejection of population research.

<a id="s29"></a>
