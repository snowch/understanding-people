# What to take with you

You began with a situation you wanted to understand. Return to it now: **what do you know, what are you still guessing, and what would you do next?** You may have a better question or a clearer limit, even if you still do not know someone's reasons.

## Six things to remember

- **Keep what happened separate from your explanation.** “They did not reply” is an observation. “They do not care” is a possible explanation. Check alternatives and what the evidence actually supports. Revisit [misinterpretation](chapters/01-before-you-explain.md) and [evidence](chapters/02-what-counts-as-knowing.md).
- **Wanting something and being able to do it are different.** Ask about practical difficulties and competing goals. The same offer or event can also mean different things to different people. Include your own preferred explanation. Revisit [motivation](chapters/03-needs-motives-goals.md) and [beliefs and identity](chapters/04-beliefs-identity-history.md).
- **Look at the occasion as well as the person.** Stress, familiar routines and surroundings can affect what someone does. Notice patterns and exceptions; a label does not explain every action. Revisit [emotion and stress](chapters/05-emotion-stress-coping.md) and [personality and habits](chapters/06-personality-habits-situations.md).
- **Ask what taking part or disagreeing costs.** Group expectations, access to information and unequal power can shape behaviour. Silence need not mean agreement or lack of interest. Revisit [culture, groups and power](chapters/07-culture-groups-power.md).
- **Listen, then check what matters.** Let someone correct your understanding. Confidence, eye contact and a convincing story do not establish that a claim is true. Revisit [questions and listening](chapters/08-questions-listening-disagreement.md) and [truth and lies](chapters/09-truth-lies-uncertainty.md).
- **Choose a useful response and review it.** Agree what improvement means, respect the other person's choices, and check what changed. You can set a limit or seek support without proving a hidden motive. Revisit [cooperation](chapters/10-motivation-influence-cooperation.md), [boundaries](chapters/11-conflict-manipulation-boundaries.md) and [putting the ideas together](chapters/12-putting-understanding-to-work.md).

## Keep these questions close

**“What did they say or do? Why do I think they did it? How could I check?”**

Include your own words, actions and circumstances. Then ask: **“What can I usefully and safely do next? What happened when I tried it?”**

Understanding someone does not require accepting harm. If there is fear, coercion or a risk of retaliation, use the [support page](support.md) to consider a safer next step.

## Put it into practice {#take-it-with-you}

### Try one fresh situation

**A new fictional situation:** Your neighbour agreed to water your balcony plants while you were away. You return to dry soil and think, “They didn't bother.”

What do you know? Name another possible explanation and one useful question. Say or write your answer before opening the example.

<details class="answer-check" markdown="1">
<summary>Show an example answer</summary>

You know what was agreed and that the soil is dry now. That alone does not establish whether, when or how much your neighbour watered. Hot weather or unclear instructions are possibilities to check, not established excuses.

You could ask, “How did the watering go? Which pots did you water, and when?” Include whether you made the instructions clear. Depending on the answer, agree a more specific arrangement or make another plan next time. You need not reach a verdict about their character.

</details>

### Use it once this week

Choose one ordinary, safe situation. Use the questions above, take one useful step, and choose a time to review what happened. Reopen your [Chapter 12 practice notes](chapters/12-putting-understanding-to-work.md#complete-cycle) or use the [worksheet](worksheet.md) if writing helps.

In a week, try recalling the questions before looking back. Notice whether you actually used one—not just whether it sounded familiar. Return to the chapter you need; you do not have to keep assessing everyone around you.
